# Making Simple Containers

Containers hold water, food, tools and waste, and a household without them quickly becomes disorganised and unsafe. Many useful containers can be made from materials already at hand. The skill lies in choosing a material that will not leach harmful substances, sealing it properly, and labelling it so its contents are never confused.

> @image Assorted improvised containers on a workbench: a stitched cloth sack, a plastic bottle with a cut top, and a bucket lined with tar paper.

## Choosing Materials

Strong, watertight and inert materials are best. Food-grade plastic bottles and jugs are excellent, provided they have held food or water and are thoroughly cleaned. Tin cans work well once the sharp edges are smoothed. Ceramic and glass are very stable but break easily. Avoid containers that held chemicals, pesticides, fuel or medicines, since residues can persist even after washing.

1. Check each item for cracks, stiff plastic that might split, or deep scratches where germs can hide.
2. Remove labels and adhesive residue.
3. Note the original contents and never use a container for a purpose it cannot safely serve.

## Bags

A simple cloth bag can be made from a rectangle of sturdy fabric folded in half, with the sides sewn shut and a handle stitched on. Double the seams at the base where weight pulls hardest. Use a tight weave so contents do not escape, and line bags for dry food with a clean inner sack if moisture is a concern.

- Choose cotton or canvas for dry goods.
- Use a waterproof liner, such as a plastic sheet, for wet items.

## Buckets

Buckets can be made from a plastic container with a sturdy handle fitted through holes on opposite sides, secured with knots and wire. A tin can with its top removed and a wire bail through two punched holes makes a serviceable small pail. Smooth any rough edges with a file or tape before use.

## Bottles and Jugs

Plastic bottles are useful for carrying water, storing liquids and making funnels. Clean them with hot soapy water or a dilute chlorine solution, rinse several times, and dry upside down. A bottle cut in half makes a funnel or a scoop. Label every container with its contents and the date filled. Keep liquids in cool, dark places to slow degradation.

1. Rinse the bottle thoroughly before the first use.
2. Fill and cap, then check for leaks by standing it upright for an hour.
3. Mark the container with a waterproof marker or tape.

## Safe Reuse

Reuse is the most common source of contamination. A container used for food must never be used for fuel, chemicals or waste afterwards. Keep a dedicated set for water, another for food and a third for waste, colour-coded or clearly marked. Clean and dry containers between uses, and store them with lids off or loosely resting to prevent odour.

## Sealing and Closing Containers

A container is only as useful as its closure. Plastic bottles can be sealed with their original caps, and a second cap or a folded cloth pressed under the lid improves a tight seal. For bags, use a drawstring, a folded top with a tie or a flap that overlaps the opening. Test every closure by tipping the container gently over a clean surface before trusting it with valuable contents.

Waxed paper or wax-soaked cloth can line a bag for dry food and protect against damp. Keep the wax thin and check it regularly, since it cracks with age and handling. Avoid using glue or sealants that are not known to be safe for food.

## Designing for Carrying

Think about weight and balance before you make a container. A bucket should have a handle wide enough to grip with a gloved hand, and a bag strap should sit on the shoulder without cutting into the skin. Keep the total weight a person can comfortably carry in mind, and divide heavy loads between several containers rather than one large one.

**Warning:** Never store drinking water in a container that once held fuel, pesticide or chemicals, even after washing, because residues can leach into the water. Never cook in a container that is cracked or leaching colour. Do not use improvised containers for medicines unless they are clearly labelled and sealed. Keep all containers out of reach of children, since bright plastic and small bottles are tempting and hazardous.

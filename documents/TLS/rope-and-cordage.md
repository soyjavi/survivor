# Rope and Cordage

Rope holds shelters, carries loads and joins tools. Choose strong rope, look after it, and know its limits. The right rope depends on the job: a fixed line needs little stretch, while a line that takes sudden loads needs some.

![Labelled row: four rope-coil types left to right, leader labels paracord, nylon, polyester, plant cord; arrow-both-ways between strength and stretch; strength and stretch trade off, so the load decides the rope.](assets/rope-and-cordage-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Rope is the connection between a shelter and the ground, between a load and a vehicle, and between a person and safety. Most people do not notice rope until it fails, and a failure rarely gives warning. A thin cord that snaps under a tarp in a storm, or a line that frays through an edge, can turn a manageable situation into an injury. Understanding rope lets you match it to the work and spot trouble early.

## Choose rope

- **Paracord:** light, strong and easy to handle. Good for everyday use.
- **Nylon rope:** strong and stretchy. Good for lines that take shock loads.
- **Polyester rope:** strong, does not stretch much, and resists UV. Good for fixed lines.
- **Natural fibre:** biodegradable, but weak when wet. Use only if you have nothing else.

## Care

- Store rope dry and out of sunlight.
- Coil it loosely, not in tight loops that kink.
- Wash salt and mud out with fresh water, and dry it away from heat.

> @image Rope coiled loosely on a wooden rack in a dry shed, photo

## Test strength

Check rope for fraying, glazing, cuts and soft patches. Replace any rope with damage at the core. Bend the rope sharply over your hand and look for a soft or flattened section, which often signals internal damage.

![Cutaway: rope-coil with the sheath pulled back, line-solid core, leader labels sheath, core; arrow-straight marks a flattened soft section with caution; a soft, flat spot signals hidden core damage; NEEDS: rope-cross-section.](assets/rope-and-cordage-2.svg)

## Make cordage from plants

1. Gather long, strong fibres, such as nettle stem or inner bark of certain trees.
2. Soak the fibres and beat them to separate.
3. Twist two strands in one direction, then twist the two together in the opposite direction.

Test homemade cordage before using it with weight.

> @image Plant fibres soaking in a bucket of water before beating, field photo

## Step-by-step in detail

Making a two-ply cord:

1. Split the soaked fibres into bundles of similar thickness, so each strand will carry an equal share.
2. Roll each bundle against your thigh with your palm, in one direction, until it holds a firm twist.
3. Lay two strands end to end with an overlap, and twist them together in the opposite direction to the strand twist.
4. Add new strands at the overlap as the first ones run out, keeping the join spread along the length.
5. Dry the finished cord slowly, under tension, so it keeps its shape and does not stretch unevenly.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Wet conditions** swell natural fibres and weaken them. Keep natural cord dry, and expect it to lose strength after a soak.
- **Cold weather** stiffens synthetic rope and makes knots harder to tie. Warm the rope in a pocket or near the body before use.
- **Heat and sunlight** degrade polyester and nylon over time. Shade the rope when it is not in use, and replace it when it chalks or feels brittle.
- **Sharp edges** cut rope under load. Pad the contact point with cloth or a folded piece of rope.

## Common mistakes

- Storing rope wet, which grows mould and weakens the fibres.
- Tying a knot in synthetic rope without checking it, since some knots lose strength.
- Trusting cordage made in a hurry, with uneven strands.

## Troubleshooting

- **Rope untwists:** the homemade cord was not twisted enough, or the plies were laid in the same direction. Retwist the cord in the correct directions.
- **Cord stretches and thins:** the fibres were weak or unevenly prepared. Discard the section and make a stronger cord from fresh fibres.
- **Knots slip in synthetic rope:** the rope is too slick for the knot. Add an extra turn, or switch to a knot that grips well on that material.
- **Rope smells musty:** moisture is trapped inside. Dry the rope fully in open air before you store it again.

## Checklist

- [ ] Rope matched to the load and the job
- [ ] No fraying, glazing or cut sheath
- [ ] Stored dry and out of sunlight
- [ ] Homemade cordage tested before use

## Practice before you need it

Make a short length of cord from plant fibre once a season, so you learn the feel of a good twist and the time it takes. Test it with a light load in a safe place. Keep a few metres of paracord in the kit, and check its condition every few months, because a rope you have never looked at is a rope you cannot trust.

## When it fails

If a rope shows core damage, cut out the damaged section and tie a new end, or replace the rope. Never splice a damaged section back in and call it sound. A rope that fails under load gives no warning.

**Warning:** never trust a rope with a load it has not been tested for. Overloaded rope fails without warning.

# Basic Lashing and Frames

Lashing joins poles or branches with cordage, creating structures far stronger than the individual pieces. A few lashings, learned well, support most shelters, tables, racks and stretchers. The key principles are tight wraps, neat turns, and joints placed where loads will press them together rather than pull them apart.

> @image Two green poles being lashed together in a clearing, with a coil of rope and a pair of gloves on a flat stump.

## Materials and Preparation

Use strong, rot-resistant cordage such as hemp, nylon or synthetic twine. Choose straight poles of similar thickness, with bark removed where it will sit under the lashing, since bark can slip and rot. Work with cordage about three to four times the length of the area you intend to wrap, and keep the coil tidy so it does not tangle.

1. Dry the poles if they are green, or choose dry ones.
2. Lay the poles side by side and decide the orientation before tying.
3. Start each lashing with a clove hitch or a secure anchor turn.

## The Basic Lashings

The **square lashing** joins two poles that cross at right angles. It is the most important lashing to master. Begin with a clove hitch around the first pole, then wrap the cord around both poles in tight turns, always passing over the crossing. After several wraps, make a frapping turn: wrap the cord around the gap between the poles several times, pulling tight, to draw them together. Finish with a clove hitch or two half hitches.

- A **diagonal lashing** joins two poles crossing at an angle, using a similar wrap that spirals around the joint.
- A **sheer lashing** joins two poles side by side to make a longer pole, wrapped tightly in a series of turns.
- A **tripod lashing** binds three poles at their tops so they stand as a tripod.

## Building a Square Frame

A square frame is useful for a shelter wall, a table, or a rack. Lay four poles in a square with overlapping corners, then lash each corner using square lashing. Check the frame by measuring both diagonals. If they are equal, the frame is square. Adjust one corner before finishing the final lashing.

1. Cut four poles of equal length, with ends trimmed square.
2. Overlap two poles at each corner, with the outer pole sitting on top.
3. Lash each corner with a square lashing, tight and neat.
4. Brace the frame with a diagonal pole if it will carry a load.

## Using Frames in Shelters

Shelters need frames that resist wind and rain. Set the frame on firm ground and tie it to stakes or anchors so it cannot slide. Lean roof poles against a ridge pole and lash them where they touch. Add crossbars for stability and to hold a covering.

- Keep joints as high as practical to avoid wet ground.
- Tension cords evenly so that no single lashing bears the whole load.
- Check lashings daily, since wood shrinks and cords stretch.

## Common Mistakes

The most frequent error is using too few turns. A lashing with only one or two wraps slips under load, and a joint that moves will eventually fail. Aim for at least four to six tight turns in each direction, then frap well. Another mistake is pulling the cord too loosely, which allows the poles to rotate within the lashing.

Placing a joint in the wrong position also causes trouble. Joints should sit where the load pushes them together. A horizontal beam that carries weight should rest on a vertical post, not hang from a lashing alone. Checking this before you tie saves time and effort.

## Maintaining a Frame

Wet cordage shrinks and natural fibres weaken over time, so inspect every lashing after rain and tighten it as needed. Replace any cord that is frayed, stiff or rotten. Keep frames off the ground on stones or pads, and move them away from fires and heat sources.

**Warning:** Test every frame before trusting it with weight. A poorly tied joint can collapse suddenly, causing crush injuries. Never stand on an untested frame, and never place a shelter under dead branches or unstable trees. Wear gloves when cutting poles and handling cordage, since rope burns and splinters are common.

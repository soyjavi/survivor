# Sharpening a Knife

A sharp knife is safer than a blunt one, because it cuts where you intend and needs less force. Learn to sharpen with a stone. Sharpening is slow work, but a steady angle and light pressure produce a better edge than speed does.

![Side view: knife blade at an angle on a stone, bracket arc labelled 15 to 20 degrees, arrow-straight stroke heel to tip; the angle is held constant through each stroke; NEEDS: sharpening-stone.](assets/sharpening-a-knife-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Food preparation, cord cutting and firewood work all depend on the edge. A blunt knife tears where it should slice, and it pushes the hand harder against the work, which raises the risk of a slip. Sharpening also extends the life of the knife itself. Small, regular touch-ups remove less steel than a rebuild after the edge has rolled or chipped, so a little attention each week keeps the knife useful for years.

## Angle

Most kitchen and camp knives are sharpened at about 15 to 20 degrees per side. Keep the angle steady. A useful trick: place a coin under the spine to set the angle.

![Coin under the blade spine on a stone, knife, arrow-straight angle arrow, leader labels coin, spine; the coin lifts the spine to set the angle, so the same coin repeats it; NEEDS: coin, sharpening-stone.](assets/sharpening-a-knife-2.svg)

## Using a stone

1. Wet the stone with water, or use oil if the stone requires it.
2. Hold the blade at the set angle, with the edge leading.
3. Draw the blade across the stone, edge first, with light pressure.
4. Work each side the same number of times.
5. Move to a finer stone for a polished edge.

> @image Hands holding a knife at an angle on a wet whetstone, workshop photo

## Step-by-step in detail

Building a bevel on a coarse stone:

1. Set the stone on a towel so it cannot slide, and check the surface is flat by drawing a marker across it.
2. Place the blade heel first on the stone at the set angle, then lower the spine gently.
3. Push the blade forward in a smooth arc, moving from heel to tip so the whole edge touches the stone.
4. Lift the blade at the end of each stroke, then return it to the start without pressing.
5. After about ten strokes on each side, look for a thin, bright line along the edge. That line, called a burr, shows the edge has reached the far side.
6. Remove the burr by passing the blade lightly on the stone with the back edge leading.

## Using a steel

Hold the steel upright, and draw the blade down and across it, from heel to tip, at the same angle. Alternate sides.

![Blade drawn down a steel: knife, vertical steel, arrow-both-ways alternating sides, bracket fixed angle; labels: heel, tip, left, right; the steel is held upright and strokes alternate sides; NEEDS: honing-steel.](assets/sharpening-a-knife-4.svg)

## Test the edge

Draw the edge gently across a fingernail. A sharp edge catches. Never test with your finger on the blade.

> @image Close view of a blade edge with light catching a fine bright line along it, photo

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Cold weather** makes stones and oil thicken. Keep the stone indoors and warm before use, and work at a steady pace.
- **Dirty or dusty work areas** scratch the blade and fill the stone pores. Clean the stone with clean water and a soft brush after each session.
- **Dull, chipped or very thick blades** need a coarser stone and more strokes. Use a finer stone only once the edge is already even.
- **Serrated edges** cannot be sharpened on a flat stone. Use a round rod made for serrations, or accept some loss of cutting ability.

## Common mistakes

- Changing the angle partway through, which leaves a rounded edge.
- Pressing too hard, which wears the stone unevenly and can chip the blade.
- Working one side far more than the other, which pulls the edge off centre.

## Troubleshooting

- **Edge will not catch the fingernail:** the bevel is not yet formed. Continue on the coarse stone for more strokes, keeping the angle constant.
- **Burr breaks off and returns:** the edge is thin and rolling. Use lighter pressure and finish with a few strokes on the finer stone.
- **Stone clogs and grinds:** the surface is loaded with metal dust. Scrub it with water and a soft brush, then continue.
- **Scratches appear on the blade face:** grit has been trapped under the blade. Clean the stone and the blade before sharpening further.

## Checklist

- [ ] Stone wet or oiled as required
- [ ] Angle set and held steady
- [ ] Same number of strokes on each side
- [ ] Edge tested on a fingernail

## Practice before you need it

Sharpen an old or cheap knife at home before you rely on your main blade. Practise holding a steady angle for a full minute of strokes, and check the result with the fingernail test. Keep a towel, a small bottle of water and a fine stone in the kit, so the knife can be maintained during a long stay.

## When it fails

If the edge will not catch, check that you are working the same angle along the whole blade. If a burr forms on one side only, change the number of strokes on that side. If the blade chips, stop and use a coarser stone to reshape the edge.

**Warning:** always move the blade away from your body and your hands. Keep a towel under the stone to stop it sliding.

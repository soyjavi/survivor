# Estimating Height and Distance

Without a rangefinder or tape measure, you can still judge how far away a landmark is and how tall a tree or cliff stands. Field estimation relies on known body measurements, a fixed hand span and some geometry. These methods are rough, typically within ten to twenty percent, and they are most useful when you calibrate your own body against a known measure first.

## Calibrating Your Pace

Your pace is the distance covered by one natural stride. To calibrate it, walk a known distance such as a 100-metre stretch of road or a marked length. Walk at your normal speed, count steps, and divide the distance by the count. Repeat three times and average the results. Terrain changes the pace, so calibrate separately for flat ground, slopes and rough ground.

1. Choose a straight, level route and measure its length beforehand.
2. Walk it at a relaxed, natural speed, counting every time your left foot lands.
3. Divide the length by your step count to get metres per step.
4. Repeat on different surfaces and note the results.

## Pacing Distance

Once calibrated, count your paces over the ground you need to measure. Walk in a straight line, keeping your stride even. Uphill and downhill walking shortens or lengthens paces, so count on level ground where possible. For long distances, count in blocks of one hundred paces and mark each block with a small stone or knot in a cord.

- Note the wind and visibility, since both can make distances seem shorter or longer.
- Keep a record of total paces, not just estimated kilometres.

## Estimating Height With Your Hand

A closed fist held at arm's length subtends roughly ten degrees of view. The width of your thumb subtends about two degrees, and your little finger about one degree. To measure height, hold your arm straight out, close one eye, and count how many fists or fingers fit between the base and the top of the object. Multiply by the known angle to estimate the angle of the object, then use that angle with a known distance.

1. Stand a known distance from the object, such as ten paces.
2. Stretch your arm out and align the bottom of the object with your fist.
3. Count how many fists fit from the base to the top.
4. Multiply the fist count by ten metres per fist at a distance of about one hundred metres, adjusting for your own distance.

## Angles and Heights by Triangles

The most reliable method uses a known height and a shadow or sight line. Stand a known distance from a tree, look up to the top, and note the angle using a hand or stick held at arm's length. If your eye is at height h above the ground and the distance is d, the object's height is roughly h plus d times the tangent of the angle. Practise this on objects with a known height before relying on it.

## Rough Measures in Practice

- A tree's height is often close to its trunk girth times ten, a rough rule for many species.
- A person's height is about the same as their arm span, which provides a quick comparison.
- The distance to a sound can be estimated by counting seconds between a flash and its thunder, dividing by three to get kilometres.

## Checking Your Estimates

Compare two methods wherever possible. If pacing and the hand method give similar answers for the same object, the estimate is more trustworthy. When they disagree widely, look for the cause: a slope, a stride change, or an object partly hidden by ground or vegetation. Record the reasons for the disagreement so the next estimate is better informed.

Keep notes in a consistent form, with the method, the distance or height, the conditions and the date. Over time, these records show how accurate each method is in your terrain and season.

## Working in Poor Light

Estimates are harder at dusk or in fog, when edges blur and depth is hard to judge. Stop and wait for better light where possible, or use larger safety margins. Fog in particular makes distant objects appear farther away than they are, so assume a greater distance than the one that looks right.

**Warning:** Do not walk toward a cliff edge, unstable slope or river crossing to make an estimate. Always keep a safe distance from anything that might fall or collapse, and never stand under dead trees when estimating their height. Estimates are rough, so add a generous safety margin before planning a route, a crossing or a climb.

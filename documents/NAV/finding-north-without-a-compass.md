# Finding North Without a Compass

If the compass is lost or broken, the sun and stars still give direction. Each method has limits, so use two where possible. Confirm your result before you commit to a long walk, because a small error at the start grows with every kilometre.

## Why it matters

A compass can be lost in a river, broken in a fall or forgotten at home. Without a way to find direction, people walk in circles, and a circle in unknown country is one of the most common ways a lost walker gets into trouble.

Natural methods are also useful as a check. A bearing from a compass that disagrees with the sun by a wide margin tells you that something is wrong, whether the needle is affected by metal or the map has been misread. Two independent methods make that disagreement visible.

## Materials

- An analogue watch, or a stick you can push into the ground.
- A clear view of the sun or, after dark, the stars.
- A pencil or a stone to mark shadows on the ground.

## The sun and a watch

1. Hold the watch flat and point the hour hand at the sun.
2. Find the midpoint between the hour hand and twelve o'clock. That line points south in the northern hemisphere, and north in the southern hemisphere.
3. If the watch is set to daylight saving time, use the time one hour behind the clock time before you point the hour hand.

![Top view: ground-line with shadow-stick, two waypoint-pin tips, line-solid between them with direction-of-travel; labels first tip, second tip 15-20 min, west, east; level ground is needed for a true east-west line.](assets/finding-north-without-a-compass-2.svg)

## The sun's shadow

1. Place a stick upright in flat ground.
2. Mark the tip of its shadow, wait 15 to 20 minutes, and mark it again.
3. The line between the two marks runs roughly east to west. The first mark is west.

## The North Star

In the northern hemisphere, the North Star (Polaris) sits almost exactly north. Find the Big Dipper, follow the two outer stars of its bowl upward, and the North Star lies along that line. In the southern hemisphere, use the Southern Cross to find south.

## Step-by-step in detail

Use the shadow method first when you have a few minutes, then check it with the watch method. A shadow line is fast and easy to read, while the watch method is quick to repeat if you move to a new spot.

| Method | Needs | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Watch and sun | Watch, sunshine | Quick to repeat | Sensitive to tilt |
| Stick shadow | Stick, 15 to 20 minutes | Clear east-west line | Needs flat ground |
| North Star | Clear night sky | Very steady | Only after dark |

Mark both results on the ground or on a note, and if they agree within a rough margin, use the average direction. If they do not agree, move a few metres and repeat both methods before you choose.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Wind and wet ground:** a stick is harder to keep upright in wind, so hold it steady or lean it against a rock.
- **Low sun:** shadows are long and the sun is low, so the watch method becomes less precise close to dusk or dawn.
- **Hills:** a slope bends the shadow line. Place the stick on the flattest patch you can find.
- **Cloud:** if the sun is hidden for more than a few minutes, stop the test and wait rather than guessing.

## Practice before you need it

Try both methods in a park on a clear day, and compare the results with a compass you carry for practice. Repeat the test in the evening with the North Star, and learn how to find it from the Big Dipper. A method you have checked at home is more reliable when your hands are cold and your heart is racing.

## Common mistakes

- Holding the watch tilted, which moves the hour hand off the true sun line.
- Forgetting the daylight saving adjustment, which turns the result by about 15 degrees.
- Trusting a single method without a second check.
- Placing the stick on a slope, which bends the shadow and turns the east-west line.

## Troubleshooting

- **The two marks lie almost on top of each other:** wait longer, at least 15 minutes, so the shadow has moved far enough to read.
- **The North Star is hard to find:** look for a clear, dark patch of sky away from town lights, and check the Big Dipper's position first.
- **The result disagrees with the map:** check the map orientation, then repeat the shadow test on flat ground before you trust either.

## Checklist

- [ ] Two methods tried, and their results compared.
- [ ] Watch held flat, or stick placed on level ground.
- [ ] Result written down before you walk.

## When it fails

If the sky is covered, do not guess. Stay near a landmark you know, and wait for the sun or stars to return. A direction you cannot confirm is not safe to follow over a long distance.

**Warning:** these methods depend on clear skies. Cloud or nightfall can take them away, so carry a map and a compass whenever you can.

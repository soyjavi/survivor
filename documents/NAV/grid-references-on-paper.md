# Grid References on Paper

A grid reference gives a precise position on a printed map. It is the clearest way to tell rescuers where you are, because it removes the guesswork that comes from describing a place in words.

![Map close-up: map with table-grid square centre, leader from bottom edge to step-1 label easting, leader from side edge to step-2 label northing, bracket for tenths; labels easting, northing, tenths; easting is read first, then northing, each with tenths.](assets/grid-references-on-paper-1.svg)

## Why it matters

A call that says "near the lake" can cover many square kilometres. A six-figure reference narrows the search to a square of about 100 metres, which can save hours when light is fading and the caller is cold.

A reference also works as a shared language. A searcher, a rescuer and a family member who all read the same square on the same map are looking at the same place, even if none of them has walked there before.

## Materials

- A printed map of the correct scale, with its grid lines visible.
- A pencil and a scrap of paper.
- A ruler or the edge of a card, to help you line up the grid lines.

## Read a grid reference

1. Find the grid lines on the map. They form squares with numbers along the edges.
2. Give the easting first: read along the bottom or top edge to the left of your position, using the numbers printed on the grid line.
3. Estimate the tenths of the grid square to the east, to add one more digit.
4. Give the northing next: read up the side edge to the grid line below your position, then estimate the tenths above it.

A reference such as 123456 means the easting is 123 and 4 tenths, and the northing is 456 and 6 tenths, within the square.

## Figures and square sizes

The number of digits sets how small the square is. Use the level your situation needs, and never claim more precision than the map and your estimate support.

| Figures | Example | Square size |
|---|---|---|
| Four | 1234 | 1 km by 1 km |
| Six | 123456 | 100 m by 100 m |
| Eight | 12344567 | 10 m by 10 m |

![Comparison: table-header and table-grid rows for 4, 6 and 8 figures, beside three nested squares joined by arrow-straight; labels 1 km, 100 m, 10 m; each extra pair of figures shrinks the square tenfold.](assets/grid-references-on-paper-3.svg)

## Keep it precise

- Use the same grid system as the map, and say which one it is.
- Give the square's name or number first, then the figures.
- Say the figures in pairs: easting, then northing.

## Practice before you need it

Choose a landmark on your home map, such as a church or a bridge. Read its six-figure reference from the map alone, then check it against the map again with a ruler. Do this for three different landmarks, and time yourself. Practice in a quiet room first, then outdoors, where the map may flap in the wind and your hands may be cold.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Rain:** protect the map in a clear sleeve, and write the reference on paper that will not run.
- **Low light:** use a torch held close to the map, not from above, so that the grid lines do not cast shadows.
- **Wind:** fold the map to the section you need, so you can keep it steady with both hands.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the northing first, which swaps the two figures.
- Dropping a zero, which moves the position by a whole kilometre.
- Confusing the map's grid with the north line, which points in a different direction.
- Reading the reference from the wrong map sheet, when two sheets share an edge.

## Troubleshooting

- **The reference lands in the wrong place:** check that you read the easting from the bottom or top edge, and the northing from the side edge.
- **The square name is not on the map:** the map may be a different sheet. Check the margin for the sheet number, and find the sheet that covers your position.
- **The listener cannot find the square:** give the nearest landmark, and ask them to read the reference back.

## Checklist

- [ ] Grid system named.
- [ ] Easting read before northing.
- [ ] Tenths estimated for both figures.
- [ ] Reference repeated back by the listener.

## When it fails

If you are not sure of your position, give the nearest landmark as well, and say how confident you are. A reference with a note such as "on the road, 200 metres past the bridge" is far more useful than a precise figure you cannot check.

**Warning:** a grid reference is only as accurate as the map and your estimate. Give the nearest landmark too.

# Evacuation Routes

An evacuation route is only useful if you know it works. Find at least two from home, and walk or drive them before you need them. A road that looks simple on a map can be blocked by traffic, debris, floodwater or a closed bridge, so testing it is the only reliable check.

![Route map: map with waypoint-pin home centre, arrow-straight primary route one way, arrow-branch secondary route another way, river with warning at shared crossing; labels home, primary, secondary, hazard; shared first road means not two routes.](assets/evacuation-routes-1.svg)

## Why it matters

When a warning comes, minutes count. People who have never checked their routes often lose time choosing a road on the spot, and crowds on the main road slow everyone down. A known route, with a known second option, lets you leave calmly and early.

## Materials

- A paper map of your area, with both routes marked in different colours.
- A written list of phone numbers, in case the phone runs out of battery.
- A small kit in the car with water, a torch and a first aid kit.

## Find the routes

1. Mark your home on the map. Mark work and your children's school too, if they are part of your daily life.
2. Mark the official evacuation routes, which local authorities often publish.
3. Choose a primary route and a secondary route in a different direction. Two routes that share the same first road are not really two routes.
4. Avoid routes past rivers, dams, dense forest and steep slopes if those are the hazards you face.

## Check the routes

- Walk or drive each route at least once a year, at the time of day you would most likely use it.
- Note the time it takes, and any hazards: narrow roads, bridges and low points.
- Note the places where the route may be blocked, such as level crossings, tunnels and busy junctions.
- Check that signs are easy to see at night.

## Practice before you need it

Once a season, run a drill. Choose a weekday morning, set a timer, and leave home exactly as you would in a warning, with the bag, the documents and the family in the car. Record the time at each checkpoint, and write down anything that slowed you down. Then drive the secondary route and compare the two logs. The difference tells you which route to trust under pressure and where the bottlenecks sit.

| Checkpoint | Time from home | Hazard noted |
|---|---|---|
| First main junction | 5 minutes | Busy at school pick-up |
| River bridge | 15 minutes | Single lane, slows traffic |
| Edge of town | 25 minutes | Clear road ahead |

## Step-by-step in detail

1. Start the drill at a set time, with the car loaded with what you would really take.
2. Write the time at each checkpoint on the paper map, beside the route line.
3. At every hazard, note whether it can be passed safely at night, in rain and in heavy traffic.
4. Repeat the drive on the secondary route, keeping the same start time and load.
5. Write a short summary on the back of the map: which route is faster, which is safer, and what to avoid.
6. Share the summary with everyone in the household and with the neighbours who agreed to help.

## Keep them open

- Keep your car and the route clear of fuel, rubbish and debris.
- Keep the fuel tank at least half full during a warning season.
- Agree with neighbours to share the route and warn each other.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Night:** signs and road edges are harder to see, so test each route in the dark at least once.
- **Winter:** check steep hills and shaded bends for ice, and keep a warm layer, gloves and a blanket in the car.
- **Flood season:** prefer routes on high ground, and treat any road that runs beside a river as the first to fail.

## Use them

When a warning comes, follow the official route first. If it is blocked, use the second route. Tell the family where you are going and when you expect to arrive.

## Troubleshooting

- **The car will not start:** do not wait for it to fix itself. Walk with the bag to the meeting point, or use the public transport option you noted earlier.
- **A road is closed with no sign:** turn around safely, and take the second route from the nearest junction you can reach.
- **The phone has no signal:** use the paper map and the written phone numbers.

## Common mistakes

- Relying only on a phone map, which may not load without signal.
- Choosing a second route that crosses the same river or bridge as the first.
- Leaving the check until the warning arrives.

## Checklist

- [ ] Two routes marked on paper.
- [ ] Each route walked or driven this year.
- [ ] Hazards and blockage points noted.
- [ ] Family and neighbours know the plan.

## When a route fails

If the first road is blocked, stop, check the official updates, and switch to the second route at the next junction you can reach safely. Do not turn back into the danger area to try again.

**Warning:** do not take a shortcut through unknown or flooded land. Official routes are chosen because they are safer.

# Planning Routes

A planned route is safer than an improvised one. Plan it on paper, check it against the terrain, and keep a second route ready. A route is a set of decisions made in calm conditions, so you do not have to make them under stress.

![Map: map with line-solid main route and line-dashed alternative from one waypoint-pin, further waypoint-pin checkpoints along both; labels main, alternative, checkpoints; where the routes meet are the natural decision points.](assets/planning-routes-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Under pressure, people choose the shortest-looking line, which often crosses water, cliffs or blocked roads. A plan made in advance lets you judge distance, time and hazards before they become a problem.

A plan also gives the people waiting for you something to check. If they know your checkpoints and your expected times, a late arrival at the first point becomes a signal to act rather than a cause for guessing.

## Materials

- A map of the right scale, a ruler and a pencil.
- A notebook for checkpoints, times and contact details.
- A copy of the plan for a trusted person.

## Plan the main route

1. Mark the start and destination on the map.
2. Draw the route along roads, tracks or paths that are known to be usable.
3. Measure the distance with a ruler against the map scale.
4. Mark checkpoints every few kilometres, with names or landmarks.

## Read the terrain

Contour lines tell you where the climbs are. Lines drawn close together show steep ground, while widely spaced lines show gentle slopes. A route that follows a river valley is often flat but may flood, and a route over a ridge gives views but takes more effort. Choose the line that matches the time, the group and the weather.

## Add alternatives

- Pick a second route in a different direction.
- Check it for water crossings, steep ground and dead ends.
- Note the places where the routes meet, as these are your natural decision points.

## Estimate the time

Walking on flat, good ground covers about 4 to 5 km per hour. Add time for rest and for slow ground, steep ground and carried loads. A safe rule is to double the time for a route with many climbs.

| Terrain | Typical speed | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Flat, firm path | 4 to 5 km per hour | Best case for a fit group |
| Rough or wet ground | 2 to 3 km per hour | Watch every step |
| Steep climbs | Slower again | Add rest at each rise |

## Share the plan

- Give a copy of the route to the family, a trusted contact, or the meeting point.
- Agree when you will check in, and what to do if you do not arrive.

## Practice before you need it

Choose a shorter route near home and plan it exactly as you would a real one. Walk it with the plan in your pocket, and compare the actual times with the estimate. Note the checkpoints you missed or passed too early, and adjust the next plan. A few practice runs make your estimates far more trustworthy.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Wet weather:** streams rise and paths become slippery, so add time and avoid low crossings.
- **Heat:** plan the hardest climbs for the cool morning, and schedule long rests in shade.
- **Short daylight:** count back from sunset, and allow the full estimate plus a margin before dark.
- **Group changes:** a child or a walker with a sore knee changes the speed for everyone, so plan for the slowest person.

## Troubleshooting

- **The estimate looks too short:** double-check the scale, then add more time for steep sections.
- **A checkpoint is missing on the ground:** stop and wait at the last confirmed point, and check the map for a nearby alternative.
- **The plan no longer fits the weather:** shorten the route to the nearest safe checkpoint, and inform the contact of the change.

## Common mistakes

- Using the straight-line distance without adding for the real path.
- Forgetting that the time includes rest stops.
- Sharing the plan with nobody, so no one knows to raise the alarm.
- Planning a route across water you have never seen, based only on a line on the map.

## Checklist

- [ ] Main route drawn on a usable road or path.
- [ ] Checkpoints marked every few kilometres.
- [ ] Second route checked for hazards.
- [ ] Time estimate doubled for steep routes.
- [ ] Copy given to a trusted contact.

## When the route is blocked

Go to the nearest checkpoint, check the situation, and choose the alternative route from the meeting points you have already noted. Update the plan on paper, and tell your contact about the change.

**Warning:** do not trust a route you have not seen or checked, especially across rivers or through hazards.

# Walking Distances and Pace

Knowing how far you can walk turns a map into a plan. Test your pace on the ground, and adjust for the people and loads you carry. A pace measured on a flat road at home will not match a day on steep, wet ground with a heavy pack.

## Why it matters

Walking too far in a day exhausts people and leads to falls and poor decisions. Walking too little leaves you short of shelter when darkness comes. An honest pace estimate keeps the day within the group's ability.

Pace also decides what you can promise. If the group plans to reach a checkpoint by four in the afternoon, the estimate must be honest enough that the promise holds even when the weather turns or a child slows down.

## Materials

- A tape measure, or a long cord marked at 100 metres.
- A watch or a timer, for the pace test.
- A notebook, to record your average pace and step count.

## Test your pace

1. Measure 100 metres on flat ground, with a tape or by pacing it.
2. Walk it at a steady pace, and count your steps or time it.
3. Repeat three times and average the results.

## Average speeds

- Flat ground with a light load: about 4 to 5 km per hour.
- Uphill or on rough ground: about 2 to 3 km per hour.
- With children or heavy loads: slower, plan for about 3 km per hour.

## Step-by-step in detail

Use the step count from the test to estimate distance on the ground. Multiply the number of steps you took over 100 metres by the number of 100-metre lengths you expect to cover, then add a margin for turns and detours. Keep the step count for flat ground separate from the one for slopes, because the two differ.

| Factor | Effect on pace |
|---|---|
| Steep uphill | Slows the group the most |
| Wet or muddy path | Slows steps and adds caution |
| Heavy pack | Reduces speed and adds fatigue |
| Children | Set the speed for the whole group |
| Hot weather | Adds rest and water stops |

## Plan the day

- Allow about 20 minutes rest every hour.
- Plan the longest day at no more than about 15 to 20 km for a typical group on foot.
- Stop early enough to build shelter before dark.

## Count the distance

- Use the map to estimate distance in straight lines, then add about 20 per cent for the real path.
- In the dark or in mist, count steps to measure distance.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Heat:** slow the pace by a little, drink often, and rest in the shade during the hottest part of the day.
- **Cold:** keep moving to stay warm, but avoid sweating through your layers, which chills you later.
- **Altitude:** the body tires faster at height, so reduce the planned distance for the day.
- **Rain:** wet ground and reduced visibility slow the group, so allow extra time for each leg.

## Practice before you need it

Repeat the pace test on a path similar to the one you plan to use. Time a full day of walking with a load, including rest stops, and write down the total distance. A practice day shows you the real pace of your group far better than a flat road test.

## Troubleshooting

- **Pace drops suddenly:** check for blisters, cold hands or dehydration, and stop to treat the cause before walking on.
- **The estimate is too optimistic:** shorten the next day's plan, and record the real pace for the next test.
- **One walker keeps falling behind:** share the load, reduce the speed, or change to a route with fewer climbs.

## Common mistakes

- Using the flat-ground speed for the whole day.
- Forgetting that the rest stops are part of the time.
- Planning a long day on the first morning, before the group has warmed up.
- Counting steps on one leg and applying the number to a path with different slope.

## Checklist

- [ ] Pace tested on 100 metres, three times.
- [ ] Speed chosen for the slowest walker.
- [ ] Rest stops built into the time.
- [ ] Shelter site chosen before dark.

## When the group falls behind

Stop, and reassess the plan. Shorten the day, or change to a route with fewer climbs. Do not push the group to make up time, because a late arrival is safer than a collapse on the trail.

**Warning:** do not push a group beyond its limit. Fatigue causes falls, poor decisions and hypothermia.

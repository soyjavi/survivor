# Shelter Priorities

Shelter means controlling the body's heat. Most people in an emergency lose heat faster than they expect, so think about the body first and the building second. A good shelter is one that protects the body from the elements in the right order, not one that simply has four walls.

![Pyramid of five stacked step-box levels, base to top; labels: out of wind, dry, insulated, trap warm air, shade; the base level is most urgent and each level depends on the one below. NEEDS: pyramid](assets/shelter-priorities-1.svg)

## The order

1. **Get out of the wind.** Wind strips warmth from the body faster than cold air does.
2. **Stay dry.** Wet clothing and wet ground pull heat from the body quickly.
3. **Insulate from the ground.** Lie on something between you and the earth.
4. **Trap warm air.** Use layers of clothing and blankets.
5. **Limit exposure to sun and heat** in hot places, with shade and airflow.

> @image Survivor sitting in the lee of a rock out of the wind, with a tarp drawn over a small space (documentary photograph of an improvised shelter)

## Where to shelter

- Choose a site above the flood line, away from dead trees, cliffs and dry riverbeds.
- Use the natural shelter of a building, a hedge or a rock, if it is safe.
- Avoid ridge tops in wind and hollows in cold, where cold air settles.

## Safety first

Never shelter under a dead tree or next to a dead branch. Never shelter in a building that is structurally damaged, even if it looks solid from the outside.

## Make it better

- Put a tarp over the top and sides to shed rain.
- Keep the shelter small, so it is easier to heat with body warmth.
- Ventilate, especially if you use a fire.

![Side by side: slope with arrow-straight cold air pooling in a hollow, and a ridge with arrow-straight wind; labels: hollow, ridge, cold air; low ground is colder, not safer. NEEDS: cold-air-arrow](assets/shelter-priorities-2.svg)

## Why the order matters

The body loses heat through wind, wet, ground contact and air movement, and each of these works faster than most people expect. Fixing the wind before adding insulation is worth more than a thicker blanket, because a blanket soaked by wind-driven rain loses its value quickly. The order also gives a clear sequence when time and materials are short.

## Setting up step by step

1. Find the most sheltered spot, and set the shelter with its back to the wind.
2. Lay down the ground layer before anything else inside.
3. Pitch the cover over the top and along the windward side, keeping the leeward side open for air.
4. Add the bedding and clothing layers once the first three are secure.
5. Check for drafts, and close small gaps with cloth or leaves.

## Reading the signs

- Shivering that is hard to control means the layers are not holding heat.
- Damp patches under the bedding mean the ground protection has failed.
- A shelter that rattles or flaps means the cover is not secure.
- Warm, stale air with condensation means the shelter needs more airflow.

## Practice before you need it

Set up a small shelter once in a park or garden, using only the materials you carry. Time the job, and check the order in which you worked. Note what you forgot, such as a knife or cord, and add it to your kit before the next trip.

## Choosing between two sites

If two sites are available, compare them on the five priorities, starting with wind. A dry hollow sheltered by a bank is usually better than an exposed ridge, even if the ridge has the better view. Walk both sites at the time of day you plan to arrive, so you see how the light and wind change.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a site for the view rather than for protection from wind.
- Building a large shelter that is impossible to keep warm.
- Forgetting the ground layer and trying to fix cold with a bigger roof.

## Checklist

- Wind is blocked on the windward side.
- The site is dry and above any flood line.
- Dead trees and branches are avoided.
- The ground is insulated.
- Air can move, even when the shelter is closed.

## When it fails

If the shelter is cold, check the ground layer and the draughts before adding more cover. If it becomes wet, move the bedding and rebuild the drainage, starting on the uphill side. If the site is no longer safe, move to a better one before dark.

**Warning:** do not seal a shelter so tightly that it fills with smoke or carbon monoxide. Keep an opening for air whenever a fire is burning.

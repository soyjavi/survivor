# Building a Debris Hut

A debris hut is a small, insulated shelter built from natural material. It traps your body heat in a dense pile of leaves, needles and grass, and it can be built with nothing more than a few poles and a lot of organic matter. The hut works best when it is small, well-insulated and built on a site that stays dry. Its strength comes from the insulation, not from the frame.

## Choosing the Site

A good site decides whether the hut will keep you warm or leave you cold and wet.

1. Look for a flat or gently sloping spot with good drainage, away from hollows where cold air and water collect.
2. Avoid standing dead trees, loose branches and rock falls overhead.
3. Choose a place sheltered from the prevailing wind, such as the lee side of a fallen tree or a boulder.
4. Check that there are plenty of leaves, needles and dry ground cover within easy carrying distance.

## Building the Frame

The frame is simple and only needs to hold the weight of the insulation.

1. Find a sturdy ridgepole about two metres long and rest one end on a fork or log, with the other end on the ground or a low support.
2. Lean smaller poles and branches along both sides of the ridgepole to form ribs, spacing them roughly every thirty centimetres.
3. Tie or wedge the ribs together where they meet the ridgepole so the frame does not slide.
4. Keep the overall shape low and narrow, just wide enough for your body to lie in with a small margin.

## Adding the Insulation

Insulation makes the difference between a survivable hut and a comfortable one.

- Pile dry leaves, needles, grass and moss onto the frame in layers, starting from the bottom and working upward.
- Aim for a depth of at least sixty centimetres over your body on the roof and sides, and more if the nights are very cold.
- Pack the material down as you go, so it does not settle and leave gaps.
- Add extra material to the floor, at least thirty centimetres deep, because the ground draws heat from your body faster than the air does.
- Check the insulation by feeling the inside walls. If you can feel cold air coming through, add more.

## Positioning the Entrance

The entrance is the weak point of the hut, so design it with care.

- Place the entrance on the side facing away from the wind.
- Keep it small, just large enough to crawl through, so that the hut stays warm inside.
- Build a short, insulated tunnel or a flap of material to cover the entrance at night.
- Do not block the entrance completely, because you need air to circulate and prevent carbon dioxide from building up.

## Inside the Hut

Once you are inside, keep the space manageable and dry.

1. Lay a thick layer of dry insulation under your body, then add your sleeping gear on top.
2. Keep your clothing dry and avoid bringing wet items inside the hut.
3. Carry a small amount of cordage and a knife to repair the frame or adjust insulation during the night.
4. Make a small opening at the top of the hut or the side of the entrance so that you can breathe fresh air without losing too much heat.

## Safety Checks

Before you lie down, check that the hut will not collapse and that you can get out quickly.

- Test the frame by pressing lightly on the roof. If it bends or shifts, reinforce it before sleeping.
- Make sure that no part of the hut is in contact with a fire, a stove or a heat source.
- Keep a torch inside the hut so you can see the entrance and the frame at night.
- Tell someone outside your location and expected return time.

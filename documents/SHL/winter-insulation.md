# Winter Insulation

Good insulation keeps a home warm for longer on the same fuel. Most of the work is free or cheap. Start with the places where heat escapes fastest, then add layers where the house is still cold, and check the results after each change.

![Cutaway: hut, arrow-straight at roof, walls, doors and windows with the roof arrow largest, tip callouts for cheap insulation at each; labels: roof, walls, doors, windows; heat escapes fastest through the roof. NEEDS: house, door, window](assets/winter-insulation-1.svg)

## Where heat is lost

- Ceilings and roofs, because warm air rises.
- Windows and doors, through gaps and single glazing.
- Floors, especially over cold cellars or crawl spaces.
- Walls, where there is no insulation.

> @image Attic space with loose straw and blankets laid over the joists (documentary photograph of a home insulation project)

## Low-cost steps

1. **Draught-proof** doors and windows with tape, foam or towels. See Sealing a Room Against the Cold.
2. **Cover windows** with thick curtains, or plastic sheeting taped to the frame.
3. **Insulate the loft or attic** with any spare material: blankets, straw, newspaper.
4. **Rug the floors** and use cardboard under mats.
5. **Close off unused rooms** and keep their doors shut.

## Use what you have

- Newspaper packed into a wall cavity or under a window is effective.
- Straw bales are good insulation for outside walls and sheds.
- Old duvets and blankets can be hung over doors and windows.

## Where to start

Start with the attic, since heat rises and a thin layer there saves more fuel than almost anything else. Then seal the doors and windows of the room you use most. Leave the rest for later, once you know where the cold still comes in.

## Why insulation works

Insulation slows the movement of heat from warm air to cold air. It does not create heat, so its value comes from keeping what the stove or body already makes. A layer that traps still air, such as a blanket, straw or newspaper, is the cheapest way to achieve this, provided it stays dry and in place.

## Choosing materials

| Material | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Newspaper | Wall cavities, under windows | Must stay dry |
| Straw | Outside walls, sheds | Can attract rodents |
| Blankets | Doors, windows, loft | Bulky to store |
| Cardboard | Under rugs and mats | Soaks up moisture |

## Step-by-step in the loft

1. Clear a path, and check the ceiling below for signs of damp.
2. Lay the first layer between the joists, filling the gaps without pressing it down hard.
3. Add a second layer across the joists, so the gaps in the first are covered.
4. Keep the layer clear of any light fittings, and never cover a loft vent.

## Measuring the results

Check a room a day after each change. Hold a hand near the wall, the window and the floor, and compare the feel with the room before the work. Keep a note of the room temperature in the morning and evening, so the effect of each step is clear.

## Cost and priorities

Most of the work costs little more than tape, cardboard and a few hours of labour. Spend first on the attic and on the room used most in the evening. Keep a record of what each material cost and what it changed, so the next round of work goes where it will help most. Replace any insulation that becomes wet, since soaked material loses most of its value.

## Common mistakes

- Insulating the attic while leaving the ground floor open to draughts.
- Packing insulation against a flue or chimney, where it can catch fire.
- Stacking materials so tightly that moisture cannot escape, which causes mould.

## Checklist

- Attic has a layer of insulation.
- Doors and windows are draught-proofed.
- Windows have a second layer of curtain or plastic.
- Floors are covered with rugs or cardboard.
- Unused rooms are closed off.

## When it fails

If a room stays cold after insulating, check for a gap you have not found yet, using a lit candle near the frame and the skirting. If condensation appears on the windows, open a small vent to let damp air escape, and remove any insulation that has become wet.

**Warning:** insulation can be flammable. Keep it away from heaters and fires, and do not seal vents for combustion appliances. They need air to burn safely.

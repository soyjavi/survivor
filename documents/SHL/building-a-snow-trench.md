# Building a Snow Trench

A snow trench is one of the simplest ways to shelter from wind and cold when you have a deep, stable snowpack and a shovel. The snow itself insulates well, and a trench keeps you out of the wind. The main dangers are collapse and carbon dioxide build-up, and both can be managed with careful siting, sensible dimensions and an open vent.

## Choosing the Site

A trench works only where the snow is deep enough and stable enough to hold its shape.

1. Look for at least sixty centimetres of consolidated snow, with no layers of weak, sugary snow near the surface.
2. Avoid slopes steeper than about thirty degrees, cornices, and areas below avalanche paths.
3. Stay away from trees with heavy snow loads, because branches can drop without warning.
4. Choose a spot sheltered from the prevailing wind, such as the lee side of a ridge or a dense stand of trees.
5. Test the snow by pressing a shovel blade into it. If the snow cracks or slides, move on.

## Dimensions

Keep the trench just big enough to lie or sit in, because a smaller space is easier to keep warm and safer to dig.

- Dig the trench about two metres long, enough for a person to lie down, and about one metre wide.
- Keep the floor about forty centimetres below the surface of the snow, so you have walls on all sides.
- Make the sides slightly sloped inward at the top, at about a twenty degree angle, to reduce the chance of collapse.
- Build the roof no lower than about seventy centimetres above the floor, so you can sit up.

## Digging Method

Work in pairs if you can, and rotate the digging to avoid exhaustion.

1. Mark the outline of the trench in the snow with a stick or a ski pole.
2. Dig the top layer first, throwing the spoil well away from the site.
3. Dig down to the floor depth, keeping the walls smooth and straight.
4. Pack the snow along the walls with the back of the shovel to make them firmer.
5. Dig a short entrance tunnel at one end, then cut a sloping ramp down into the trench.

## Roof and Entrance

The roof is the part most likely to fail, and the entrance is the route for air and people.

- Cover the trench with branches, skis, a tarp or a sheet of plywood, then pile snow over the top.
- Leave the roof at least thirty centimetres thick, and add more on the windward side.
- Build the entrance on the side away from the wind, with a short tunnel that turns once so that drifting snow does not blow straight in.
- Seal the entrance at night with a door made of a pack, a piece of wood or a block of snow, leaving a small vent.

## Ventilation

Carbon dioxide from your breathing builds up in a sealed trench, so ventilation is essential.

- Make a vent hole about ten centimetres across through the roof, at the far end from the entrance.
- Mark the vent with a stick or a bright cloth so that it can be found and cleared.
- Check the vent regularly and clear any snow that drifts into it.
- Keep a small opening in the entrance door, even when it is closed for warmth.

## Avoiding Collapse

Collapse usually happens because the walls are too steep, the roof is too thin, or the trench was dug too close to the edge of a slope.

- Keep the trench in the middle of a stable area, at least two metres from any slope edge.
- Check the roof and walls every few hours, especially after new snowfall or wind.
- If the roof sags or cracks, get out and repair it before going back in.
- Never tunnel under an unstable overhang, and do not stand on the roof.

## Keeping Warm Inside

Sit on insulation such as spare clothing or a sleeping mat. Keep your clothes dry, and do not let snow melt on your body or equipment, because wet clothing loses its warmth fast.

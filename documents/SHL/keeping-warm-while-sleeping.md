# Keeping Warm While Sleeping

Most cold-related harm happens at night, when people sleep and stop moving. Sleep warm, dry and insulated from the ground. The body produces less heat during sleep, so a bed that works in the evening may not be enough by morning.

## Why it matters

During sleep the body's core temperature drops, the muscles stop producing the small heat they make when moving, and the skin loses warmth to whatever it touches. A sleeper who is only slightly cold at bedtime can become dangerously cold by dawn. Many people also wake up without noticing, because shivering eases as they drift off. Planning the night in advance is the simplest way to avoid this.

## Insulate from the ground

- Lie on at least two layers between you and the ground: a mat, dry leaves, folded blankets, or cardboard.
- Ground insulation matters more than the top covering. Cold ground takes heat from the body fast, even through a thick blanket.

## Dress for sleep

- Wear dry, loose layers. Do not sleep in wet clothes.
- Wear a hat, since a lot of heat leaves through the head.
- Keep socks dry and spare, and change them if they get wet.

## Build the bed

1. Use a blanket or sleeping bag rated for the cold you expect.
2. Add a second layer on top, and tuck it around the body.
3. Keep the head and face covered, but leave the mouth and nose free for breathing.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In very cold nights,** add a third layer, and put a hot water bottle filled with warm (not boiling) water inside the bedding, wrapped in cloth.
- **In damp conditions,** keep a waterproof layer under the bedding, not over it, so moisture from the ground stays out.
- **For children,** add one layer more than an adult would use, and check their hands and feet before bed, since small bodies cool faster.
- **For older people,** keep a light by the bed and a drink close, so they do not need to get up and go into the cold.

## Share warmth

Two people sleeping together under one covering stay warmer. Children, older people and those who are sick need the warmest place, away from draughts and the door.

## Avoid

- Alcohol before sleep. It makes the body lose heat faster while the person feels warmer.
- Sleeping in a closed room with any fuel burning.
- Tight clothing that restricts blood flow to the hands and feet.

## Practice before you need it

Spend one night in the planned bed before the crisis, ideally in the cold season. Note which layer you were missing, how often you woke, and whether your hands and feet stayed warm. Write the answers on a card kept with the bedding, so the next setup is faster and better.

## Night checks

Once or twice during the night, check that the sleepers are still warm. Touch the hands, feet and neck, and look for shivering or confusion. If someone is cold, add a layer before they are fully awake, since waking chilled makes it harder to drift back to sleep warm.

## Morning routine

Before leaving the bed, put on dry clothes and move the arms and legs to restart circulation. Hang damp bedding in the sun, and store the sleeping layers somewhere dry. Eat something warm before the day begins, so the body starts with fuel.

## Common mistakes

- Using one thick blanket on a cold floor with no ground layer.
- Wearing damp clothing from the day, which keeps drawing heat out.
- Covering the face completely, which traps moisture and makes breathing harder.

## Checklist

- Ground layers are in place under the body.
- Clothing is dry, loose and includes a hat.
- Bedding is rated for the expected cold.
- The sleeping space is dry and has some air movement.
- Anyone vulnerable has the warmest spot.

## When it fails

If you wake up cold, get up and move for a few minutes, then add a layer under your body before adding more on top. If you cannot stop shivering or feel drowsy and confused, treat it as a sign of hypothermia and warm the person slowly with dry layers and warm drinks.

**Warning:** watch for signs of hypothermia during the night, especially in children and older people, and act at once if they appear.

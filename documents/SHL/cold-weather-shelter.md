# Cold Weather Shelter

Cold is dangerous because it is slow. Frostbite and hypothermia can develop before you notice them. Keep dry and keep moving before you stop, and build your shelter before the light fades, not after.

## Why it matters

In freezing air the body loses heat faster than it can make it, and the loss is worst when you are tired, wet or sitting still. Errors that seem small in daylight, such as a wet sleeve or a blocked entrance, become serious within hours. A shelter built early gives you time to correct mistakes, while a shelter built in the dark forces you to accept whatever you can dig fastest.

## Choose a site

- Pick a site sheltered from the wind, under trees if they are not dead, or against a rock.
- Avoid low hollows, where cold air sinks, and avoid slopes where snow can slide.
- Check the ground for hidden hazards such as stumps, ice and streams under the snow.

## Equipment and materials

- A shovel, or a flat board or pot lid if you have nothing else for digging.
- Something to probe the snow depth, such as a long stick or ski pole.
- Insulation for the floor: boughs, a foam mat, or spare clothing.
- A sealed bag for dry spare clothes, and a tarp to keep snow off the roof.

## Snow shelter

Snow is an excellent insulator. A snow trench or a snow cave, dug into a deep drift, keeps the temperature above freezing inside when the outside is well below. Make sure the entrance is always open and the roof is thick enough not to collapse.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Fresh powder** packs poorly, so build a thicker roof and test it by pressing a hand into it before sleeping beneath.
- **Crusted or icy snow** is hard to dig but holds its shape, so a smaller trench is usually enough.
- **Heavy wet snow** is dense and can collapse under its own weight, so keep the span narrow and the roof low.
- **Wind** scours the snow, so dig the entrance on the leeward side and shield it with a branch wall.

## Keep dry

- Wear wicking layers next to the skin, and change out of wet clothing.
- Keep a dry spare set in a sealed bag.
- Shake snow off clothing before entering the shelter.
- Dry gloves and socks against the body, not near a fire where they can scorch.

## Practice before you need it

Dig a practice trench in deep snow during daylight, so you know how long it takes and how much snow you need to move. Note how the roof behaves when you press on it, and practise the entrance so you can clear it from inside.

## Watch for

- **Frostbite:** white, waxy or numb skin on fingers, toes, nose or ears. Warm the area slowly, never with direct fire or hot water.
- **Hypothermia:** shivering that stops, slurred speech, clumsiness or confusion. Treat it as an emergency and get the person into a dry, warm space.

## Keep moving

Move your hands and feet regularly, and eat and drink enough to keep up the body's heat. Snow eaten without melting drains heat, so melt it first.

## Food and water

Eating keeps the body's furnace running. Keep high-energy food that needs no cooking, such as nuts, dried fruit, chocolate and hard cheese, in an inside pocket or sealed bag so it does not freeze solid. Drink regularly even when not thirsty, since cold air is dry and every breath costs moisture. Use a wide-mouthed flask or a bottle kept inside the sleeping bag, because plastic bottles can crack when water freezes in them.

## Common mistakes

- Sitting still for long periods with wet clothing on.
- Letting the entrance fill with drifted snow overnight.
- Sleeping directly on snow without a thick insulating layer.

## Checklist

- Entrance is open and clear.
- Roof is thick and has been checked for sagging.
- Clothing is dry, with a spare set ready.
- Hands and feet are checked every hour.

## When it fails

If the shelter starts to collapse, leave at once and dig out a new one nearby. If someone shows signs of hypothermia and cannot warm up, stop moving them around and seek help without delay.

**Warning:** never fall asleep in a snow shelter with a fire inside, and never leave the entrance blocked.

# Hot Weather Shelter

In heat, the aim is to avoid the sun and keep air moving. The body needs to lose heat, and a shelter can help it do that. Shade alone is not enough if the air around it is still and stale, so plan for both shade and airflow from the start.

![Side view: tarp-a-frame under a shade canopy with bracket on the air gap between cover and tarp, arrow-curved rising out through the gap; labels: canopy, air gap, tarp; a gap cools better than a single layer. NEEDS: sun, shade-canopy](assets/hot-weather-shelter-1.svg)

## Why it matters

In high temperatures the body cools itself mainly by sweating, and that only works when the sweat can evaporate. A shelter that traps still, humid air stops the cooling, so heat builds up even in shade. The risks grow with time: a person who has been hot for several hours can slide into heat exhaustion long before they feel seriously unwell. A shelter that is planned for airflow and water is one of the most effective defences available.

## Site

- Choose shade from trees, rock or a building, away from direct sun.
- Pick a spot with a breeze, and avoid hollows where hot air sits.
- Keep the shelter off dry, bare ground that reflects heat back up at the body.
- Check that the spot is clear of dead branches, which fall without warning in dry heat.

> @image Shelter under a broad tree canopy with people resting in deep shade (documentary photograph)

## Build for shade

1. Raise a tarp or cloth high above the shelter, with a gap for air to move underneath.
2. Use a double layer over the top, with a space between layers, to reduce heat.
3. Keep the sides open to the breeze.
4. Stake the cover firmly, since gusts can lift it in hot, dry wind.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In dry heat,** wet the ground outside the shelter in the evening, since the evaporation cools the air that drifts in.
- **In humid heat,** sweat cannot evaporate well, so rely on shade and rest, and cut the amount of work done.
- **With no wind,** lift the cover higher and open the sides, or use a fan powered by a battery or solar panel if one is available.
- **Near a body of water,** place the shelter where a breeze blows over the water toward the shade, and keep the people closest to the water supervised.

## Keep cool

- Wet cloths on the neck, wrists and forehead help cool the body as the water evaporates.
- Drink water regularly, even when not thirsty, and add salts if sweating heavily.
- Move to the shade in the hottest part of the day, and rest.
- Work early or late in the day, not at noon.

## Night

Hot nights are dangerous when the body cannot cool down. Sleep in the coolest part of the shelter, keep the sides open for air, and lie on a thin layer rather than thick bedding.

![Plan view: tent open on the sides, person-sitting at the sleeping position on a thin mat, arrow-straight crossing the sleeper; labels: open sides, thin mat, wet cloth; thin bedding and open sides let air carry heat away. NEEDS: sleeping-mat, cloth](assets/hot-weather-shelter-2.svg)

## Watch for

- **Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, pale skin, headache, nausea and weakness. Move the person into shade, loosen clothing and give small sips of water.
- **Heatstroke:** confusion, hot dry or red skin, or a person who stops sweating. This is life-threatening.

## Practice before you need it

Pitch the shade cover at home in the morning, when it is still cool, and time how long the job takes. Check that the ropes and pegs hold in a breeze, and that the cover does not sag when it rains. Store a set of wet cloths, a hat and a water container together, so they are ready before the heat peaks.

## Food and water

Eat light, cool meals, such as fruit, salads and yoghurt, and avoid heavy cooked food in the hottest hours, since digestion adds warmth. Store drinking water in the shade with its lid on, because water left in the sun quickly becomes warm and less appealing to drink. Make a daily plan for how much each person needs, and share it out by the clock rather than by thirst.

## Common mistakes

- Placing the shelter on bare ground, which holds and reflects heat.
- Sealing the sides, so hot air builds up inside.
- Waiting until thirst appears before drinking.

## Checklist

- The shelter is in shade for most of the day.
- Air can move under and through the cover.
- Water is stored in the shade and shared out regularly.
- Wet cloths and a hat are close at hand.
- Heavy work is planned for cooler hours.

## When it fails

If the shelter gets too hot inside, lift the cover higher, open the sides, and wet the ground outside to cool the air. If anyone shows signs of heatstroke, cool them at once with water and shade while help is called.

**Warning:** heatstroke is a medical emergency. Cool the person at once and call for help.

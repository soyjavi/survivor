# Tarp Shelters

A tarp is one of the most useful items you can carry. It makes a waterproof roof in minutes, and it can be shaped to block wind and rain. Practise setting one up at home before you need it, since tying knots in the dark and rain is much harder.

## Materials

- A tarp of at least 3 by 3 metres for a single person, larger for a group.
- Cord of at least 10 metres, plus short lengths for guy lines.
- Tent pegs, or stout sticks to use as pegs.
- A knife or scissors for cutting cord.

> @image Blue tarp pitched as an A-frame between two trees, pegged at the corners (outdoor product photograph)

## Basic A-frame

1. Tie a rope between two trees at about head height.
2. Drape the tarp over the rope, centred.
3. Pull the two corners down to the ground on each side, and peg them out.
4. Pull the edges out and peg them, keeping the shape tight.

## Lean-to tarp

1. Tie one edge of the tarp to a rope or a ridge line, high on one side.
2. Pull the other edge down and peg it out, at a slope.
3. Keep the open side facing away from the wind.

## Tension

- Tight tarps shed water and resist wind.
- Use taut-line hitches, which can be adjusted, so you can tighten them when they stretch.
- Check the tension after rain or wind.

## Drainage

Set the tarp at a slope so water runs off the lowest corner. Dig a small trench on the uphill side if the ground is soft, so runoff does not pool under the shelter.

## Why it matters

A tarp weighs little and packs small, so it can be carried on every trip and used for many jobs. Its usefulness depends on how it is pitched. A tarp tied loosely between two points will flap, collect water and tear, while a tight one with a sensible slope will keep the rain out for days.

## Choosing a ridge and anchor points

- Pick two anchor points at a height of about 2 metres, such as sturdy trunks or a fence post and a tree.
- Check that the anchors will not snap or move in wind. Dead wood breaks without warning.
- Leave enough space beneath the tarp for a person to sit up, and for air to move.

## Knots to know

- **Clove hitch:** quick to tie around a post or pole, and easy to adjust. It can slip under heavy, repeated loading, so add a half hitch.
- **Bowline:** forms a fixed loop that does not slip, which suits the end of a ridge line.
- **Taut-line hitch:** the adjustable knot for guy lines, which holds when tensioned and releases when slid.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In heavy rain,** lower the lowest corner further so water flows off, and open the sides a little for air.
- **In strong wind,** pitch the tarp low and close to the ground, with the open end facing away from the gusts.
- **In snow,** pitch steeper so snow slides off, and clear the top regularly.
- **In hot sun,** raise the tarp higher and leave the sides open, so heat does not build up underneath.

## Packing the tarp

Fold the tarp along its seams rather than across them, and roll the cord into a loose coil. Store the tarp dry, since damp fabric grows mould and smells. Practise setting the tarp up in daylight at least once a season, and tie each knot in the dark once, so you know how it feels when visibility is poor.

## Common mistakes

- Pitching the tarp too low, so the roof sags and holds water.
- Tying knots that cannot be untied when the tarp must come down.
- Leaving the open end facing the prevailing wind.

## Checklist

- Ridge rope is at head height and tight.
- Corners are pegged to the ground.
- Lowest corner has an exit for water.
- Open side faces away from the wind.
- Guy lines are checked after rain.

## When it fails

If the tarp flaps or sags, tighten the guy lines and add a peg at the sagging point. If the rope breaks in wind, lower the tarp at once and rebuild with a shorter, stronger ridge line, anchored to two sturdy trunks.

**Warning:** never run a fire under a tarp. The plastic can melt, and the smoke can build up dangerously.

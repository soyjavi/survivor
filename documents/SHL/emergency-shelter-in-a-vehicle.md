# Emergency Shelter in a Vehicle

A vehicle offers real protection from wind, rain and snow, and it keeps its occupants out of the weather while help is on the way. It also carries a serious hidden hazard. The engine, the exhaust and the fuel system all produce gases that can kill quietly, so using a car as shelter means managing air, heat and the exhaust with discipline.

## Choosing Whether to Shelter in the Vehicle

Decide before you stop whether the car is the right place to wait.

1. Stay with the vehicle if the road is blocked or the terrain is unsafe to walk.
2. Use the car if you are in a known location and help has been alerted.
3. Avoid sheltering in the car in a flood zone, beside a river, or where the vehicle could be swept, buried or struck.
4. Get out if the car is in a place where falling trees or snow drifts threaten it.

## Keeping the Air Moving

Air quality inside a closed car drops faster than people realise.

- Crack at least one window on the side away from the wind, enough to let air circulate without letting the cold in too fast.
- Keep the air vents set to draw fresh air from outside rather than recirculating cabin air.
- Clear any snow or leaves from the vents at the base of the windscreen, where the intake usually sits.
- Check the air every so often by noticing whether you feel drowsy, confused or short of breath.

## Engine and Heat Management

Heat comes from the engine, but the engine is also the main hazard.

1. Run the engine only in short periods, perhaps ten minutes at a time, then turn it off and let the cabin cool slightly.
2. Keep the fuel level above half so that you can run the heater for longer periods without refuelling.
3. Check the exhaust pipe before each run and clear it of snow and debris.
4. Keep the car's rear window and tail area clear so you can see whether the exhaust is blocked.
5. Turn off the engine as soon as you notice any headache, dizziness or nausea.

## Carbon Monoxide

Carbon monoxide has no smell, no colour and no taste, and it is produced by the exhaust of any running engine.

- Carbon monoxide can build up inside a car when the exhaust is blocked by snow or when the vehicle sits against a wall or drift.
- Symptoms include headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion and a feeling of heaviness. These may appear slowly, so people often miss them.
- If you suspect exposure, open the doors, get out into fresh air, and seek medical help.
- Keep at least one carbon monoxide alarm available if your vehicle or camp has a battery-powered unit.

## Staying Warm Without the Engine

When the engine is off, protect yourself from cold.

- Stay dressed in layers, including a hat and gloves.
- Use blankets or sleeping bags, and keep them dry.
- Huddle with others, since shared body heat helps.
- Keep the cabin small by closing unused compartments and seats.

## Electrical and Fuel Care

Do not drain the battery. Use the engine to charge it only briefly and keep the headlights off when possible. Do not run a generator or a portable stove inside or next to the car.

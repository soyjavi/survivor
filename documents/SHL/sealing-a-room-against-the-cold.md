# Sealing a Room Against the Cold

Most heat is lost through gaps, windows and the ceiling. A few simple steps can make a room much warmer for little fuel. Work on the largest gaps first, since a small change to a big draught often matters more than many small ones.

## Why it matters

Cold air moves into a room through every opening, and warm air escapes through the same openings as it rises. Even a small gap under a door can carry enough air to undo the warmth of a stove. Sealing a room costs little, uses materials most homes already hold, and lets a smaller fire keep a room comfortable, which saves fuel for the nights that need it most.

## Find the gaps

Hold a lit candle or a strip of tissue near doors, window frames, skirting boards and sockets. Where the flame flickers or the tissue moves, cold air is coming in. Mark each spot with chalk or tape so you can find it again when sealing.

> @image Hand holding a lit candle near a gap under a wooden door, flame bending in a draught (documentary photograph)

## Seal

1. **Doors:** place a rolled towel or blanket along the gap at the base.
2. **Windows:** cover them with plastic sheeting taped at the edges, or with thick curtains.
3. **Sockets and vents:** block with tape or cloth. Leave the flue or chimney opening clear.
4. **Gaps around frames:** stuff with fabric or paper, then tape.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Near a stove,** leave a small air gap at the base of the room door, so the fire does not pull air through the wrong openings.
- **On a damp wall,** avoid sealing it tightly against the room, since moisture trapped behind plastic will collect as mould.
- **For a window that opens,** tape the frame shut from the inside, but keep the handle reachable in case of an emergency.
- **In very windy weather,** secure the draught blockers with weights, since they can be dislodged by the air pressure at the door.

## Keep warmth in

- Close curtains at dusk.
- Hang blankets over doorways.
- Use a rug or cardboard on the floor.

## Keep air moving

Sealed rooms become damp and stuffy, and any fire inside will be starved of oxygen. Open a small gap in one window or vent when a fire is burning, and keep it open even in the coldest weather.

## Practice before you need it

Walk the room with the candle on a mild, windy day, so you learn where the draughts are before you need to seal them. Keep the blockers, tape and plastic sheeting together in one bag, and check that the tape still sticks. A rehearsal means the seal can be made in minutes, not hours, when the room has already cooled.

## Windows and doors in more detail

Measure each window before cutting plastic sheeting, leaving a margin for the tape on all sides. Tape the top edge first, then the sides, so the sheet pulls taut as you work down. Door blockers should be as long as the gap and lie flat on the floor, so nobody trips over them. Check the blockers each morning, since they shift as people come and go.

## Ceilings and floors

Warm air collects at ceiling height, so an unused loft hatch or a gap around a light fitting lets heat escape upward. Block loose hatches with a board and tape. Floors lose heat through gaps between boards, so place a rug or cardboard over bare boards and close any gap around pipes with cloth.

## Common mistakes

- Sealing every gap in the room, including the vents a fire needs.
- Using plastic sheeting on a window that is also used for escape.
- Forgetting the chimney, which must stay clear for smoke.

## Checklist

- Draughts are found with a candle or tissue.
- Largest gaps are sealed first.
- Sockets and vents are taped, but flues are open.
- Curtains are closed at dusk.
- One small air gap is kept open when a fire burns.

## When it fails

If the room is still cold after sealing, check that the draught blockers are on the correct side of the door and that the window seal is intact. If the room becomes damp, open the sealed windows for part of the day, then close them again before the evening.

**Warning:** do not block a chimney, flue or any vent that a fire needs for air.

# Shelter for People with Disabilities

A shelter that works for most people may not work for someone who uses a wheelchair, cannot see well, or needs regular care. Plan for their needs from the start, not after the first night, when problems are hardest to fix.

![Plan view: arrow-straight step-free route from door to bed, bedside items within reach, line-solid room edge; labels: step-free route, bedside items, bed; placement decides how independent a person can be. NEEDS: door, bed, wheelchair](assets/shelter-for-people-with-disabilities-1.svg)

## Plan the space

- Choose a room on the ground floor, or the lowest level with a clear route.
- Keep paths clear of debris, mats and cables, with a width that allows a wheelchair to turn.
- Place a bed or resting place within easy reach of the toilet and water.
- Keep essential items within reach from a seated or lying position.

> @image Wheelchair user resting in a ground floor room with a clear path to a bed and a water container (documentary photograph)

## Mobility aids

- Keep wheelchairs, crutches and frames close and charged or repaired.
- Carry a spare battery or repair kit for powered chairs.
- Ask about ramps or a second person to help with steps, and practise the route before it is needed.

## Medicines and equipment

- Keep prescribed medicines, dressings and equipment with the person, along with their list.
- Keep a supply of spare batteries for hearing aids, glucose monitors or breathing equipment.
- Store medicines that need cool conditions in a small insulated bag.

## Communication

- Agree a simple signal for help, using a whistle or bell.
- Use large printed notes or pictures for people who cannot read easily.
- Speak clearly and face the person when giving information.

## Support the carer

Ask neighbours or other household members to share caring tasks. Rest matters for the carer too, and a tired carer makes more mistakes with medicines and transfers.

![Plan view: arrow-straight route from door to bed, arrow-loop as the turning circle on the floor; labels: route, turning circle, bed; leave room to turn so the bed is not in a corner. NEEDS: wheelchair, bed, door](assets/shelter-for-people-with-disabilities-2.svg)

## Why it matters

Emergencies remove the supports people rely on, such as lifts, familiar routes, pharmacies and carers' visits. A plan made in advance restores some of that independence. It also protects against the common failure of a shelter that is accessible on paper but not in practice, because a single step or narrow door can cut a person off from toilets, water and help.

## Night arrangements

- Place a bed on a firm, level surface with space on both sides for transfers.
- Keep a light, a water container and a call signal within reach of the bed.
- Agree a plan for the toilet at night, with a clear path and a commode if one is needed.
- Check the person's position in bed at intervals if they cannot move themselves.
- Keep a torch within reach of the carer, and make sure the route to the toilet is lit at night.
- Check that the bed height matches the chair, so transfers stay level.

> @image Carer helping a person transfer from a wheelchair to a bed using a sliding board (documentary photograph)

## Evacuation routes

1. Identify two routes from the room, both step-free if possible.
2. Walk each route with the person, timing the journey and noting any obstacles.
3. Agree who will assist, and check that they can lift or move the chair safely.
4. Keep a small, lightweight evacuation chair or sheet near the exit if stairs cannot be avoided.

## Support equipment

Keep a copy of the person's medical history and emergency contacts, and make sure carers and family know the plan. Label every assistive device with the person's name, so it is not lost in a crowded hall. Store a small set of tools for adjusting a wheelchair, such as a hex key and a spare puncture repair kit.

## Common mistakes

- Placing the bed in a room reached only by stairs.
- Relying on a lift during a power cut.
- Leaving the person's medicine list in a different bag from the medicines.

## Checklist

- Room is on the ground floor or has a clear step-free route.
- Paths are clear and wide enough for mobility aids.
- Medicines, equipment and the medicine list travel together.
- Batteries are charged and spares are stored.
- A help signal has been agreed with the household.

## When it fails

If a route is blocked, find an alternative path before moving anyone, and warn others about the change. If a mobility aid breaks, use a spare or ask for a loan from the shelter, and keep the person seated and supported until a replacement is found.

**Warning:** never leave a person who depends on help alone in a dangerous situation. Plan an evacuation route that works for them.

# Building a Lean-To

A lean-to is a single-sided shelter with a sloped roof. It is quick to build, blocks wind, and works well with a fire in front of it. Because it needs only a few poles and a cover, it is one of the first shelters worth learning, whether you need a short stop on a walk or a longer stay after a disaster.

## Why it matters

A lean-to needs less time, less material and less skill than most other shelters, so it is the practical choice when light is fading and the group is tired. Its open front lets heat from a fire reach the sleeper, which a closed tent cannot do safely. It also teaches the principles behind almost every improvised shelter: a stable ridge, a roof that sheds water from top to bottom, a protected front and a small air gap at the back. Once you understand these, you can adapt the design to whatever poles and cover you find.

## Materials

- A strong ridge pole, about 2 to 3 metres long.
- Several smaller uprights of similar length.
- Branches, bark, grass or leaves for the roof.
- A tarp, plastic sheet or spare cloth for waterproofing.
- Cord or strips of cloth for lashing, if you have them.

## Site

- Pick level, dry ground, away from dead trees and drainage paths.
- Face the open side away from the prevailing wind and toward the fire.
- Look up before settling in, and move away from loose branches.

## Build

1. **Ridge pole.** Lay a strong pole between two supports, about waist height at the back, sloping down to the front.
2. **Uprights.** Lean smaller poles against the ridge pole, spaced about 30 cm apart.
3. **Roof.** Lay branches, bark or grass across the uprights from the bottom up, like shingles, so rain runs off.
4. **Cover.** Add a tarp or a layer of leaves over the roof for waterproofing.
5. **Bed.** Lay insulating material on the floor. See Keeping Warm While Sleeping.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In rain,** steepen the roof slightly so water runs off faster, and add a second cover layer at the lowest seam.
- **In snow,** keep the roof steep so snow slides off, build the ridge stronger, and clear snow from the roof regularly. Never let it pile up on the cover, since the weight can collapse a shallow roof.
- **In strong wind,** face the open side away from the gusts, peg the ridge pole to both supports, and add a lashing at every upright.
- **On soft ground,** place a flat log or board under each support so the posts do not sink, and keep the front edge slightly raised so runoff leaves the shelter.
- **In hot weather,** raise the roof a little higher and keep the front wide open, so air keeps moving through the shelter.

## Practice before you need it

Build the lean-to once in a garden, park or woodland while the weather is mild. Time yourself, note which poles were hard to find, and practise the lashing with the cord you plan to carry. A first build done at dusk, in wind, with cold hands is far harder than one practised in daylight. Record how many poles and how much roof material you used, so you know what to gather next time.

## Fire and reflection

Build a fire in front of the open side, about a metre away, so its heat reflects into the shelter. Use a reflector wall of logs or stones behind the fire to bounce more heat back. A reflector wall built too close to the fire will scorch the back of the shelter, so keep a sensible distance between them.

## Ventilation

Leave a gap at the top of the back wall, so smoke and moisture can escape. Without this gap, the shelter fills with smoke and condensation, which makes it cold, damp and unpleasant.

## Common mistakes

- Building the roof too flat, so rain pools on it and soaks through.
- Placing the fire too close, so sparks land on the cover.
- Skipping the bed, which leaves the body cold even under a good roof.

## Checklist

- Site is level, dry and clear of hazards.
- Roof sheds water from top to bottom.
- Fire is in front of the shelter, not under the roof.
- Back wall has an air gap at the top.
- Bed is insulated from the ground.

## When it fails

If the shelter leaks, add another layer of cover from the top down and check the seams. If it sags or collapses in wind, lower the pitch and add a second support at the back, then rebuild the roof before sleeping in it.

**Warning:** never build the fire under the roof. Keep it in front, clear of overhanging branches.

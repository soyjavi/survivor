# Ventilation in Shelters

Shelter needs fresh air. Stale air causes headaches, damp and mould, and in a building with a fire it can be deadly. Good ventilation and good insulation work together, so the goal is to keep warmth in while still letting fresh air in.

> @svg Cross section: tent centre with a low vent and a high vent, arrow-straight entering low, arrow-curved leaving high; labels: low vent, high vent; the two openings must sit at different heights to move air. NEEDS: vent

## Why it matters

- People breathing use up oxygen and release carbon dioxide and moisture.
- Cooking and fires release carbon monoxide, which has no smell or colour.
- Damp air leads to mould, and mould harms breathing.

> @image Child sleeping in a sealed room with condensation running down the window glass (documentary photograph of a poorly ventilated room)

## Simple ventilation

1. Open a window or vent slightly on the side away from the wind, every few hours.
2. Leave a gap at the top of a tent or lean-to, so warm air rises out.
3. Keep at least one opening clear at all times when a fire burns.
4. Clean vents and mesh so they do not clog with dust or insects.

## Signs of poor air

- Headache, drowsiness or heavy breathing.
- Condensation running down walls and windows.
- A smell of smoke or fuel.

## Act

Open the shelter, move outside into fresh air, and rest. Check the source of the problem. Stop any fire until the problem is solved, and do not go back in until the air is clear.

> @image Window kept open a few centimetres with a fire stove burning safely inside (documentary photograph of a ventilated room)

## Measuring the air

Air quality is hard to judge by smell alone, because carbon monoxide has no odour at all. Use a battery carbon monoxide alarm wherever a fire or stove runs, and place it at head height in the room where people sleep. Keep a spare battery with the alarm, and test it each morning when the fire is first lit. Do not rely on a single alarm, since one placed in the wrong room may miss the gas.

## Seasonal changes

- **In winter,** keep a small opening even when the room is cold, and check condensation on the glass each day.
- **In summer,** use the widest openings on the shaded side, so the air moves without bringing in sun or dust.
- **In wet weather,** keep the openings covered from the rain, using a tarp or board with a gap below it.

## Equipment to keep

- A carbon monoxide alarm with a spare battery.
- A small fan, powered by battery or solar where possible.
- Mesh or cloth to keep insects out of open vents.
- A pole or stick to open high vents safely.

## Ventilating a tent or lean-to

Open the bottom edge of the door or a side panel, and leave the top vent clear, so cool air enters low and warm air leaves high. Wipe condensation from the inside of the fabric each morning, since it drips back onto the bedding during the day.

## Ventilating a flat or house

Open windows on opposite sides of the room, if possible, so air flows across it. After a shower, keep the bathroom door shut and run any extractor fan, so damp air does not spread into the bedrooms.

## Practice before you need it

Check the vents and openings once a week in cold weather, and note how quickly condensation returns after you wipe it. Practise opening and closing the vents in the dark, so the routine is familiar when a fire is lit late in the day.

## Common mistakes

- Sealing the shelter fully to keep out cold, then lighting a fire inside.
- Blocking the top vent with a blanket or bag.
- Ignoring condensation, which is an early sign of poor airflow.

## Checklist

- At least one opening is kept clear when a fire burns.
- Vents and mesh are clean and unblocked.
- Air is refreshed every few hours.
- Condensation is wiped and the source is found.
- Anyone with headaches or drowsiness goes into fresh air.

## When it fails

If the shelter is still stuffy after opening it, add a second opening on the opposite side so air can cross through. If the smell of smoke or fuel persists, put out the fire, leave the shelter, and check the flue or stove before using it again.

**Warning:** a shelter that is sealed for warmth must still have an airflow path when a fire burns. Carbon monoxide has no smell, so treat any headache or drowsiness as a sign to get outside.

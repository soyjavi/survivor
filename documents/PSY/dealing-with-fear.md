# Dealing with Fear

Fear is a normal response to danger. It becomes harmful when it blocks clear thinking or stops people acting. In a crisis, fear narrows attention, speeds the heart and makes simple choices feel impossible. Learn to notice it early, and bring it back into proportion before it guides your decisions.

![Breathing steps: five step-box in a row joined by arrow-straight, the Out box widest; labels Sit, In through nose, Hold, Out through mouth, Repeat; the out-breath is the longest phase, which slows the heart.](assets/dealing-with-fear-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Fear is useful for a few seconds. It makes you jump away from a falling branch or pull a child back from the edge of a pit. But fear that lasts for hours changes how the brain works. Attention shrinks to the nearest threat, memory becomes patchy, and small tasks take much longer. Decisions made in that state are often rushed or repeated. Recognising this helps you slow down at exactly the moment when slowing down is hardest.

## Notice it

Fear shows as a racing heart, fast breathing, shaking, a tight chest, or a narrow focus on one worry. Many people do not realise they are afraid until they make a rushed decision. Name it, quietly and to yourself: "I am afraid. I can act anyway." Naming the feeling lowers its grip.

## Slow the breath

Slow breathing calms the body within a few minutes. Follow these steps:

1. Sit or stand with your back straight and your shoulders loose.
2. Breathe in slowly through the nose for four counts.
3. Hold for two counts.
4. Breathe out slowly through the mouth for six counts.
5. Repeat for two minutes, then check how you feel.

The out-breath is longer than the in-breath, which is what slows the heart. Practise when you are calm, so the method is familiar when you need it.

![Breath cycle: waves rising, line-solid flat hold, waves falling wider; labels In, Hold, Out; the exhale is drawn wider than the inhale.](assets/dealing-with-fear-2.svg)

## Focus on the next task

![Path: rock stepping stones along line-dashed, each with a step-box; labels Fill bottle, Check door, Next task; only the next stone is visible so only the next task needs thought.](assets/dealing-with-fear-3.svg)

Fear grows when people think about everything at once. Pick the next small job, such as filling a bottle or checking a door, and do it. Completing one task gives the mind a clear goal and a small sense of control. Repeat the cycle: next task, done, next task.

## Talk to people

> @image Photograph of two people sitting together and talking calmly on a low wall, documentary style, black and white

Speaking calmly with others reduces fear. Ask people how they are, and share what you know. A group that talks is less likely to panic than one that keeps its worries private.

## Limit exposure

![Schedule: two timeline-marker on timeline-axis for morning and evening, rest of the axis left empty; labels Check news, Free; news is checked at fixed points and the day is otherwise free.](assets/dealing-with-fear-4.svg)

Too much news or rumour keeps fear high. Check information at set times, such as once in the morning and once in the evening, then stop. Repeated alarming updates keep the body in alarm mode.

> @image Photograph of a radio and a notebook on a table in a camp shelter, documentary style, black and white

## Adjusting for conditions

At night, fear is strongest and judgement weakest. Keep decisions for the morning unless someone is in immediate danger. In a crowd, speak quietly and keep your breathing slow, since others copy what they see. When you are alone, say the next task aloud, which gives the mind something concrete to hold. When the group is frightened by a specific threat, name the threat, state what is being done, and say when the next update will come.

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Stop moving** for a moment, and put down whatever you are carrying.
2. **Name the feeling** in one short sentence.
3. **Breathe slowly** for two minutes, using the pattern above.
4. **Ask one question:** what is the next small safe step?
5. **Act on that step only,** then return to the breathing if the fear returns.

## Common mistakes

- Making major decisions while the heart is racing.
- Checking every rumour immediately, which keeps the mind on alert.
- Keeping fear private, so that it grows unseen.
- Trying to stop fear entirely, rather than making room for it while you act.

## Troubleshooting

If breathing exercises make you dizzy, stop, sit down and breathe normally with your eyes open. If the fear returns in waves, return to the next task and repeat the cycle rather than fighting the feeling. If a child copies your fear, sit beside them, breathe slowly and invite them to count with you.

## Ask for help

If fear stops you sleeping, eating or working for more than a few days, tell someone and seek support. Fear that lasts this long is not a personal failure, and it responds well to rest, routine and conversation.

![Ladder: ladder with three step-box, arrow-straight upward with decision at each; labels Self-care, Talk to someone, Professional support; move up only when the step below has not helped.](assets/dealing-with-fear-5.svg)

## Practice before you need it

Practise the breathing pattern twice a day for a week, even when you feel fine. Choose your three next-task cards in advance, and agree with your household what each person will do first if an alarm sounds. Familiar steps are much easier to follow when the heart is racing.

## Checklist

- [ ] I have named the fear to myself.
- [ ] I have done two minutes of slow breathing.
- [ ] I have chosen one small next task.
- [ ] I have limited my news checks to set times.

**Warning:** fear can make people act without thinking. Pause for a breath before any major decision, especially decisions about fire, water sources or leaving shelter.

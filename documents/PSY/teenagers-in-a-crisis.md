# Teenagers in a Crisis

Teenagers want independence and responsibility, and they feel fear and loss strongly. They may hide their feelings, test rules or withdraw into themselves. Involve them in the plan, give them real tasks, and keep the rules clear. Treated as capable, most teenagers rise to the challenge.

![Roles chart: table-header with role names over table-grid, a step-box rule beside each role; labels Role, Rule; every role comes with a stated rule so responsibility arrives with a limit.](assets/teenagers-in-a-crisis-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Adolescence is a period of building identity, and a crisis can interrupt that work abruptly. Teenagers lose school, friends, sports and the simple freedom to plan their own days. Many feel responsible for problems they cannot solve, and some will take risks to prove they are grown up. Giving them a meaningful place in the group meets a real developmental need, and it turns a worried observer into a contributor. A teenager who is trusted is also more likely to ask for help when something goes wrong.

## Give them a role

> @image Photograph of a teenager leading a water collection line with a notebook, documentary style, black and white

Real responsibility builds self-respect and reduces risky boredom.

- Let them lead a task, such as water collection, the radio log, or caring for younger children.
- Ask for their ideas, and use them where they are good. A teenager who suggests a new route for water collection may see a problem the adults missed.

## Keep rules clear

![Board: table-header Rule and Reason, table-grid rows with step-box pairs joined by line-solid; each rule is paired with its reason so it can be discussed openly.](assets/teenagers-in-a-crisis-2.svg)

Clear rules protect teenagers more than strict ones.

- Agree the rules together where possible, and explain why they exist.
- Be consistent. Teenagers respond to fairness, and they notice when adults change the rules to suit themselves.
- Write the main rules on the shared board, so that no one has to argue from memory.

## Keep them connected

![Weekly grid: table-header with days, table-grid with message windows as legend-dot cells; labels Friends window; contact is scheduled in set blocks, neither banned nor unlimited.](assets/teenagers-in-a-crisis-3.svg)

Isolation worsens distress in young people.

- Let them keep in touch with friends, when the networks allow, and agree how and when.
- Give them time alone, within safe limits, such as a quiet corner or a walk within sight of the camp.

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Invite them to the planning meeting,** and ask what they think the group most needs.
2. **Offer two or three roles,** and let them choose one.
3. **Agree the rules for that role** in a short conversation, with the reason for each.
4. **Write the role on the board** with their name and a back-up.
5. **Check in each evening,** asking how the task went and what they would change.

## Watch for risk

![Icons: waypoint-pin, two person-standing, timeline-marker in a row; labels Where, With whom, When back; the three check-in questions are asked at every departure.](assets/teenagers-in-a-crisis-4.svg)

Watch for risky behaviour, such as unnecessary trips, drinking or drug use. Know where they are, and who they are with. Ask openly, without accusation: "Where are you going, and when will you be back?"

## Talk honestly

Explain what is happening, and admit what you do not know. Teenagers can handle the truth better than they expect, and they often pick up adult secrets anyway. Honesty builds the trust you will need when the situation is hardest.

![Two-way board: table-grid with a note left and a note right joined by arrow-both-ways; labels Adult to teen, Teen to adult; the board is a shared channel, not one-way instruction.](assets/teenagers-in-a-crisis-5.svg)

## Adjusting for conditions

When the group is moving, give teenagers the job of scouting the route ahead and reporting back, which uses their energy well. When supplies are short, involve them in the stock count, which teaches them the stakes without frightening them. When a teenager is caring for younger children, agree a maximum shift length and a back-up adult. When the crisis drags on, rotate their tasks every week, so that the work stays interesting and they do not feel stuck.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a teenager as a child in front of others, which provokes resistance.
- Using punishment for a mistake before finding out why it happened.
- Leaving them with no task at all, which encourages restless behaviour.
- Asking them to keep adult secrets about danger or shortage.

## Troubleshooting

If a teenager refuses a role, ask what they would prefer, and offer a smaller version of the task. If they break a rule, ask them to explain the reason in their own words before deciding on a consequence. If two teenagers become rivals, separate their tasks and give each a clear, equal share of the work.

## What to do when they pull away

Withdrawal is common. Keep a calm, steady presence. Offer a small, specific task or a shared activity, and avoid long lectures. Let them come back in their own time, while keeping the basic safety rules in place.

## Practice before you need it

Talk with each teenager about the group's roles and rules on an ordinary day, before the crisis. Agree in advance how they will report to an adult, and where they will go if they feel unsafe. Practise the check-in question so that it sounds natural rather than suspicious.

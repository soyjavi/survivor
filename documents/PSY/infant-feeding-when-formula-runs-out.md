# Infant Feeding When Formula Runs Out

When formula becomes scarce, the most protective step is to keep the baby at the breast or restore milk supply. Breast milk is the safest food available for an infant in almost every situation, and it needs no water to prepare. Everything below protects that option first, then sets out safe choices if it is not enough.

## Protect and support breastfeeding

1. Put the baby to the breast often, at least eight to twelve times in twenty-four hours, including at night.
2. Let the baby feed from one breast until it softens or the baby lets go, then offer the other side.
3. Keep the mother warm, rested, and fed. She needs extra food and fluids, especially when stress is high.
4. If mother and baby are apart, express milk by hand or pump every two to three hours and store it safely.
5. Avoid bottles and pacifiers in the early weeks where possible, because they can make latching harder.

Breastfeeding that has become difficult can often be restarted. Skin-to-skin contact, calm positioning, and gentle breast massage before feeding all help. Some mothers can rebuild supply over days, but this takes patience and usually guidance from a health worker.

## Choosing safe water

Formula must never be mixed with water that could carry germs. Contaminated water is one of the most common causes of severe illness in babies, and it is often invisible.

1. Boil water for at least one full minute, longer at high altitude, and let it cool until it is lukewarm.
2. If boiling is not possible, use water treated with chlorine or a filter rated for bacteria, and follow the product instructions exactly.
3. Store prepared water in a clean, covered container and use it within a day.
4. Wash hands with clean water and soap before preparing any feed.

- Never add extra powder to make a tin last longer.
- Never dilute formula with more water than the label says.
- Use the scoop supplied with the tin, filled level rather than heaped.
- Discard any feed the baby did not finish within one hour of starting it.

**Warning:** Watered-down formula does not provide enough nutrition and can cause dangerous sodium imbalance and severe malnutrition. Never stretch formula.

## Recognising hunger and dehydration

A baby who is getting enough milk usually has at least six wet nappies a day after the first week, regular stools, and steady weight gain. Watch these numbers closely when supplies are unreliable.

Signs of hunger include rooting, sucking on hands, waking often, and crying that starts softly and builds. Calming the baby before the feed helps it go better.

Signs of dehydration include:

- Fewer than four wet nappies in twenty-four hours.
- Dark, strong-smelling urine.
- A dry mouth, cracked lips, or few or no tears when crying.
- A sunken soft spot on the top of the head.
- Unusual sleepiness, floppiness, or difficulty waking.
- Hard, dry stools, or no stools for an unusually long time.

Offer breast milk or prepared feeds in small amounts often. If the baby takes little at once, offer small sips from a cup or spoon. Do not give plain water in large amounts to infants under six months, because it can dangerously lower blood sodium.

## When to get help

1. Seek care quickly if a baby under three months refuses feeds for more than a few hours.
2. Seek care urgently if there are no wet nappies for eight hours, or if the baby has vomiting, diarrhoea, or fever.
3. Go immediately if the baby is limp, breathing fast or with effort, has a bluish tinge to the lips, or cannot be woken properly.
4. Bring any remaining formula tins, the scoop, and a note of how much the baby takes each day. This helps the health worker decide what to do.
5. Ask the nearest clinic or aid post for advice on weighing the baby. Weight gain can often be checked without special equipment.

Accept donated infant formula only when it is sealed, in date, and suitable for the baby's age, and ask a health worker to confirm it before use. Breast milk substitutes handed out without guidance can be prepared wrongly.

**Warning:** Never give cow's milk, goat's milk, plant milks, honey, sugar water, or tea to a baby under six months. These can cause severe illness or dangerous chemical imbalances.

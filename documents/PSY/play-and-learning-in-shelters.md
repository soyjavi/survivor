# Play and Learning in Shelters

Children in shelters lose routine, school, friends, and privacy. Regular play and simple learning restore a sense of normal life, lower stress, and give tired caregivers a little breathing room. Activities do not need materials or much space. They need consistency, a few clear rules, and an adult who joins in.

## Why structure helps

Shelters are noisy and unpredictable. Children feel safer when they know roughly what comes next. A simple daily pattern, repeated each day, is one of the most calming things a caregiver can provide.

1. Set a rough schedule with fixed times for waking, meals, quiet play, learning, active play, and rest.
2. Post the schedule where children can see it, drawn as pictures for younger children.
3. Give each child a small job, such as carrying water or sorting shared items.
4. End each day with the same calm routine, such as a story or a song.

## Games for small spaces

Many games need only voices, hands, and a patch of floor. Choose games that let children move without knocking into others or breaking things.

- **Statues:** Play music or clap, and children freeze when the sound stops. Anyone who moves sits out.
- **I spy:** Choose an object in the room and let children guess its colour or first letter.
- **Animal walk:** Children crawl, hop, or waddle like animals, and others guess which one.
- **Simon says:** Give simple instructions, and only follow those that begin with the words Simon says.
- **Counting hands:** Children clap a rhythm and count together, speeding up and slowing down.
- **Story chain:** Each child adds one sentence to a story, which keeps everyone listening.

Rotate games so that no single activity dominates. Keep rules short and explain them once, clearly, before starting.

## Reading and language

Reading is one of the most valuable activities a child can do during a long stay. Books are rare, so use what exists and invent the rest.

1. Read aloud every day, even for ten minutes, and let children turn the pages.
2. Ask simple questions about the story: who, where, what happened, and what might happen next.
3. Tell stories from memory, including family histories and local tales.
4. Write words in the dust, on cardboard, or on the ground, and let children copy them.
5. Make a word wall from pictures and labels, and add one new word each day.

If there are older children who read well, ask them to read to younger ones. This builds confidence in both groups.

## Simple lessons

Teaching does not need a classroom. Short, repeated lessons work better than long sessions, because young attention spans are limited. Keep each lesson to about twenty minutes.

1. Begin with a question that connects to the child's own life, such as how many people live in the room.
2. Teach one idea at a time, using objects to show it, such as stones for counting.
3. Let children practise in pairs or small groups, moving between them to help.
4. Finish with a quick recap, asking each child to say one thing they learned.

Useful topics include counting and simple arithmetic, basic geography of the region, hygiene and health, and practical skills like washing hands or folding clothes. Ask adults with skills to volunteer a session.

## Calm and creative activities

Quiet activities help children settle after busy play and give them a chance to express feelings they may not have words for.

- Drawing with sticks in sand, or with pieces of charcoal on any clean surface.
- Making simple toys from cloth, paper, or bottle tops.
- Breathing games, where children breathe in slowly for four counts and out for four.
- Listening games, identifying sounds outside and naming them.
- Quiet storytelling, sitting in a circle with the lights dimmed.

Keep craft materials in a labelled box, and collect them carefully so they are not lost or swallowed.

## Keeping play safe

- Watch younger children closely during active games, especially near doors and stairs.
- Remove sharp objects, broken items, and anything small enough to swallow from play areas.
- Make sure every child can see and hear the adult leading the activity.
- Include children with disabilities, adjusting rules as needed.
- Respect children who do not want to play; offer a quiet role instead of forcing participation.

## Looking after the adults too

Caregivers carry much of the emotional weight in a shelter. Ask other adults to share the work of supervising play, so that no one person is always on duty. A short break for a caregiver is good for the children as well.

**Warning:** Never leave young children unsupervised with small objects, cords, or medicines while playing. A single moment of distraction can lead to choking or poisoning.

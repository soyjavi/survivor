# Boredom and Idle Time

Long periods with nothing to do increase tension, arguments and risky behaviour. Boredom is not a small problem in a crisis. It wears down patience, it encourages people to wander into danger, and it can quietly turn into low mood. Plan activities, especially for children and young people, before the empty hours arrive.

![Grid diagram: table-header with seven day labels, table-grid below with rows games, stories, skills and shared activity, arrow-curved beside the non-shared rows; labels Games, Stories, Skills, Shared; non-shared rows rotate each week so no game goes stale.](assets/boredom-and-idle-time-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Empty hours are dangerous hours. A bored child will explore a storage shed, climb a broken wall or follow older children to the edge of the camp. A bored adult may start arguments, drink more than usual or sit without eating. Activity does not solve the crisis, but it gives the mind a task, a rhythm and a reason to stay near the group. People who have something planned for the day are easier to keep safe.

## Simple activities

> @image Photograph of children playing a card game on a blanket indoors, documentary style, black and white

Choose activities that need few materials and no power. Good options include:

- **Games:** card games, board games made from paper and stones, and guessing games such as twenty questions.
- **Stories:** storytelling, reading aloud and writing letters or diaries.
- **Skills:** drawing maps, learning knots or practising first aid.
- **Work that feels like play:** gardening, cooking together and sorting supplies.

Keep a short list of these ideas where everyone can see it.

## Plan the week

![Board diagram: table-header with seven day labels, table-grid of activity rows, bracket across the shared row; labels Shared activity, Rotating rows; the same group activity repeats daily while other rows change.](assets/boredom-and-idle-time-2.svg)

Make a simple list of activities, and rotate them so that no single game becomes stale. Put a rough plan on a board:

1. Choose three or four activities for the week.
2. Assign each one to a set time, such as after the midday meal.
3. Include one activity that everyone does together each day.
4. Review the plan at the weekend, and change what did not work.

Shared activities build cohesion, and they give quieter people a reason to join in.

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Count the hours.** Mark the times when people are usually idle, such as the late morning and the hour before dark.
2. **Match activities to the gaps.** Quiet games for the heat of the afternoon, active games for early morning.
3. **Name a leader for each activity.** A teenager can run a story circle, and an older adult can teach a game.
4. **Start small.** Begin with one activity for one hour, then add more if people want them.
5. **Stop before people tire.** End while the group still wants more, and the next session will be easier to start.

## Use the space

![Floor plan: waypoint-pin for the quiet corner, bag for the pencil box, threshold for the doorway, line-dashed foot-traffic path with direction-of-travel; labels Quiet corner, Doorway, Foot traffic; the corner sits off the main route. NEEDS: blanket, bookshelf](assets/boredom-and-idle-time-3.svg)

Make a corner for quiet work, reading or drawing, away from noise and foot traffic. A blanket on the floor, a box for pencils and a shelf for books are enough. Children need a space they can return to, and adults need one too.

## Keep it safe

![Zone map: line-dashed boundary enclosing person-sitting as the play area; outside it flame, jerrycan, knife, and hut for kitchen and storage; labels Fire, Fuel, Sharp tools, Storage; play sits at a distance, not just supervised.](assets/boredom-and-idle-time-4.svg)

Keep games and activities away from fire, fuel and sharp tools. A child absorbed in a game can wander into a kitchen or a storage area without noticing. Supervise children closely, and set clear boundaries about where they may play.

## Adjusting for conditions

> @image Photograph of older adults and teenagers playing a board game together under a tree, documentary style, black and white

In heat, move activities to the shade and to the early morning or evening, and keep games short. In rain, use the most sheltered room and choose quiet games that need no space. When the group is small, pair older and younger people so that each has company. When the group is large, split into smaller circles, each with its own leader, so that everyone can speak.

## Common mistakes

- Filling every hour, which leaves no time to rest.
- Planning only for children and forgetting older adults.
- Allowing screens or noisy games to replace conversation.
- Letting one game dominate the week until everyone is tired of it.

## Troubleshooting

If nobody joins in, start the activity with two or three willing people and let others watch. If a game becomes an argument, pause it and agree a fair rule before resuming. If children drift away, ask them to teach the game to someone younger, which gives them a role and brings them back.

## Watch for

Persistent boredom can become low mood. If someone seems withdrawn for more than a few days, check in with them, and ask what they would enjoy doing. See Coping with Grief for signs of prolonged distress.

## Practice before you need it

Run one game each week on a calm day, even before the crisis starts. Notice which activities people return to and which they drop. Keep the box of materials packed, and agree who leads each activity, so that the first hard day does not begin with a search for ideas.

## Checklist

> @image Photograph of an adult teaching a young girl to tie a knot with a rope, documentary style, black and white

- [ ] Activities need few materials and no fuel.
- [ ] Each day includes one shared activity.
- [ ] Children are within sight during play.
- [ ] Someone checks on the quietest person each day.

**Warning:** do not fill time with activities that drain water, fuel or energy without need. A game that burns a candle or a fire for hours can cost more than it gives back.

# Asking for Help

Many people wait too long to ask for help, because they feel ashamed or do not want to burden others. In a crisis, asking early is a strength, and it helps the whole group. Help is limited, and the sooner the right person knows about a problem, the sooner it can be solved. A household that stays silent often ends up in a worse position than one that speaks up on the first day.

![Request card: table-header over four step-box stacked; labels Problem, Help needed and urgency, What I can offer, Name; the offer is part of the request, not an afterthought.](assets/asking-for-help-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Silence hides problems from the people who could solve them. A neighbour cannot bring a spare blanket to a family that never mentioned the cold. A health worker cannot prioritise a child with a fever if nobody reports it. Asking early also keeps small problems small. A cut that is cleaned today needs no help tomorrow. A request made on the first day is usually simple. The same request made on the fifth day may have grown into an emergency.

## Know what you need

Before you ask, sit down for two minutes and write the request on paper. Keep it short. Include:

- **What the problem is,** in one sentence.
- **What help you need,** and how urgent it is: now, today, or this week.
- **What you can offer in return,** such as labour, spare tools, cooking or childcare.

Writing the request first stops you from asking vaguely, and it makes you think about whether the problem is truly urgent.

## Ask clearly

> @image Photograph of a real handwritten help request card passed from one household to a neighbour, documentary style, black and white

Be specific. Compare two requests. "We need help" gives the listener nothing to act on. "I need two litres of clean water for my son, who has diarrhoea, before the evening" gives them a task they can start immediately. Clear requests get faster answers, and they are easier to pass on to someone else if you cannot be reached.

Repeat the answer back to confirm the time, place and quantity.

## Step-by-step in detail

![Vertical flow: six step-box joined by arrow-straight downward, arrow-loop from the last back to the first; labels Pause, Choose person, Say request, Repeat back, Record, Follow up; follow-up returns to asking if nothing arrives.](assets/asking-for-help-2.svg)

1. **Pause.** Take two minutes and write the request as described above.
2. **Choose the person.** Pick the nearest person who can act on the request, not the person you like most.
3. **Say the request in one breath.** Give the problem, the help needed and the time it is needed by.
4. **Ask them to repeat it.** A short confirmation prevents a wrong quantity or a wrong place.
5. **Record the answer.** Write down the name, the time and what was promised.
6. **Follow up.** If nothing arrives at the agreed time, ask again, and note the delay.

## Ask the right person

![Ladder of sources: ladder with four step-box on its rungs, arrow-straight down the side; labels Family and neighbours, Clinic or health post, Police, Local authority; move down only when the level above fails.](assets/asking-for-help-3.svg)

Different problems have different sources of help. Work down this list:

1. Start with family and neighbours. They are the fastest source, and they know your situation.
2. For medical needs, contact the nearest clinic or health post.
3. For safety, contact the police or emergency services.
4. For shelter and food, ask the local authority or the registration point.

If the first source cannot help, do not stop. Ask the next one, and note who you have already asked so that you do not waste their time.

## Adjusting for conditions

> @image Photograph of a child running a message between two houses along a dirt path, documentary style, black and white

When roads are blocked or phones do not work, a runner or a written note carried by a child may be the only channel. Walk the request to the nearest household, and ask them to pass it on. When many households ask at once, rank the requests by danger to life, then by urgency, and say so openly.

## Accept help

![Exchange loop: person-standing left, bucket, arrow-curved, person-standing right, hammer, arrow-loop back to the left; labels Water, Tools, Thanks; help is a loop that returns.](assets/asking-for-help-4.svg)

Accepting help is a skill. Some people find it harder than asking.

- Say thank you, and mean it.
- Keep your promises, and return help when you can.
- Do not expect the helper to solve everything. Accept the part that is offered.

## Common mistakes

- Asking for a large favour with no time limit, so the helper cannot plan.
- Asking the same person for everything, which exhausts them.
- Refusing a small offer because it is not the full solution.

## Troubleshooting

If nobody responds, check whether the request reached the person at all. Ask a second person to carry it. If the helper says they cannot help, thank them, and ask whether they know who can. If the help arrives late, do not treat the delay as a refusal. Write down what happened and ask again with the new time.

## Help others too

Asking for help is part of a community that works. The people who ask today may be the ones who give tomorrow. Offer help when you are able, and check on those who do not ask, because the quiet households are often the ones in greatest need.

## Checklist

![Checklist card: table-header over table-grid with four rows, each with do tick, note beside; labels Problem, Urgency, Source contacted, Thanks given, Request note; four checks complete a request.](assets/asking-for-help-5.svg)

- [ ] I have written down the problem and the urgency.
- [ ] I have asked the most local source first.
- [ ] I have noted who I have already contacted.
- [ ] I have thanked the helper and kept my promise.

**Warning:** if you cannot get help, do not wait. Move to the next source, and keep asking until someone responds. Silence in a crisis is a risk, not a sign that help is coming.

# Caring for the Carers

Those who care for children, older people or the sick often carry the heaviest load and give the least rest. They are awake at night, they worry constantly, and they put others first at every meal. Burnout harms them and the people they care for, because an exhausted carer makes slower decisions and less careful mistakes.

![Rota grid: table-header with carer names, table-grid rows Morning, Afternoon, Night, a legend-dot cell beside each shift for the back-up; labels Carer, Back-up; no shift lacks a second name.](assets/caring-for-the-carers-1.svg)

## Why it matters

A carer is a single point of failure. When one adult does all the nursing, cooking and night watch, a fever, a fall or a fit of despair can leave a child or an older person with no care at all. Protecting the carer is therefore a plan for the patient too. Rest, food and a second pair of hands are not extras. They keep the care going for as long as it is needed.

## Signs of burnout

Burnout builds slowly, so check yourself each day. Watch for:

- Constant tiredness, even after sleep.
- Irritability, impatience or feeling numb.
- Neglecting your own food, water, hygiene or medicines.
- Dreading the next task, or feeling that nothing you do matters.

If two or more of these last longer than a few days, treat it as a warning.

## Share the load

![Relay diagram: person-standing left, arrow-straight labelled Handover, person-standing right, person-sitting beside as back-up, timeline-marker at the exchange; labels Carer A, Carer B, Back-up, Handover time; handover is a timed exchange. NEEDS: baton](assets/caring-for-the-carers-2.svg)

Care is safer and lighter when several adults share it.

- Agree shifts with other adults, so each person has time off. Write the rota on a board.
- Name a back-up carer for each person who needs care, so that a single illness does not leave one person in charge.
- Ask for help without apology. A neighbour may be glad to sit with a child for an hour.

## Protect your own needs

![Cycle diagram: bowl, pills, person-sitting, phone-call in a ring, arrow-loop returning to the first; labels Food, Medicine, Short break, Outside contact; self-care is a daily cycle, not a one-off.](assets/caring-for-the-carers-3.svg)

Carers often do this last. Put it first:

1. Eat and drink regularly, even when you are not hungry.
2. Keep your own medicines, check their dates, and refill them early.
3. Take short breaks each day, even ten minutes alone, away from the person you care for.
4. Keep some contact with people outside the household, when possible.

Small, repeated breaks do more than one long rest that never arrives.

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Write the care plan.** List every task the person needs, from washing to medicine, with the time each is due.
2. **Sort the tasks.** Mark which tasks only you can do, and which can be shared with a neighbour, a child or a back-up carer.
3. **Fill the rota.** Give each back-up carer a named slot, including one full night off each week.
4. **Hand over clearly.** At each change of shift, tell the next carer what has been done, what is due and what has changed.
5. **Check yourself.** Before taking over again, ask whether you have eaten, slept and taken your own medicines.

![Handover card: table-header over three step-box stacked, caution beside the third; labels Patient needs, Due times, Recent changes; the card lists what changed, not only the routine.](assets/caring-for-the-carers-4.svg)

## Set limits

> @image Photograph of a carer resting on a bench in a shaded yard, documentary style, black and white

You cannot give what you do not have. Be realistic about what you can do in a day. Choose the two or three tasks that matter most, and tell others which ones you cannot take on. Saying "I cannot do that today" protects the care you are able to give.

## Adjusting for conditions

When the patient's condition changes quickly, shorten the shifts and bring in a back-up earlier. When the carer is ill, stop caring for others until recovered, and ask a neighbour to take over for the day.

## When your own care slips

Some practical signs that you need a change:

- You have missed more than one meal or dose in a row.
- You snap at the people you love over small things.
- You have stopped sleeping for more than two nights.

When these happen, call a back-up carer at once, and rest for a full shift.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a night off as a failure rather than a planned part of the week.
- Doing every task yourself because it is faster than explaining it to someone else.
- Waiting until you collapse before asking for help.

## Troubleshooting

If the back-up carer does not turn up, call the next name on the list and do not wait. If the patient refuses care from anyone but you, let a back-up sit with them while you rest in the same space, so that the patient gets used to the change. If you feel guilty about resting, remind yourself that the rota is the plan, and that the plan needs you well.

## Get support

Tell a trusted person when you are struggling. Be specific about what you need, such as one night off or help with washing. If you feel hopeless or cannot cope, seek help from a health worker or a counsellor. See Sleep and Stress for rest strategies that also apply to carers.

## Checklist

![Checklist: table-grid with four rows each with do tick, person-sitting beside; labels Food, Medicine, Break, Talking; self-care is checked like any other task.](assets/caring-for-the-carers-5.svg)

- [ ] I have a named back-up carer.
- [ ] I have eaten, drunk and taken my medicines today.
- [ ] I have had at least one break away from care.
- [ ] I have told someone how I am really doing.

**Warning:** a carer who is exhausted makes mistakes. Rest is part of the care plan, not a reward for finishing the work.

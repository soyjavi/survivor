# Making Rationing Decisions as a Group

Scarcity divides groups quickly. Decisions made openly, with clear reasons, are accepted far more readily than those made in secret. People can tolerate a small share when they trust how it was decided. They cannot tolerate suspicion, and suspicion spreads faster than hunger.

![Cycle: five step-box in a row joined by arrow-straight, arrow-loop from the last back to the first; labels Facts, Principles, Decide, Record, Review; the cycle repeats whenever stock changes.](assets/making-rationing-decisions-as-a-group-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Rationing is not only a question of arithmetic. It decides who eats first, who waits and who feels cared for. If the process looks unfair, people stop trusting the group, hide what they have, and stop cooperating on other tasks such as water collection and repairs. A transparent process protects the stock and the community together. It also protects the people who make the decisions, because their choices can be checked and defended.

## Put the facts on the table

![Ledger: table-header over table-grid; labels Item, Count, Daily use, Days remaining, Initials; days remaining drives the decision and two initials confirm each count.](assets/making-rationing-decisions-as-a-group-2.svg)

Start with numbers, not opinions.

- Count the stock and write it down where everyone can see it.
- Explain how many days the supply will last at the planned rate, and what happens if the rate changes.
- Say what you do not yet know, and when you will update the group.

## Agree the principles

> @image Photograph of a handwritten principles board in a community meeting room, documentary style, black and white

Decide the rules together before they are needed. It is far harder to agree fairness during a shortage, when everyone is afraid. Common principles include:

- Children, pregnant people and the sick come first for food and water.
- Everyone gets the same share unless there is a medical reason.
- Those doing the hardest work get a little more, and this is stated openly.

Write the principles on a board, and read them at each meeting.

## Decide and record

1. Make the decision in a short, calm meeting, with the facts already shared.
2. Write it down, with the reason, the start date and the review date.
3. Review it every few days, or sooner if the stock changes.

## Step-by-step in detail

![Steps: step-1, step-2, step-3 and two more step-box joined by arrow-straight, bowl as portion scale beside the last; labels Count stock, Principles, Decide, Portion, Post; the portion is set last.](assets/making-rationing-decisions-as-a-group-3.svg)

1. **Count the stock** with two people, and record the total in the ledger.
2. **Calculate the days left** at the current rate, and at a reduced rate.
3. **Read the principles** aloud, and ask whether anyone wants to change them.
4. **Propose one portion size** with the reason, and allow questions.
5. **Confirm and post** the decision, the start date and the next review.

## Protect fairness

![Checking: barrel as locked store, person-standing left and right each with do tick; labels Store, Counter 1, Counter 2; two independent people check the same count so none holds it alone.](assets/making-rationing-decisions-as-a-group-4.svg)

- Keep the stock in one place, and have two people count it, so that no single person controls the figures.
- Rotate who serves food, so no one holds the power alone.
- Hear complaints in private, and answer them openly, in front of the group, once the answer is clear.

## Adjusting for conditions

When supply drops suddenly, call a short meeting the same day, even if the detail is not yet complete. When supply is steady, review the rules weekly rather than daily, so that people do not feel constantly unsettled. When people are ill, adjust the medical exception and record it, rather than making a private arrangement. When a new arrival joins the group, recount the stock and recalculate the days left before serving anything extra.

## Common mistakes

- Making the decision in a rush, without the numbers.
- Letting the leaders take larger shares without saying so.
- Changing the rules without telling anyone.
- Counting the stock only once, at the beginning.

## Troubleshooting

If the numbers do not match, count again with a different pair of people, and report the difference honestly. If a person is refused a share they believe they are owed, reread the principles together and show where the decision was made. If the group wants to change a principle mid-crisis, delay the change to the next review unless the danger is immediate.

## What to do when someone takes more

![Path: person-standing with note, arrow-straight to step-box Private talk, arrow-curved to step-box Group; labels Concern, Private talk, Agreed change, Group decision; private steps feed back into public ones.](assets/making-rationing-decisions-as-a-group-5.svg)

If you discover that someone has taken more than their share, do not punish them in public. Speak to them privately, first, to understand why. If the need was real, adjust the rules. If the taking was deliberate, agree a fair response with the group, and make the stock count transparent again.

## Accept the cost

Rationing feels unfair to someone every time. Explain the reasons, and show that the rules apply to everyone, including the leaders. A group that accepts a hard decision for clear reasons is usually stronger afterwards.

## Practice before you need it

Practise a mock ledger with paper and stones before the real shortage. Agree the principles on a calm day, when people can think clearly about fairness. Write the first version of the board, so that the process is already familiar when the stock falls.

## Checklist

- [ ] The stock has been counted by two people and written down.
- [ ] The principles were agreed before the decision.
- [ ] The decision has a start date and a review date.
- [ ] The rules apply to the leaders too.

**Warning:** never hide supplies from the group, or take more than your share. It breaks trust and can break the group. Hidden food is a danger to everyone, including the person who hid it.

# Routines for Children

Children feel safer when they know what happens next. A simple daily routine gives them security, even when everything else has changed. Their questions often come from fear of the unknown, and a predictable day answers many of them before they are asked.

![Routine card: five step-box in order of the day; labels Meals, Play, Learning, Sleep and others; the card's order is the schedule itself. NEEDS: picture-icon](assets/routines-for-children-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Children read the mood of the adults around them, and they notice when plans change without explanation. A crisis removes their school, their friends and often their own space. A steady routine restores some of that structure. It also helps them sleep, eat regularly and keep their minds active, which lowers the risk of illness and of behaviour that adults find difficult. Routine is not a luxury for children in a crisis. It is part of their care.

## Keep the same shape

![Sequence: six step-box in a row with arrow-straight and arrow-loop back to the first; labels Wake, Eat, Work, Play, Rest, Sleep; order repeats daily and matters more than clock times.](assets/routines-for-children-2.svg)

Routine does not need a timetable. It needs a repeated order:

- Wake, eat, work, play, rest and sleep at roughly the same times each day.
- Keep meals at set times, even when the food is simple. A shared meal is a reliable anchor.
- Give the day a clear start and a clear end, such as a morning song and a bedtime story.

Draw the routine as a row of pictures on a card, and put it where the child can see it.

## Give them a job

> @image Photograph of a child carrying a small water container carefully, documentary style, black and white

Children who contribute feel less helpless, and they sleep better after a useful day.

- Small jobs such as carrying water, sorting wood or helping with meals give children purpose. Match the job to the child's age and strength.
- Praise the effort, not only the result. "You carried that carefully" builds more confidence than "well done" alone.

## Make learning part of the day

![Sun cue: ground-line horizon, sun at three heights, compass-rose and shadow-stick beside; labels Morning, Noon, Evening; the sun's position is a time and direction cue. NEEDS: sun](assets/routines-for-children-3.svg)

Learning does not need books or power.

- Read aloud, count, draw maps, or practise writing in the dust or on paper.
- Play simple games that teach skills, such as knots or navigation by the sun.

Keep learning sessions short, perhaps twenty minutes, then switch to play.

![Day split: timeline-axis with a short step-box and a longer step-box after it; labels Learning, Play; learning stays a short slot and play follows. NEEDS: clock-face](assets/routines-for-children-4.svg)

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Draw the card** with five or six pictures, in the order of the day.
2. **Walk through it** with the child, naming each picture aloud.
3. **Start the day** at the same time each morning, with the same greeting.
4. **Run each block** for its agreed time, and signal the change with a sound or a song.
5. **End with the talk** and the bedtime story, in the same place each evening.

## Give play space

Children need active play and time outdoors, within safe limits. Set a boundary, such as the edge of the courtyard, and keep them within sight. Check the space for sharp objects, open water and unstable walls before play begins.

## Talk about the day

![Circle: four person-sitting around a ring, arrow-curved passing round, a token each; labels Speaker, Next; the evening talk gives every child a turn. NEEDS: token](assets/routines-for-children-5.svg)

End the day with a short talk: what went well, what was hard, and what happens tomorrow. Keep it honest and calm, and avoid details that frighten. A child who can name a worry is better placed to manage it.

## Adjusting for conditions

In heat, move the outdoor play to the cool hours and keep the afternoon for quiet games. In rain, use the same routine indoors, with the play block replaced by a game or a song. When a child is ill, keep the anchors, the meal and the story, but reduce the work and learning. When there are many children, pair older children with younger ones for the work blocks, so that each child has a companion.

## Common mistakes

- Changing bedtime and mealtimes every day, which leaves children unsure.
- Giving no task at all, which can leave children restless and anxious.
- Asking children to hold adult secrets about danger or shortage.
- Keeping the routine only in an adult's head, so that children cannot check it.

## Troubleshooting

If a child refuses the routine, ask them to draw one of the pictures differently, which gives them a sense of ownership. If the bedtime story keeps being interrupted, move it earlier and shorten it. If a child is frightened of the dark, keep a small light in the sleeping area and let them choose the story.

## What to do when a routine breaks

Children may become clingy, angry or unable to sleep when routine breaks. Return to the anchors first: the meal, the bedtime story and the morning greeting. Explain the change in simple words, and keep the next day as close to normal as possible.

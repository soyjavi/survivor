# Daily Structure

Without routine, days blur together and people lose energy and motivation. Tasks get forgotten, meals are skipped, and nobody is sure who was supposed to collect the water. A simple structure gives each day shape and purpose, and it makes the crisis easier to manage one block at a time.

![Timeline: timeline-axis with four timeline-marker, each over a step-box task, axe at midday, person-sitting in the afternoon; labels Morning, Midday, Afternoon, Evening; heavy work sits at midday, rest in the afternoon.](assets/daily-structure-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Crises produce constant small demands: a leaking jar, a crying child, a visitor at the gate. Without structure, every demand feels urgent, and people spend their energy reacting instead of choosing. A daily shape lets the group decide in advance what matters most. It also gives people something to return to after a bad moment, which lowers anxiety and reduces the number of mistakes made in a rush.

## Plan the day

Divide the day into four blocks. Keep the times flexible, since light and weather decide much of the timing.

1. **Morning:** check the stock, water and weather, then set the day's tasks. Do this before any heavy work starts.
2. **Midday:** main work, such as water collection, cooking and repairs. Schedule the hardest tasks while energy is highest.
3. **Afternoon:** rest, maintenance and learning. Many people feel the heat and fatigue most at this time.
4. **Evening:** shared meal, a short review of the day, and preparation for the night, such as checking the fire is safe and the doors are secure.

## Set priorities

![Priority stack: three narrow step-box at the peak, one wide step-box as base, bracket around the peak; labels Finish first, Everything else; the base is optional when the day goes wrong.](assets/daily-structure-2.svg)

Write the three most important tasks for each day. Finish those first. Everything else is a bonus. This keeps a long list from paralysing you, and it means that if the day goes wrong, the essentials are still done.

## Step-by-step in detail

![Board layout: table-header with Date and rain for weather, three step-box priorities, each with person-standing owner and person-sitting back-up; labels Owner, Back-up; no priority depends on one absence.](assets/daily-structure-3.svg)

1. **Open the board.** At the morning meeting, write the date, the weather and any new information.
2. **List every task.** Let each person add the jobs they can see, without judging them yet.
3. **Pick three.** Choose the three tasks that protect life, water and shelter, and mark them clearly.
4. **Assign names.** Next to each task, write the name of one person and one back-up.
5. **Close the day.** In the evening, tick what is done, move what is not into tomorrow, and note any problem.

## Protect rest

![Clock diagram: a large arrow-curved arc for work and a smaller arrow-curved arc for rest in the afternoon; labels Work, Rest; rest is a fixed slice of the day, not leftover time. NEEDS: clock-face](assets/daily-structure-4.svg)

Plan at least one period of rest each day. Do not fill every hour with work. Rest is not wasted time: tired people make slow decisions and careless errors, especially with fire, knives and water.

## Keep a shared board

> @image Photograph of a shared task board on a wall in a camp with names and ticks written on it, documentary style, black and white

Keep a visible board with the day's tasks, who is doing what, and what is done. Use a large sheet of paper or a wall surface. Update it at the morning and evening meetings. This keeps the group aligned and stops two people from doing the same job while another is left undone.

## Make room for meaning

Include something that matters beyond survival, such as a conversation, a game, prayer, reading or music. People need meaning as well as food. A daily activity that has no practical purpose is still worth keeping.

## Adjusting for conditions

In the hottest hours, move heavy work to early morning or late evening, and make the midday block a rest block. When rain closes the outdoors, move repairs to dry tasks such as sorting, mending or inventory. When the group is small, combine blocks and keep the three priorities. When the group is large, give each section its own board and send one representative to the main meeting.

## Common mistakes

![Warning set: three warning in a row, each beside an element, timeline-marker with no gap, person-standing alone holding the plan, empty step-box; labels No rest, One person, No name; each sign points to a rigid routine.](assets/daily-structure-5.svg)

- Starting the day with a long list and no priorities.
- Leaving no time for rest, which leads to exhaustion by the third day.
- Keeping the plan in one person's head rather than on the board.
- Writing tasks without names, so that everyone assumes someone else will do them.

## Troubleshooting

If nobody updates the board, ask the person who wrote the last entry to update it at the next meeting, and make the job rotate. If the priorities keep changing, stop and check whether new information has arrived that the group has not shared. If the board becomes cluttered, rewrite it, keeping only today's tasks and the three priorities.

## What to do when the plan fails

Some days will not go to plan. A storm, an illness or a broken pump will break the schedule. Do not abandon the structure. Instead, move the three priorities into the next block, and make a new plan at the evening review.

## Checklist

- [ ] Today's three priorities are written down.
- [ ] The shared board is up to date.
- [ ] There is a rest period in the afternoon.
- [ ] The evening review has been held.

**Warning:** do not let the routine become rigid. Adjust it when the weather or needs change. A plan that cannot bend will fail on the first bad day.

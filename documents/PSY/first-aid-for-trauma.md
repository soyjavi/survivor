# First Aid for Psychological Trauma

After a frightening event, many people feel shocked, confused or unable to act. They may shake, cry, go silent or repeat the same words. Simple, calm support helps most people settle, and it reduces the risk of longer harm. You do not need training to offer it. You need a steady voice, a safe place and a willingness to stay.

![Sequence: four step-box in a row joined by arrow-straight, do-not above reversed order; labels Safety, Sit, Speak, Listen; each step waits for the one before.](assets/first-aid-for-trauma-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Trauma changes how a person processes the world for hours or days. A shocked person may not understand instructions, may not feel thirst, and may act in ways they will later regret. Early, calm contact is often the difference between a person who settles within an hour and one who becomes stuck in fear. Supporting them also protects you, because a calm helper keeps the rest of the group steady.

## Make the scene safe

![Safe-area map: caution zone with rock for debris, flame, waves; line-dashed gap, then person-sitting at a quiet gathering spot; labels Hazard zone, Gathering spot, Distance; the spot sits far from danger.](assets/first-aid-for-trauma-2.svg)

Before you offer support, check that the person and you are out of danger. Move them away from the source of the event, such as debris, smoke, water or a crowd, to a quiet place with people they trust. Do not approach a dangerous scene to reach them. Wait for it to be secured.

## Equipment and materials

Keep a blanket, a cup, a bottle of clean water and a small torch within reach. A seat, such as a stool or folded cloth, helps the person rest without lying on cold ground. If there is an injury, a basic first aid kit is needed as well, but treat injuries first and emotional support second.

## Support

> @image Photograph of a responder kneeling at eye level beside a seated person and offering a cup of water, documentary style, black and white

Follow these steps in order:

1. Stay with them, and speak calmly and slowly. Your tone matters more than your words.
2. Introduce yourself and say what you are doing: "I am Ana. I am going to sit with you for a while."
3. Ask simple questions: "Are you hurt? Do you need water?" Keep each question short, and wait for an answer.
4. Offer practical help, such as a blanket, water or a seat. Physical comfort often calms distress faster than talk.
5. Do not force them to talk about the event. Some people need to be quiet.

> @image Photograph of a blanket and a folded jacket placed on a bench beside a seated survivor, documentary style, black and white

## Listen

![Dialogue: person-standing left, person-standing right, two speech-bubble linked by arrow-both-ways; labels Reflective sentence, Key word repeated; listening echoes their words, not fixes the feeling. NEEDS: speech-bubble](assets/first-aid-for-trauma-3.svg)

Let them speak, if they want to. Acknowledge what they say without judging it: "That sounds very frightening." Repeat the key words back to them, which shows you are listening. Do not rush to fix the feeling.

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Check the scene** for hazards, and move away from any that remain.
2. **Assess the person** for injury, and treat bleeding or breathing problems first.
3. **Sit at their level,** a little to the side rather than directly in front.
4. **Offer one thing,** such as water or a blanket, and wait.
5. **Stay for at least ten minutes,** or until the person is settled or handed to a health worker.

## Avoid

![Avoid set: three do-not in a row over a pointing hand, a promise card and arrow-loop; labels Blame, Broken promise, Looping question; these three habits restart fear. NEEDS: pointing-hand](assets/first-aid-for-trauma-4.svg)

Some responses make distress worse, even when they are kindly meant:

- Do not say it was their fault, or that they should be fine.
- Do not make promises you cannot keep, such as "your house will be rebuilt".
- Do not ask them to repeat the details again and again. Each retelling can renew the fear.
- Do not crowd them with many people at once.

## Adjusting for conditions

In heat, move the person into shade and offer water in small sips. In cold, wrap them in dry layers before anything else. When many people are affected, assign one helper to each person, and keep a list of who has been seen. When children are involved, sit at their height, use simple words, and let a trusted adult stay close.

## What to do when it fails

If the person becomes more distressed, grows louder, or cannot follow simple instructions, reduce stimulation. Lower your voice, move other people back, and offer one clear thing to do, such as holding a cup of water.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with questions about what happened, before the person is calm.
- Leaving a person alone after the first contact.
- Telling them how they should feel.

## Watch for

Seek professional help if someone cannot calm down, stays unresponsive, or shows signs of injury, or if the distress lasts for days. Watch for changes in sleep, eating and talking, and tell a health worker about them.

## Practice before you need it

Practise the five support steps with a partner, taking turns to be the one who is shocked. Learn the phrases you will use, such as "I am here" and "You are safe now", and say them aloud until they come easily.

## Checklist

- [ ] The place is safe, and the person is out of danger.
- [ ] I have introduced myself and explained what I am doing.
- [ ] I have offered water, warmth or a seat.
- [ ] I have listened without pressing for details.

**Warning:** a person who is confused or drowsy after a head injury needs medical care. Do not treat it as shock alone.

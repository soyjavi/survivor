# Resolving Conflict

Stress increases disagreement. Tired, hungry and frightened people argue over small things, and those small things often stand for deeper worries about fairness or respect. Left alone, small quarrels turn into lasting divisions. Address them early, calmly and in private where possible.

![Sequence: six step-box in a row joined by arrow-straight; labels Pause, Meet privately, Each speaks, Summarise, Shared goal, One change; the pause comes before any meeting.](assets/resolving-conflict-1.svg)

## Why it matters

In a crisis, people depend on one another for water, food, warmth and safety. A quarrel between two adults can stop shared work, make children anxious and split a household into camps. Repairing the relationship is therefore a survival task. A conflict settled early takes an hour. The same conflict left to grow can absorb days of attention and leave lasting bitterness, even after the crisis has passed.

## Early signs

![Warning set: three warning in a row over a door, a speech-bubble and an empty step-box; labels Withdrawal, Remarks, Avoided jobs; early signs appear before open anger. NEEDS: door, speech-bubble](assets/resolving-conflict-2.svg)

Catch conflict before it becomes open anger. Watch for:

- Withdrawal, sharp remarks, or someone avoiding shared tasks.
- Arguments over small items that are really about fairness or respect, such as who used the last of the soap.
- Two people who stop speaking to each other, or who take sides in every discussion.

## Steps

Work through these steps in order, and do not skip the pause:

1. **Pause.** Do not respond while angry. Wait until both people can speak calmly, which may take an hour or a day.
2. **Meet privately.** Choose a quiet place, away from others, and sit at the same level.
3. **Each person speaks,** without interruption, about what happened and how it felt. Set a time limit for each turn if needed.
4. **Summarise.** Repeat each person's point back, so they feel heard. Ask, "Have I understood you correctly?"
5. **Find a shared goal,** such as safety, fairness or a working household.
6. **Agree one small change,** and check it again in a day or two.

Do not try to settle every grievance in one meeting. One agreed change is a success.

![Card: step-box with One agreed change in the centre and timeline-marker with Check-by date in the corner; one change with a date, not a full settlement.](assets/resolving-conflict-3.svg)

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Open calmly,** by saying what the meeting is for and how long it will last.
2. **Set two ground rules:** no interruptions, and no insults.
3. **Let the first person speak** for their agreed time, without comment.
4. **Let the second person speak** in the same way, and do not compare.
5. **Summarise both points** in neutral language, and confirm each person agrees.
6. **Propose one change,** ask each person to accept or reject it, and write it down.

## Keep it fair

![Seating plan: table-grid, person-sitting left and right at equal height, person-sitting at the head; labels Person A, Person B, Mediator; equal seating and a neutral third person are the set-up.](assets/resolving-conflict-4.svg)

Apply the same rules to everyone, including the people who mediate. Avoid taking sides, and do not discuss one person's problems with others. Gossip turns a private disagreement into a public one.

## Adjusting for conditions

When the quarrel is about a shortage, bring the stock ledger to the meeting and show the figures, so the argument is about numbers rather than character. When the quarrel is between two young people, include a trusted adult as the neutral third person. When the group is large, handle conflicts within the sub-group first, and escalate only if the agreed change fails. When people are exhausted, schedule the meeting after a rest, not in the middle of the night or during a shift.

## Common mistakes

- Mediating while one person is still angry.
- Trying to prove who is right, rather than finding what each person needs.
- Ending the meeting without an agreed next step.
- Letting a third person take sides in the debate.

## Troubleshooting

If one person refuses to meet, speak with them separately, listen to their concern, and ask whether they will accept a written agreement. If the agreed change is broken within a day, meet again without blame and ask what made it hard. If the argument returns to the same point every time, write the disagreement on a card and set it aside until the basic needs are secure.

## What to do when it fails

> @image Photograph of two people working on separate tasks in the same yard, documentary style, black and white

If the agreed change does not hold, meet again, and ask what got in the way. Sometimes a third person, such as a respected elder or a neutral neighbour, can help. If the two people cannot share a space, arrange their shifts and tasks so they are not working side by side until the tension eases.

> @image Photograph of an elder sitting between two people at a shaded table, documentary style, black and white

## When it is serious

![Distance diagram: person-standing left and right at ends of a line-dashed safe distance, phone-call outside the space; labels Safe distance, Call for help; separation comes before any mediation when a threat is present.](assets/resolving-conflict-5.svg)

If a conflict involves threats, violence or abuse, separate the people involved and seek help. Do not try to mediate a violent conflict alone.

## Practice before you need it

Rehearse the six steps with two volunteers on a calm day, using a small, harmless disagreement. Practise summarising the other person's view until it feels natural. Agree in advance who the neutral third person will be for the group.

## Checklist

- [ ] Both people have had calm time to speak.
- [ ] I have repeated each point back.
- [ ] We have agreed one change, with a date to check it.
- [ ] I have not discussed either person with others.

**Warning:** never leave a conflict to fester. A small disagreement, unresolved, can divide a whole group, and it can turn into harm when people are under pressure.

# Leadership in a Group

A group in crisis needs clear direction, but it also needs people who can act when the leader is tired, hurt or absent. Good leadership shares the load. A group that depends on one person breaks when that person falls ill, and a leader who makes every decision alone often becomes the group's bottleneck.

![Role chart: step-box Leader at top, elbow-connector to six step-box roles, each with a smaller step-box beneath; labels Water, Food, Fire, Medical, Children, Communication, Deputy; no role lacks a second name.](assets/leadership-in-a-group-1.svg)

## Why it matters

In a crisis, every hour without a decision costs something: water is not collected, a fire is not checked, a sick person waits. Yet decisions made in haste, or by one exhausted person, cause the same damage. Leadership is the discipline of making decisions quickly, openly and with enough people involved that the group will carry them out. The leader's real job is to make the group capable, not to be needed for everything.

## What a good leader does

![Radial: survivor-mark at centre, five step-box around it joined by line-solid; labels Calm instructions, Explains reasons, Listens to dissent, Admits mistakes, Cares for wellbeing; leadership is habits, not control.](assets/leadership-in-a-group-2.svg)

- Gives clear, calm instructions, one task at a time.
- Explains the reasons for decisions, so people can accept them.
- Listens to people who disagree, and takes their points seriously.
- Admits mistakes and changes course when needed.
- Looks after the group's wellbeing as well as its tasks.

## Share the work

Divide the group's needs into roles, and write them on the shared board:

- Assign roles: water, food, fire, medical, children, communication.
- Name a deputy for each role, so the work continues when someone is unavailable.
- Rotate heavy and unpleasant tasks, such as latrine cleaning or night watch, so that no one carries them forever.

## Make decisions well

![Decision flow: four step-box in a row joined by arrow-straight, arrow-loop from Review back to Facts; labels Facts, Options, Decide, Review; review feeds fresh facts into the next decision.](assets/leadership-in-a-group-3.svg)

Use a simple sequence for every important decision:

1. Gather the facts quickly. Ask the people who know, and write down what is confirmed.
2. Consider the options, including the risks of doing nothing. Inaction is a decision too.
3. Decide, and announce the decision clearly, with the date it takes effect.
4. Review it when new information arrives. See Making Rationing Decisions as a Group.

Keep decisions short. A meeting that lasts an hour in a crisis usually means the question was not framed well.

![Meeting: table-grid as round table with person-sitting figures, a timer beside and a notebook at the centre; labels Time limit, Written record; both are built into each meeting. NEEDS: round-table, clock-face, notebook](assets/leadership-in-a-group-4.svg)

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Name the question** in one sentence, and say who must decide.
2. **Set a time limit** for hearing views, usually five to ten minutes.
3. **Ask the quiet members first,** so that louder voices do not set the answer.
4. **Write the decision and its reason** in the notebook, with the review date.
5. **Name the person** who will carry it out, and their deputy.

## Look after yourself

![Rota: table-header with days, table-grid rows, rest cells legend-dot, each with a step-box deputy; labels Leader, Deputy, Rest; handover is built into the rota.](assets/leadership-in-a-group-5.svg)

Leaders carry a heavy load. Rest, eat, and ask others to take over tasks. A leader who collapses leaves the group without direction, so rest is part of the job, not a luxury. Name a deputy who can take charge for a full day.

## Adjusting for conditions

When the group is small, the leader can make routine decisions alone, but must still record them. When the group is large, create sub-groups for each area, each with its own lead, and bring their leads together once a day. When leaders are elected, set a term, such as one week, so that the position is never permanent by default. When the group has a hard decision ahead, bring in an outside adviser, such as a health worker, to check the facts.

## Common mistakes

- Holding all information personally, so that others cannot act.
- Making a decision in private and expecting the group to accept it without reasons.
- Ignoring quiet members who may see a problem earlier than anyone.
- Confusing being busy with being effective.

## Troubleshooting

If a deputy does not take charge when the leader is absent, ask them to shadow the leader on the next task, then hand over a small decision. If meetings keep running over time, cut the agenda to three items and move the rest to the next day. If people stop attending, go to them with the decision in person.

## What to do when the group splits

> @image Photograph of a community meeting under a shade canopy with people seated in a circle, documentary style, black and white

If the group divides over a decision, pause the vote, hear each side in turn, and look for an option that meets the main concern of each. If the split continues, agree a fair method to decide, such as a short secret vote, and accept the result together.

## Practice before you need it

Run a mock meeting with the role chart, and make one practice decision with a clear reason and a review date. Check that each deputy knows their role and where the notebook is kept. Practising the handover is the surest way to avoid a leadership gap in an emergency.

## Checklist

- [ ] Every role has a named deputy.
- [ ] Decisions are written down, with reasons and dates.
- [ ] I have heard from the person who disagrees with me.
- [ ] I have rested, and someone has taken over a task.

**Warning:** do not make decisions alone in a crisis that affects many people. Involve trusted members of the group, and never let one person control water, food or medicine without oversight.

# Coping with Grief

A disaster brings loss: of people, homes, possessions and certainty. Grief is a natural response. It takes time, and it does not follow a set pattern. Some people cry at once, some feel nothing for weeks, and some are overwhelmed by small reminders such as a familiar cup or a song. All of these responses can be normal.

![Sequence: three step-box in a row joined by arrow-straight; labels Listen, Acknowledge, Offer practical help; acknowledgement comes before any offer so help lands as care.](assets/coping-with-grief-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Grief that is ignored does not disappear. It can show up as anger at a neighbour, as withdrawal from the group, or as a body that will not rest. Naming the loss, even in a few words, gives the grieving person permission to feel it without guilt. It also helps the group. When one person is visibly mourning, others know it is safe to mourn too, and the shared work of the crisis is less likely to be disrupted by sudden outbursts.

## Common reactions

![Radial diagram: start-or-end circle at centre, four step-box around it joined by line-solid; labels Numbness, Sadness and guilt, Poor sleep or appetite, Waves of memory; all four are expected, not failures.](assets/coping-with-grief-2.svg)

Grief can appear in the body and the mind:

- Numbness, shock or disbelief.
- Sadness, anger or guilt, including guilt about surviving.
- Trouble sleeping, eating or concentrating.
- Sudden waves of memory or tears, often triggered without warning.

These reactions are normal for weeks or months. They become a concern when they stop someone from functioning at all.

## Look after yourself

Grief drains energy, so basic care matters more than usual.

- Keep eating and drinking, even if food tastes of nothing. Small amounts often work better than large meals.
- Rest when you can, and accept that you may not manage a full day.
- Keep some routine, even small. A set time for water or a short walk gives the day shape.
- Talk about the person or loss with someone you trust. Naming the person keeps them present without becoming overwhelming.

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Acknowledge the loss out loud.** Say the name of the person or the thing that is gone, once, in a calm voice.
2. **Allow a pause.** Do not rush into tasks. Sit for a few minutes, even if the body feels restless.
3. **Choose one small act of care.** Eat one meal, wash your face, or walk to the water point and back.
4. **Mark the day's end.** Light a small lamp, say a few words or share a memory before sleep.
5. **Check in tomorrow.** Grief changes from day to day, so ask again how the person is feeling.

## Support others

![Phrase pair: two speech-bubble, the left with do-not and At least, the right with do and I am sorry, this is very hard; the helpful phrase names the loss directly. NEEDS: speech-bubble](assets/coping-with-grief-3.svg)

Most people want to help but do not know what to say.

- Listen more than you speak. Let the person decide how much to share.
- Avoid phrases that minimise the loss, such as "at least". Say something simpler, such as "I am sorry. This is very hard."
- Offer practical help, such as food, a walk or a task. Be specific: "I will bring you dinner at six" is easier to accept than "let me know if you need anything".

## Children

> @image Photograph of a child drawing a picture of a family member, documentary style, black and white

Children grieve in short bursts, then play again. Answer their questions honestly and simply, using words they understand. Let them see that sadness is allowed, and that adults also cry. Avoid telling them the person is "sleeping", which can cause fear about bedtime.

## Adjusting for conditions

![Memorial: stone as rock, beside a candle; label Memorial; a ceremony can be made from two household objects when no funeral is possible. NEEDS: candle](assets/coping-with-grief-4.svg)

When there is no funeral or burial, hold a small ceremony with whatever is available: a song, a candle, a few words at the group meal. When bodies cannot be recovered quickly, respect the uncertainty and avoid pressing for final answers. When several people are grieving at once, rotate who sits with each one, so that no single helper is worn out.

## Common mistakes

- Expecting a fixed timeline, such as "should be over by now".
- Hiding your own grief completely from children, which can make them feel alone.
- Taking on another person's grief as your own task.
- Forcing someone to talk about the loss before they are ready.

## Troubleshooting

If the grieving person refuses food, offer small portions and sit with them while they eat. If they shout or withdraw, keep your voice low and stay within reach without pressing for conversation. If anger turns toward others, separate the people involved and give the grieving person a short task away from the conflict.

## When to seek help

![Decision path: start-or-end Distress sign, decision Self-harm or weeks not functioning, arrow-branch yes to three step-box; labels Yes, No, Health post, Counsellor, Community leader; the reader sees when to escalate.](assets/coping-with-grief-5.svg)

Seek professional support if someone talks about harming themselves, cannot function for weeks, or uses alcohol or drugs to cope. Support can come from a health post, a counsellor or a trusted religious or community leader.

# Child Fever and Dehydration Signs

Fever and diarrhoea are common in shelters, and both make children lose fluid faster than they can replace it. Dehydration can become dangerous within a day, and young children have less reserve than adults. Watching carefully, offering fluids often, and knowing the urgent signs are the main tools available.

## Recognising fever

A child has a fever when the body feels hot to the touch, or when a thermometer reads above the normal range for the child's age. Feeling warm is not always reliable, so check the chest or back and watch behaviour as well as temperature.

1. Check the child's temperature with a thermometer if one is available, and note the time and reading.
2. Look at how the child behaves. A child who plays, drinks, and responds is usually less worrying than one who is limp or confused.
3. Keep the child lightly dressed and in a cool room. Do not wrap a feverish child in heavy blankets.
4. Offer fluids regularly, even when the child does not feel like eating.

## Recognising dehydration

Dehydration means the body has lost more water and salts than it has taken in. Early signs are subtle, and later signs can be serious.

Signs in children include:

- Fewer wet nappies or toilet visits than usual, or urine that is dark and strong-smelling.
- A dry mouth and tongue, and few or no tears when crying.
- Sunken eyes, or a sunken soft spot on the head in babies.
- Unusual thirst, or refusal to drink.
- Dry, cool hands and feet, or skin that stays pinched when lifted.
- Lethargy, irritability, or a child who is hard to wake.

To check the skin, gently pinch the skin on the back of the hand or the belly and release it. In a well-hydrated child it springs back quickly. In a dehydrated child it returns slowly. This check is helpful but not sufficient on its own.

## Giving fluids

Fluids are the core treatment for dehydration caused by fever or diarrhoea. The goal is to replace what is lost in small, steady amounts.

1. Give oral rehydration solution if it is available, following the packet instructions exactly.
2. If it is not available, offer clean water, breast milk for infants, and soups or broths with some salt.
3. Give small sips every few minutes, using a cup or spoon. A child who vomits should be given a little more slowly, not stopped.
4. Continue normal feeding as soon as the child is able, because food helps recovery.

- Avoid large volumes of plain water at one time for very young children.
- Avoid sugary drinks, strong tea, and undiluted fruit juice, which can worsen diarrhoea.
- Do not stop fluids because a child is vomiting; keep offering small amounts.

**Warning:** Do not use homemade salt and sugar mixes unless you are sure of the measurements. Too much salt can be as dangerous as too little.

## Fever medicine and comfort

Fever itself is usually the body fighting infection, so the goal is comfort and hydration, not forcing the temperature down at any cost. Use only medicines that are known to be suitable for the child's age and weight, and read the section on children's doses before giving anything.

Sponging a child with lukewarm water can help comfort. Never use cold water, alcohol, or vinegar.

## When to seek care urgently

Seek urgent care for any of these signs:

1. A child under three months with any fever above the normal range.
2. A child with no wet nappies or no urine for eight hours or more.
3. Repeated vomiting that prevents any fluid from being kept down.
4. Blood in the stool or vomit, or diarrhoea that lasts more than a few days.
5. Fever with a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade when pressed, or sudden confusion.
6. A child who is floppy, cold, breathing fast or with effort, or cannot be woken properly.
7. Fits, seizures, or a child who seems much sicker than the fever alone would explain.

If you are unsure, seek care. A health worker can assess the child and advise on fluids, medicines, and whether admission is needed. Bring a note of the time the fever started, the temperature readings, the number of wet nappies or urine output, and any medicine already given.

**Warning:** A child who is drowsy, will not drink, or has not passed urine for many hours needs immediate medical attention. Do not wait to see whether the condition improves.

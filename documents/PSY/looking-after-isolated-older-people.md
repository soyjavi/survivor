# Looking After Isolated Older People

Older people who live alone are among the most at risk in a crisis. They may be unable to leave, short of medicine, or unnoticed by others. Their illnesses can worsen quickly, and they may not ask for help until they are in serious trouble. A short daily visit can prevent a great deal of harm.

![Records: table-grid street list beside table-grid tick sheet with table-header; labels Name, Address, Time seen, Water checked; the list is the source and the sheet records each visit.](assets/looking-after-isolated-older-people-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Isolation hides danger. An older person who lives alone may be dehydrated for two days before anyone notices, or may fall in the night and lie on the floor until morning. Their own sense of thirst, hunger and cold often weakens with age, so they may not feel the warning signs. A regular visitor is the simplest safeguard available, and it costs little more than a few minutes of walking each day.

## Find them

![Street map: map with legend-dot houses numbered along line-solid, visited ones legend-solid, unvisited legend-dashed; labels Visited, Not yet; the blank houses are the ones still to be found.](assets/looking-after-isolated-older-people-2.svg)

Many isolated people are invisible until someone looks for them.

- Make a list of older people on your street or block, with their names and addresses.
- Ask neighbours and family about anyone who lives alone, and note the people they mention.
- Check the list each day, and mark who has been seen. Record the time of each visit.

## Check on them

![Sequence: four step-box in a row with arrow-straight; labels Visit, Water and food, Health, Toilet and washing; stock is checked before the conversation.](assets/looking-after-isolated-older-people-3.svg)

Follow a routine each time you visit:

1. Visit once or twice a day, or ask a neighbour to do so.
2. Check that they have water, food, medicine and warmth. Look at the stock, not only what they tell you.
3. Ask about their health, and watch for confusion, weakness or a change in behaviour. A sudden change often signals illness.
4. Help them to the toilet and washing if they need it.

Keep the visit short but regular. A calm, predictable visitor is often more reassuring than a long, irregular one.

![Visit log: table-header over table-grid; labels Date, Time, Note, Next check; each visit is recorded so another person can pick it up.](assets/looking-after-isolated-older-people-4.svg)

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Knock and announce yourself** by name, and wait for a reply before entering.
2. **Greet them** and ask one open question about how they slept.
3. **Look at the stock** of water and food, and note what is running low.
4. **Check the medicines** against the card, and confirm the next dose time.
5. **Record the visit** with the time, what you saw and anything to report.

## Support them

> @image Photograph of a volunteer helping an older person carry a water container, documentary style, black and white

- Share food and water, and keep them supplied first, before the rest of the household.
- Keep their medicines and a written list of doses with them, in one clear place.
- Help them move to the shelter if needed, and arrange transport before the move.
- Talk with them, and listen. Loneliness is a real danger, and a conversation can lift mood as much as a meal.

## Signs of serious problems

![Warning column: four warning stacked, each beside a step-box; labels Confusion, Fall, Chest pain, Breathing trouble; these are emergencies to report at once, not to wait on.](assets/looking-after-isolated-older-people-5.svg)

Seek medical help for confusion that is new, a fall, chest pain, breathing problems, or signs of dehydration, such as dry mouth, dark urine or dizziness when standing.

## Adjusting for conditions

In heat, visit in the cool hours and check that the person has shade and drinking water through the afternoon. In cold, check the fire or heater and confirm they have dry blankets. When the streets are flooded or blocked, ask a neighbour with a route to visit, and mark the house as needing a different approach. When a person has memory loss, keep the visit short, repeat the same steps, and write the routine on a card they can see.

## What to do when they refuse help

Some older people will not want to leave their home or accept help. Respect their choices where they can make them, and explain the risks clearly. Keep checking in, and ask a family member or the local authority to visit if you are worried about their safety.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming that a quiet person is well.
- Checking only once, at the beginning of the crisis.
- Forgetting that medicines run out faster when supply is short.
- Visiting without recording, so that nobody knows who was seen.

## Troubleshooting

If nobody answers the door, call their name, check the windows and ask a neighbour before entering. If you cannot enter safely, call a health worker or the local authority at once. If the person is unwilling to eat, offer small amounts often, and report any refusal of water to the health post, since dehydration can follow quickly.

## Practice before you need it

Walk the street list with a partner once on a calm day. Time how long each visit takes, and make the route efficient. Practise the questions you will ask, so that the visit feels routine rather than intrusive.

# Daily Hygiene Routine

A routine makes hygiene automatic. Set it at the start of the crisis, write it down, and it protects everyone without constant effort. Most infections in a household come from small gaps in habit, not from one large failure, so a steady routine is the strongest defence available.

## Why it matters

Without electricity, running water or a fixed timetable, hygiene slips quickly. People forget to wash hands after the latrine, food is left uncovered, and clothes stay damp. A routine turns those tasks into habits that need no decision, which matters most when people are tired, frightened or ill. It also spreads the work across the household, so that one exhausted adult is not left carrying every task alone.

## Equipment and materials

- A labelled wash bucket with a tap or lid, and a basin for washing hands.
- Soap, a small towel for each person, and a clean cloth for the face.
- A covered rubbish container and a stock of bags.
- A weekly board or notebook, with a pen that does not run in damp.
- A marked water container for drinking, separate from the wash water.

## Morning

1. Wash hands and face with a small amount of water.
2. Clean teeth. See Dental Care Without a Dentist.
3. Check children for signs of illness, such as fever, rash or lice, and separate any child who is unwell.
4. Empty and clean the wash bowl, then refill it with fresh water for the day.

## Step-by-step in detail

Tie the routine to moments that happen anyway, such as waking, eating and going to the latrine. Put the wash station where people pass it naturally, and keep the soap in the same place. Each evening, check the next day's water and rubbish plans, and put out the clean towels. Give each person one clear job for the week, such as filling the bucket, emptying rubbish or checking the latrine lid, and rotate the jobs every few weeks so that no one feels stuck with the worst one.

## Through the day

- Wash hands before food, after the toilet and after caring for anyone who is sick.
- Keep food covered, and clean up spills at once so they do not attract insects.
- Clean shared surfaces once a day. See Disinfecting Surfaces.
- Empty rubbish and food waste daily. See Rubbish and Garbage.

## Evening

1. Body wash with a damp cloth. See Body Washing with Little Water.
2. Wash the feet and dry them thoroughly, especially between the toes.
3. Clean and dry the bedding if it is damp. Air it in the sun where possible.
4. Check wounds and dress them.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Rainy season:** dry bedding and clothes indoors near a fan, and cover the water containers more tightly.
- **Dry season:** water is scarcer, so reduce the wash water per person and reuse rinse water for floors.
- **Crowded shelters:** stagger the morning routine so that the wash station is not overwhelmed.
- **Households with babies or elderly members:** add a midday check on nappies, skin and fluid intake.

## Weekly

- Wash clothes and bedding. See Laundry Without Power.
- Check stored water and food for contamination, such as cloudiness, smell or insect activity.
- Check the toilets and the wash station, and repair anything that leaks or cracks.

## Keep it going

Assign tasks to each person, and check them off on the board. Children can help with simple jobs such as filling the wash bowl, carrying waste or drying cups. Review the routine every week, and change anything that is not working.

## Checklist

- Hands washed at each key moment.
- Water containers covered and labelled.
- Rubbish bagged and removed.
- Sick people separated, with their own cup and cloth.
- Weekly tasks ticked on the board.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the evening wash when the day has been hard.
- Reusing the same wash water for too many people.
- Leaving food uncovered overnight.
- Letting the weekly jobs slip until the bedding is smelly and damp.

## Practice before you need it

Run the routine for three days at home before a crisis, or for a week in a camp that is calm. Notice which steps take longer than planned and which ones people forget. Change the order or the place of a step rather than adding new rules, because a routine that fits the household's natural rhythm survives longer than a strict one.

## When it fails

If the routine breaks down, go back to the morning steps first. Ask one person to lead the board each day, and make a short list of the three most important tasks. A simple routine that is followed is better than a detailed one that is ignored.

**Warning:** when illness appears, add extra hand washing and cleaning, and separate the sick person. Keep their cups, plates and cloths apart, and wash their clothes and bedding in hot water.

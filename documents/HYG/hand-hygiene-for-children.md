# Hand Hygiene for Children

Children touch their faces, food and playmates constantly, which makes them a common route for germs to enter a household. Good handwashing is one of the most effective habits you can build, but young children rarely wash properly without guidance. Routines work best when they are short, repeated at the same moments each day, and supported by adults who model the behaviour.

> @image Small child at a low sink standing on a wooden step, with an adult's hand gently guiding a bar of soap across the child's palms.

## Why Routines Matter

Children learn habits through repetition and predictability. A routine tied to an existing event, such as coming in from play or sitting down to eat, is easier to remember than a rule given at random. Keep the steps few, the language simple, and the expectation clear. Praise effort rather than perfection, and avoid turning handwashing into a punishment.

1. Identify three to four key moments: before eating, after using the toilet, after playing outside, and after touching animals or waste.
2. Use the same words each time, such as "wash, rub, rinse, dry".
3. Keep the routine short enough that a child can complete it without losing interest.

## Making It Simple

Young children need hands that can reach the water, a stable surface to stand on, and soap they can hold. A low step, a small bar of soap and a towel within reach make a large difference. If water is scarce, use a small measured cup and a basin, so that the child learns to use a limited amount properly.

- Count together or sing a short song for about twenty seconds, which is roughly the time needed to clean hands well.
- Show the child how to rub between fingers, backs of hands, thumbs and under nails.
- Let the child dry hands thoroughly on a clean cloth.

## Teaching by Showing

Children copy what they see. Wash your own hands in front of them at the same times, and say what you are doing. Let them take turns washing an adult's hands, or use a toy to demonstrate. When children see that adults follow the same rules, they are more likely to accept the routine as normal.

1. Demonstrate slowly at first, naming each step.
2. Invite the child to copy you and correct gently.
3. Praise specific actions, such as "you rubbed your thumbs well".
4. Repeat the demonstration every few days for younger children.

## Supervision

Supervision is essential until a child can wash properly without prompts, which usually takes several years and varies by child. Check the child's hands afterwards, looking for missed areas and dirt under nails. Keep nails short, since dirt collects under long nails and makes thorough washing harder. Watch for children who wash only under running water or rush through the steps, and correct those habits early.

Keep a simple checklist on the wall if it helps. Stickers or marks for completed routines can motivate children, provided the reward is small and the focus stays on the habit rather than competition.

## Adapting to Age

Children under three need hands-on help for every step, including lifting them up to the basin and holding the soap. From about three to six, they can follow a short sequence when reminded, and they enjoy simple rhymes that mark the steps. Older children can manage the routine alone but still need checks, particularly after play or messy activities.

Set expectations that match each child. A sticker chart can help younger children, while older ones respond better to a quick inspection and a discussion of why hands matter. Adjust the length of the routine if a child struggles with concentration, and build it up gradually.

## Handling Setbacks

Children forget, rush and resist, and this is normal. When a child skips a wash, remind them calmly and show the steps again rather than scolding. If a child refuses repeatedly, look for the cause: fear of cold water, discomfort with soap, or a wish for control are common reasons. Offer a choice, such as which towel to use or which song to sing.

**Warning:** Never leave young children alone with cleaning agents, soaps that contain strong chemicals, or hand sanitiser. Alcohol-based sanitiser is dangerous if swallowed in quantity, so use it only under direct supervision and store it out of reach. Teach children not to put their hands in their mouths or eyes, and keep the bathroom and water containers secured.

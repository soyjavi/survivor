# Caring for Pets in a Crisis

Pets need food, water, shelter and medical care, and they can spread disease to people. In a crisis they often become stressed, hungry or ill, and their needs compete with the family's own. Plan for them as part of the household from the start, not as an afterthought when supplies are already short.

## Why it matters

Animals that are hungry, frightened or ill behave in ways that put people at risk. A dog that scavenges rubbish can bring back disease, a cat that sleeps on bedding can carry fleas and worms, and a stressed animal may bite. Animals also depend entirely on the household for what they eat and drink. Planning for them early keeps the family safe, keeps the animals alive, and prevents the rushed decisions that often lead to abandonment.

## Equipment and materials

- Sealed food containers with lids, and a bag of spare food for each animal.
- Two marked water containers, one for animals and one for people.
- A carrier or secure crate for each animal, and a lead or harness.
- A small first aid kit for animals, with gauze, a clean cloth and a pair of blunt scissors.
- A waterproof bag for vaccination records, microchip details and a recent photo.
- Gloves, a bucket with a lid, and bags for waste.

## Food and water

- Store pet food in sealed containers, off the floor and away from rodents.
- Feed pets at set times, and do not overfeed. Hungry animals may scavenge dangerous food.
- Keep water fresh and clean, and separate from human drinking water. Use a marked container so that no one mixes them up. See Latrines and Waste for keeping water sources clear of waste.

## Hygiene

- Wash your hands after handling pets, their food or their waste.
- Keep pets out of food preparation areas and off sleeping mats.
- Clean up pet waste daily, and dispose of it away from water. See Latrines and Waste.

## Health

- Check pets daily for injuries, fleas, ticks and signs of illness, such as poor appetite, diarrhoea or discharge from the eyes.
- Keep vaccination records with the family documents, in a waterproof bag.
- Keep a basic first aid kit for animals, and know who nearby can help.

## Step-by-step in detail

Start each day with a short check. Look at the eyes, nose, coat and paws, and count the animal's breaths while it rests. Refill the water container first, then serve food in a bowl that is used only for that animal. Clean the bowl after each meal, rinsing it with clean water and drying it in the sun. Collect any waste with gloves, bag it, and place it in the lidded bucket. Wash your hands before you touch anything else.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Heat:** provide shade and extra water, and check the animal for heatstroke, which shows as heavy panting and weakness.
- **Cold:** keep animals dry and out of wind, and give them a blanket they can call their own.
- **Crowded shelters:** keep animals in a separate area with a clear route to the outside.
- **Limited food:** feed smaller meals more often, and do not share human food, which can be salty or spiced.

## Safety

- Keep pets on a lead or in a secure place, so that they do not roam into danger.
- Do not let pets eat dead animals or rubbish. Both can carry disease.
- Keep pets away from fire, fuel and medicines, which are poisonous to animals.

## Checklist

- Seven days of food and water for each animal.
- A carrier or lead, and a collar with identification.
- Vaccination records and a recent photo of each pet.
- A plan for where each animal will stay if you must move.

## Common mistakes

- Sharing a water bowl with people, or using the same cup.
- Leaving waste near the house, where flies carry it to food.
- Waiting until evacuation to find a carrier.
- Ignoring early signs of illness because the family is busy.

## Practice before you need it

Practise getting each animal into its carrier calmly, with a treat and a blanket inside. Time how long it takes, and repeat the drill until the animal goes in without a struggle. Walk the route to a safe place with the animal on a lead, so that you know where the hazards are before you need to move quickly.

## When it fails

If an animal stops eating, vomits repeatedly, or shows bleeding or breathing trouble, separate it from others and keep it warm and quiet. Wash your hands after every contact. Seek help from a local veterinary service or a trained volunteer as soon as one is reachable.

**Warning:** do not leave pets behind in an evacuation. Take them if you can, or make arrangements for their care with a neighbour or a shelter that accepts animals. Never release a pet into the wild, because it will struggle to survive and may spread disease.

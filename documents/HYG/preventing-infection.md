# Preventing Infection

In a crisis, infection spreads quickly because people are crowded, water is short and medical care is limited. Simple habits protect the household, and they work best when everyone follows them without exception. One person who skips hand washing can start a chain of infection that reaches the whole group within days.

## Why it matters

Most infections that spread in shelters follow the same route: germs leave an infected person in faeces, breath or fluid, travel on hands, objects or food, and enter a new person through the mouth, nose, eyes or a wound. Each step can be interrupted with a simple habit. Breaking the chain early keeps a single case from becoming an outbreak, and it protects the most vulnerable people, including babies, pregnant women and the elderly.

## Equipment and materials

- Soap, ash or a clean cloth for hand washing, and a basin with a steady water supply.
- Covered containers for drinking water, and a clean ladle or cup kept for that purpose only.
- Bleach or another approved disinfectant, diluted and labelled.
- Gloves, bags and a bucket with a lid for handling vomit and faeces.
- Clean dressings and water-resistant tape for wounds.

## Core habits

1. Wash hands at the right times. See Washing Hands Without Running Water.
2. Keep food covered and cook it thoroughly until it is steaming hot all the way through.
3. Use clean water for drinking and for cleaning wounds, and store it in covered containers.
4. Keep toilets covered and clean. See Latrines and Waste.

## Step-by-step in detail

Set up the household so that the habits are easy. Place a wash station beside the latrine and another beside the food area. Store drinking water in a covered container at a fixed spot, with a clean cup that is never dipped into the store. Cook food in one batch where possible, and reheat leftovers until they are steaming throughout. When a person is ill, assign one adult to care for them, and make that adult the only one who handles the sick person's bowls, cloths and bedding.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Crowded shelters:** keep sleeping spaces at least an arm's length apart where possible, and separate the unwell from the rest.
- **Hot weather:** food spoils quickly, so cook and eat fresh meals and discard anything that smells off.
- **Cold weather:** people huddle together, so ventilate sleeping areas daily even when it is cold.
- **Outbreaks in children:** check the water and food supplies first, and keep children's cups and toys apart.

## Keep the air clean

- Cough or sneeze into the elbow, not into the hands.
- Keep sleeping areas ventilated, opening windows and doors each day.
- Separate people who are unwell, when you can, and give them a clear sleeping space.

## Keep wounds clean

Clean and cover every cut, scratch and burn, even small ones. Germs enter through broken skin, and a wound that looks minor can become serious within a day or two.

## Protect the sick

- Give the sick person their own cup, plate and cloth, and label them.
- Wash their clothes and bedding in hot water, and dry them in sun.
- Use gloves when caring for someone with diarrhoea or vomiting, and wash your hands afterwards.
- Clean up vomit and faeces at once, using disinfectant on the surface.

## Watch for spread

If several people become ill with the same symptoms, tell the group, check the water and food, and seek help. A cluster of illness often means a shared source, such as a contaminated well or a spoiled food store.

## Checklist

- Hand washing before food and after toilets or sick care.
- Food covered and cooked thoroughly.
- Drinking water covered and kept separate.
- Wounds cleaned and dressed each day.
- Unwell people sleeping apart, with their own utensils.

## Common mistakes

- Sharing cups and plates with a sick person.
- Relying on one hand wash a day, instead of at each key moment.
- Leaving food uncovered near the latrine or the rubbish.
- Ignoring a child's fever or refusal to drink.

## Practice before you need it

Run a short drill with the household on one ordinary day. Ask everyone to show the hand washing steps, the food covering routine and how they would move a sick person to a separate sleeping space. Correct any gaps while there is time, and repeat the drill each month so that the habits are held rather than remembered once.

## When it fails

If an infection spreads despite these habits, increase hand washing and cleaning, and tell the health worker or group leader. Keep a simple record of who is ill, what the symptoms are and when they started. This helps the health team find the cause quickly.

**Warning:** infections in children and older people can become serious quickly, and diarrhoea can cause dangerous dehydration within hours. Watch them closely and seek help at once if they stop drinking or become drowsy.

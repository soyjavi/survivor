# Body Washing with Little Water

Full bathing is not always possible during a crisis. Skin care still matters, because dirt, sweat and wounds lead to infection, sores and rashes. A careful sponge bath with about two litres of water keeps the body clean and comfortable, and it takes only a few minutes once the routine is familiar.

## Why it matters

Skin is the body's first barrier against infection. When sweat, dust and urine stay on it for days, bacteria multiply, and cracked skin gives them a route into the body. Children and older people are the most affected, because their skin is thinner or slower to heal. A small, regular wash keeps this risk low even when bathing facilities are gone and keeps people comfortable.

## Equipment and materials

- About two litres of clean water for each wash, measured in a marked jug.
- One basin used for washing only, and a second bowl for rinsing cloths.
- Two clean cloths for each person, and one towel kept only for drying.
- A small piece of mild soap kept in a dry dish between uses.
- Clean clothes, folded and ready on a dry surface.

## Sponge bath

Work from clean to dirty, so that you never carry dirt back onto skin you have already washed.

1. Use about two litres of water. Warm water is more comfortable, but cool water works too.
2. Wet a cloth, wring it out well, and wash the body in this order: face, neck, arms, chest, back, legs, feet, and finally the genitals.
3. Turn the cloth to a clean part for each area. A dirty corner should never touch a clean patch of skin.
4. Dry the skin fully before dressing, paying special attention to the armpits, under the breasts, the groin and the skin between the toes.

Damp skin in folds is the most common cause of rashes in people who cannot bathe often.

## Step-by-step in detail

Before you start, close the door or screen the bed, and explain what you are about to do. Place the basin, jug and towels within reach, so that the person is not left uncovered for long. Work in short sections, uncovering one part of the body at a time and covering it again when you move on. Use light strokes, and avoid rubbing hard over skin that is red or broken.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Cold weather:** warm the water, work quickly, and cover each area as soon as it is dry. Wash the upper body first, and leave the legs for a second session if the person is weak.
- **Hot weather:** wash more often, use cooler water, and dry the skin in the shade so that it does not stiffen.
- **Illness:** use separate cloths for the sick person, and wash that person last.
- **Young children and older adults:** support the head and shoulders, and keep the room warm.

## Hair

Use a damp cloth or a small amount of water and soap. Rinse with about one cup of water, pouring slowly over the head and catching the run-off in a bowl. Wash hair less often when water is short, and keep it tied back so that it does not collect dust and lice.

## Feet

Wash feet every day and dry them thoroughly, especially between the toes. Damp feet encourage fungus and blisters, and small cuts on wet feet heal slowly. Check the soles for stones, splinters and new sores.

## Skin care

- Moisturise dry skin with oil or cream, particularly on the hands, elbows and heels.
- Treat any rash, sore or wound at once, keeping it clean, dry and covered.
- Look at the skin every few days for redness, swelling or pus.

## Clean clothes

Change into clean clothes after washing, and wash soiled clothes as soon as you can. Clothes that stay wet against the skin cause irritation. See Laundry Without Power for simple hand-washing methods.

## Checklist

- Two litres of water set aside for washing.
- A clean cloth for each person, and a second cloth for the face.
- A dry towel or cloth kept only for drying.
- A clean set of clothes ready to put on.

## Common mistakes

- Washing the genitals first, then moving to other areas.
- Using the same part of the cloth for the whole body.
- Leaving skin folds damp after washing.
- Washing in a water source that others use for drinking.

## Practice before you need it

Run a practice wash at home while there is still water and time. Time how long a full sponge bath takes, and note which step uses the most water. Adjust the jug size or the number of cloths until the routine fits the daily water budget.

## When it fails

If a rash spreads, a sore smells, or a child develops a fever after a wash, stop using that cloth and that water. Use a fresh cloth, keep the area dry and seek care. Do not scrub an open sore.

**Warning:** do not wash the body in a water source that others drink from, and do not leave wet skin uncovered in cold weather. Cold wet skin loses heat quickly and can lead to hypothermia.

# Food After a Flood

Floodwater can contaminate food, packaging and containers with mud, sewage and chemicals. Throw away anything that touched floodwater, unless it is sealed, undamaged and cleaned properly. Many losses after a flood happen not because the food was ruined by water, but because it was eaten without checking.

## Why it matters

Floodwater carries sewage, fuel, pesticides and whatever the flood has picked up from roads and yards. Food that has sat in it can carry those contaminants into the body even when it looks clean. A flooded store or pantry also tempts people to salvage too much, and the risk of illness then spreads across the whole household rather than one meal. Sorting carefully protects health and makes the food you do keep safe to rely on.

## Equipment and materials

- Rubber gloves, waterproof boots and a face mask for sorting.
- Clean buckets, hot water and unscented detergent.
- Household bleach, at a strength suitable for disinfecting surfaces.
- Heavy plastic bags with ties, for removing discarded food.
- Labels and a marker for each cleaned batch.

Keep the bleach solution freshly made each day. Bleach loses strength when it is stored and diluted, so a solution left for several days will clean less well than the one you mixed at the start.

## Assess before you touch

Wear gloves and sturdy footwear when handling flood-damaged food. Sort items into three piles: keep, clean and discard. Work outside or in a well-ventilated space, and keep children away from the pile.

> @image Flooded pantry shelf with cans on the floor and mud on the lower shelves

Start with the items that are easiest to judge: anything soft, swollen or leaking goes straight into the discard pile. Leave the cans and jars for a second pass, when you have a clearer idea of how much was affected.

## Discard

- Any food in cardboard, paper or fabric packaging that was wet.
- Fresh produce, meat, fish, eggs and dairy that were in floodwater or warm for hours.
- Jars with lids that are loose, rusted or bulging.
- Food with a broken seal, dented cans with seams damaged, or anything that smells off.

## Keep, after cleaning

- Undamaged cans: wash the outside with clean water, then with a bleach solution (one part bleach to ten parts water). Rinse, dry and label them.
- Sealed glass jars: check the seal. Heat-treated food with a good seal can be kept.
- Dry goods in plastic or metal containers that stayed sealed.

Open a sample from each batch and check the smell, colour and texture before you rely on it.

> @image Cleaned cans drying upside down on a wire rack in a sunny yard, labelled with masking tape

## Adjusting for conditions

Muddy water is harder to clean from ridged cans and rough seams, so use a stiff brush and a second rinse. Cold weather slows drying, so move cleaned cans indoors to a dry room before labelling and storing them. If the water came from sewage or a chemical spill, discard anything that was not sealed in glass or metal, and be more cautious with jars.

## Clean containers

Wash storage containers with hot soapy water, then disinfect with a bleach solution before reuse. Let them dry fully in the air before filling them.

## Common mistakes

- **Keeping wet packaging:** water wicks into cardboard and carries contamination inside.
- **Trusting a clean-looking jar:** a bad seal is not always visible.
- **Cleaning only the top of a can:** the seam and lid also need washing and disinfecting.
- **Eating the first bite to test:** some contaminants are not detectable by taste.

## Checklist

- [ ] Gloves and boots are on before sorting starts.
- [ ] Each item has been checked for seal, dents and smell.
- [ ] Cans have been washed and disinfected.
- [ ] Discarded food is sealed in bags and removed from the home.

## Troubleshooting

If a can looks clean but the label has come away, or the metal is pitted, discard it. If a jar lid is slightly raised, do not use it. If you find mud inside a sealed container, the seal has failed. If a batch of cleaned cans later swells or leaks, discard the whole batch and check the others as well.

## Practice before you need it

Know where your store is kept, how high the flood line reaches, and where you would move stock to in a hurry. Keep a list of what is stored, and keep spare bags and gloves in the same place. A simple plan to move the most important stock to a higher shelf can save many meals.

## When it fails

If you cannot tell whether a can has been compromised, discard it. If a container smells sour after cleaning, do not reuse it for food. If you develop vomiting or diarrhoea after eating flood-affected food, seek medical advice.

**Warning:** when in doubt, throw it out. Food poisoning in a disaster can be serious, and medical care may be far away. Never eat food from a bulging, leaking or rusted container.

# Drying Fruit at Home

Dried fruit keeps for many months without power and is light to carry. Drying removes enough water that microbes cannot grow, and the sugar that remains concentrates the flavour. A small harvest dried well is worth more than a large one left to rot.

## Why it matters

Fruit spoils within days when it is fresh and warm, but the same fruit can be kept through winter once it is dry. Dried fruit gives energy, sugar and some vitamins, and it sweetens porridge, bread and stews without needing sugar from a shop. It also turns a short harvest into food for months, which is why drying is one of the first skills people reach for after a flood or a failed harvest.

## Materials

- Clean racks or trays with food-safe mesh.
- Fine insect mesh to cover the trays.
- A sharp knife and a cutting board.
- Lemon juice or a weak salt solution for pre-treatment.
- Airtight containers for storage.

A simple test for the tray mesh is that it must not sag under the weight of the slices, since sagging makes the middle layers thicker and slower to dry.

## Choose and prepare the fruit

- Use ripe, firm fruit without bruises or rot.
- Wash it and cut it into even slices, about 5 mm thick. Even pieces dry at the same rate.
- Remove stones, cores and any damaged parts before slicing.
- Some fruit, such as apple or apricot, darkens. Dip the slices in lemon juice or a weak salt solution to slow this.

> @image Sliced apples and apricots laid out in a single layer on a clean wooden tray

Work through the harvest in batches of the same size and ripeness. Mixed batches dry unevenly, and the thinnest pieces end up brittle while the thickest stay damp in the middle.

![Side-by-side trays split by line-dashed; left even slices, right thin and thick mixed with caution; labels Even batch, Mixed batch; uniform slices finish together and mixed slices leave damp centres. NEEDS: tray, fruit-slice](assets/drying-fruit-at-home-2.svg)

## Methods

- **Sun drying:** place the slices on a clean rack or mesh in full sun, covered with fine mesh against insects. Dry for three to five days, bringing the trays in at night.
- **Shade drying:** use the same setup in a warm, breezy, shaded place. It takes longer but keeps more colour.
- **Low oven or stove:** dry at the lowest heat with the door slightly open, for several hours.

## Adjusting for conditions

Hot, dry, breezy days are the best for sun drying. Humid weather slows the process and invites mould, so move the trays into a dry, warm room with a fan if the air is damp. Fruit with high sugar, such as figs and dates, dries faster and sticks more. Cover it more often and check it sooner.

## Check when it is dry

Dried fruit should feel leathery but not sticky. Cut a piece: no moisture should show. Squeeze a handful; if the pieces stick together or release juice, they need more time.

## Store it

1. Condition the fruit by sealing it in a container for a few days. Check for moisture. If you see condensation, dry it again.
2. Store it in airtight containers in a cool, dark, dry place.
3. Check every few weeks. Mould or a sour smell means discard.

Label each container with the fruit and the date. Use the oldest batch first, the same rule that applies to every stored food.

## Common mistakes

- **Slices too thick:** the centre stays wet and spoils.
- **Drying in damp weather:** the fruit takes too long and may mould.
- **Storing too early:** fruit that is not fully dry grows mould in the jar.
- **Leaving trays out overnight:** dew and insects get into the fruit.

## Checklist

- [ ] Fruit is ripe, sound and evenly sliced.
- [ ] Trays are clean and covered with mesh.
- [ ] Fruit is dry before it goes into storage.
- [ ] Containers are dry, sealed and labelled with the date.

## Troubleshooting

If the outside of the slices is hard but the middle is soft, the heat was too high. Lower the heat or the tray load and continue drying. If the fruit is dark and bitter, it was left in direct sun too long or dried at too high a heat. Fruit that darkens during drying was probably not dipped in lemon juice or salt solution soon enough after cutting.

## Practice before you need it

Dry one small tray of a fruit you already know in a normal week. Note the day it was ready, the weight before and after, and how it tasted on day three of storage. A small practice batch shows you the timing for your climate before a large harvest depends on it.

## When it fails

If the fruit grows mould during conditioning, discard the batch and dry the next one for longer. If it is sticky after several days, move it to a warmer, drier place or finish it in a low oven. Fruit that is sour, fermented or fizzing must be thrown away.

**Warning:** never dry fruit in a room with a charcoal stove, and keep it away from smoke and fumes. Discard any fruit with mould, sour smell or insects.

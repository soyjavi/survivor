# Acids for Preservation

Many spoilage microbes and most pathogenic bacteria struggle to grow below a certain pH. Acidity therefore protects food by making the environment hostile to growth, not by killing everything outright. The relevant measure is pH, a logarithmic scale where lower numbers mean more acid. Foods with a pH of 4.6 or below are considered acidic enough to resist most dangerous bacteria, when processed correctly.

## How Acidity Protects Food

Acid changes the environment in several ways at once. It disrupts the membranes of microbes, slows their enzymes, and makes it harder for them to take in nutrients. The effect depends on the strength of the acid, the amount present, and the salt, sugar and water content of the food. A weakly acidic food can still spoil quickly if it is warm, moist and low in other protection.

Natural acids include acetic acid from fermented liquids, citric acid from fruit, and lactic acid from fermenting vegetables. Each has a different strength and flavour, but the principle is the same.

1. Choose an acid source with a known and stable strength.
2. Measure the acidity of the final mixture, not just the ingredient.
3. Keep the acid evenly distributed so no pocket remains weaker than the rest.

## Testing Acidity

Simple pH test strips give a practical reading. Dip a strip briefly in a cooled sample of the liquid and compare the colour to the chart. Accuracy varies, so choose strips that cover the 4 to 5 range with fine gradations. Digital meters are more precise but need calibration with buffer solutions.

- Test after the food has cooled to room temperature, since heat can affect readings.
- Test several samples from the same batch, because acid may not mix evenly.
- Record the reading and the date on the container.

## Using Vinegar Safely

Vinegar in common use is typically five percent acetic acid. It is suitable for pickling only when its strength is known and when the mixture reaches the required proportion. Do not dilute vinegar with additional water unless a tested recipe calls for it, because the balance changes the pH.

1. Use the same vinegar strength throughout a batch.
2. Bring acidic liquids to the boil before pouring them over food, where the method requires it.
3. Keep food fully submerged so that no surface is exposed to air.
4. Store preserved items in cool, dark places and label them clearly.

## Limits of Acid Preservation

Acid does not sterilise food. Spores of some bacteria can survive acidic conditions and germinate later if the environment becomes less acidic. Low-acid foods such as vegetables, meat and fish cannot be made safe by acid alone and need proper heat processing or other methods. Acid also changes texture and flavour, so over-acidifying spoils the taste.

## Acidity in Fermented Liquids

Acidity often develops naturally in liquids that have been left to ferment for a period. A good fermented liquid smells sharp but clean, and it clears over time as solids settle. Liquids that smell of rot, have fuzzy growth on the surface, or develop a foul odour should be discarded, since they have moved beyond the controlled process into spoilage.

The mother, a cloudy film that forms on the surface of vinegar, is harmless in itself and indicates an active culture. It can be removed, but it should not be mistaken for mould. Mould is fuzzy, coloured and often spreads across the surface, while a healthy mother is smooth, pale and rubbery.

## Acid and Food Texture

Acid changes texture as well as flavour. Vegetables steeped in acid become firmer, while meat and fish can become tough if left too long. Keep soaking times short for delicate foods and check them regularly. A quick taste test is useful for flavour, but never rely on taste to judge safety.

## Keeping Acid Stocks Stable

Store acid stocks in dark glass or food-grade plastic, tightly capped, in a cool place. Light and heat cause gradual loss of strength and can change flavour. Use a clean spoon or pipette each time you take some out, and avoid returning used liquid to the main store, since it can introduce contamination.

**Warning:** Never assume that a sour smell or taste means a product is safe. Acid can mask spoilage, and some dangerous organisms tolerate low pH. Do not use acid preservation as a substitute for heat processing on low-acid foods. Discard any jar that bulges, spurts when opened, shows cloudiness or mould, or smells off, and do not taste suspect contents.

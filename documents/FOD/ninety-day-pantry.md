# Ninety-Day Pantry

A pantry that lasts ninety days lets you eat for three months without shops or cooking gas. Build it slowly, from the food you already eat, so the stock is used and rotated rather than forgotten. A pantry that nobody eats from becomes a pantry of waste.

![Shelf plan: table-grid with three rows, barrel and bag on the low row, tin in the middle, jar on the high row; labels Grains low, Cans middle, Dry goods high; heavy stock sits low so shelving stays stable.](assets/ninety-day-pantry-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Ninety days is a useful target because it covers most disruptions, from a long power cut to a bad harvest, without requiring a farm. It also gives a clear unit of measurement. Instead of wondering whether there is enough, you can ask how many days of food are on the shelf. A pantry sized this way is large enough to matter and small enough that a household can rotate it without special equipment.

## Core items (per adult, per 90 days)

- **Grains:** about 25 kg of rice, pasta, oats or flour.
- **Legumes:** about 8 kg of dried beans, lentils or chickpeas.
- **Canned fish and meat:** about 30 cans or packs.
- **Canned vegetables and fruit:** about 40 cans or jars.
- **Cooking oil:** about 4 litres.
- **Sugar and salt:** about 3 kg of sugar, and salt for cooking.
- **Dried fruit and nuts:** about 3 kg in total.
- **Powdered milk:** about 2 kg.
- **Tea, herbs and spices:** enough for your habits.

Adjust these numbers to the people you feed and how much they eat. Children, older people and people doing heavy work may need more.

## Equipment and materials

- Shelving that keeps the heaviest items low and away from the floor.
- Airtight containers for opened food, in glass, metal or thick plastic.
- A stock list and a marker for writing purchase dates.
- A small thermometer to check the storage room's temperature.

## Work out your own list

1. Write down what your household eats in a normal week.
2. Multiply each item by the number of weeks you want to cover.
3. Compare the result with the core list above and fill the gaps.
4. Add a small reserve of water purification tablets or a clean container for water.

Start the calculation with the items you already buy most often. Those are the items most likely to be used and most useful to keep at home. Only then add the less familiar foods that give variety.

## Storage rules

1. Keep the pantry cool, dry and dark.
2. Keep food off the floor and away from damp walls.
3. Use the oldest stock first, and write purchase dates on each pack.
4. Move opened food into sealed containers at once.
5. Check the pantry every month for damage, swollen cans and insects.

![Stock rotation: row of tin elements, arrow-straight from back to front, step-1 new at the back, oldest at the front marked do; labels New, Oldest, Use first; rotation keeps the oldest stock used first.](assets/ninety-day-pantry-3.svg)

## Adjusting for conditions

In a hot house, the pantry should be on the coolest wall, and the oil and dried fruit should be kept in a cooler place than the grains. In a damp climate, check the walls and the floor for moisture, and raise the shelves higher. Very large amounts of stock change the room's temperature, so spread the stock across more than one shelf or room where possible.

## Build the stock

- Add one or two items each time you shop.
- Keep a single stock list on the inside of a cupboard door.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying a large amount at once:** the first items may expire before they are used.
- **Ignoring the rotation rule:** the front of the shelf keeps the oldest items, which spoil first.
- **Storing food near heat or pipes:** temperature changes shorten the shelf life.
- **Forgetting cooking fuel:** the food is useless without a safe way to cook it.

## Troubleshooting

If a stock item seems to disappear faster than expected, check the containers for small holes and the shelves for crumbs. If cans are rusting on the bottom of the stack, move them to a drier shelf and check the seams. If one category runs short early, shift the plan toward grains and legumes, which carry the most energy for their weight.

## Practice before you need it

Live on the pantry for one week in a normal month, using only stored food. Note which meals are hard to make, which items run out first and how much fuel the cooking takes. The result tells you where the list is too small, and it proves the stock works before you need it.

## When it fails

If the pantry falls short, reduce the daily portions and use the most filling foods first, such as grains and legumes. If an item is damaged, remove it and check the rest of its shelf for the same damage.

**Warning:** throw away any damaged, swollen or leaking product. Do not eat food from a can that is bulging or rusted at the seam.

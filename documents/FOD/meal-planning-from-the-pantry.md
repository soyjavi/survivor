# Meal Planning from the Pantry

A pantry is only useful if you can turn it into meals. Plan around what is stored, use the perishable items first, and repeat simple meals. A short plan cuts waste, saves fuel and reduces the stress of deciding what to cook each day.

![Weekly grid: table-grid with table-header across seven days, rows Staples, Proteins, Vegetables, do mark in each cell, caution on any empty cell; every day fills all three slots, so gaps show at a glance.](assets/meal-planning-from-the-pantry-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Without a plan, a pantry gets used at random. Perishable food is left to spoil while dry goods are opened first, and a household ends up with a shelf of half-used packets and nothing to cook. A written plan makes the stock visible, tells you what to cook first and shows how many meals are left. It also means that the same skills and fuel go into a few repeated dishes that everyone knows how to make.

## Build a base

Every meal needs three parts:

- **Staple:** rice, pasta, bread, potatoes or porridge oats.
- **Protein:** beans, lentils, canned fish or meat, eggs.
- **Vegetable or fruit:** fresh, canned, dried or fermented.

Balance matters more than variety. A meal with all three parts keeps people full and healthy for much longer than a meal that is only a staple.

## Equipment and materials

- A notebook or a sheet of paper and a pen, pinned where the household cooks.
- A list of the stock, with quantities and dates, kept up to date.
- Lidded pots for cooking, sized for the household.
- A marker to highlight perishable items on the list.

## Plan the week

1. List the stock and mark the perishable items.
2. Plan the first two days around the perishables.
3. Use the same staples across the week to reduce waste.
4. Write each day's meals on a list and keep it visible.

Give each day one main dish and one simple accompaniment. This keeps the cooking routine short and makes it easier to see whether protein and vegetables are running low.

## Example meals

- **Bean stew:** beans, onion, dried vegetables, a little oil, salt.
- **Rice with fish:** rice, canned fish, fermented vegetables on the side.
- **Porridge:** oats cooked with dried fruit and a pinch of salt.
- **Lentil soup:** lentils, carrots, herbs, salt.

## Save effort

- Cook once for two meals, and store the rest safely.
- Soak beans and grains before cooking to save fuel.
- Batch-cook a large pot of stew and reheat it in portions.

## Adjusting for conditions

When fuel is scarce, favour dishes that cook slowly on a low heat, such as lentils and beans, and keep quick dishes such as porridge for days when the fire is hot. In hot weather, plan cold dishes such as fermented vegetables with rice, and cook early in the day. When fresh food arrives after a delivery or a harvest, move it to the first two days of the plan.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the least perishable items:** fresh food spoils while the pantry sits untouched.
- **Repeating one meal every day:** people stop eating it and waste the stock.
- **Forgetting the protein:** meals without it leave people hungry and weak.
- **Not writing the plan down:** the stock is then used at random.

## Checklist

- [ ] Stock is listed and perishables are marked.
- [ ] Each meal has a staple, a protein and a vegetable or fruit.
- [ ] The plan starts with the most perishable food.
- [ ] The weekly list is posted where everyone can see it.

## Troubleshooting

If the same dish keeps failing, check the cooking method before blaming the recipe. Beans that stay hard usually need longer soaking or less salt in the early stages. If a meal is too thin, add another staple, not more water. If the household is tired of one dish, change the vegetables or the spices and keep the staples the same, so the stock still rotates.

## Practice before you need it

Plan one full week of meals in a normal week, and cook from the plan on a few days. Note which dishes the household enjoyed, how long each took and where the stock ran short. This test shows gaps that a list alone never reveals.

## When it fails

If an ingredient runs out, swap in another from the same group, such as beans for lentils or dried fruit for fresh. If the plan falls behind, reduce the number of cooked meals and use the stock to make soup, which stretches food further.

**Warning:** check every item before cooking, and discard anything that is spoiled. See Food Spoilage and Safety for the rules on checking stored food.

# Smoking Fish Safely

Smoking preserves fish by combining three effects: smoke compounds that slow surface microbes, drying that reduces water available to bacteria, and heat or time that lowers bacterial load. Each effect is weaker than people assume on its own. Safety depends on controlling temperature and salt, because smoke alone does not make fish safe. Fish should be treated as a perishable food from catch to consumption.

## Preparing the Fish

Start with the freshest fish available, handled cold from the moment it is caught. Remove the guts and clean the cavity thoroughly. Split the fish or cut fillets to a consistent thickness so they dry and cook evenly.

1. Keep fish chilled in ice or cold water until processing.
2. Rinse in clean water and let excess drain.
3. Apply salt, usually as a dry cure or a brine, following a tested ratio.
4. Rest the fish cold for the time required by your method, typically several hours or overnight.

## Cold Smoking

Cold smoking keeps fish below about 30 degrees Celsius so it stays raw in texture. It preserves flavour and drying but does not cook the flesh, so it does not reliably kill parasites or bacteria. For this reason, cold-smoked fish must be salted thoroughly and kept cold afterwards, and it should be eaten cooked when possible.

- Use a smoke source placed away from the fish, with a chimney or pipe carrying smoke in.
- Keep the chamber cool and smoke gently for many hours.
- Dry the surface until it feels tacky before smoking begins, which helps the smoke bond.

## Hot Smoking

Hot smoking cooks the fish while it smokes. The target internal temperature is around 63 degrees Celsius, held long enough to make the flesh firm and flake. Hot-smoked fish keeps better than cold-smoked fish and is ready to eat, though it still spoils in warm conditions.

1. Preheat the chamber so smoke is present but not billowing.
2. Place fish skin side down on the racks with space between pieces.
3. Hold the chamber within your target range and check the thickest part of the fish with a probe thermometer.
4. Continue until the flesh is opaque and flakes under light pressure.

## After Smoking

Let the fish cool at room temperature only briefly, then refrigerate or freeze it. Store smoked fish in clean, covered containers. Eat hot-smoked fish within a few days when kept chilled. Discard any batch that has been left out for a long period in warm weather.

## Fuel and Smoke Quality

Use dry, clean hardwood such as oak, beech or alder. Softwoods, resinous woods, treated timber, painted wood and plastic produce bitter, sooty or toxic smoke and must be avoided. Chips or sawdust burn quickly and need steady feeding, while larger chunks hold a slow, even burn. Keep the fire small, since heavy smoke clouds the fish and deposits tar.

The smoke should look thin and bluish rather than thick and white. Thick smoke makes the surface of the fish dark and bitter, and it can leave a film that traps dirt. Adjust airflow to maintain a gentle, steady smoke, and avoid letting the fire flare up or go out completely, since both change the temperature inside the chamber.

## Keeping Records

Write down the fish type, its weight, the salt and time used, the smoking temperature and the total duration for each batch. These records help you repeat successful results and identify problems. If a batch tastes wrong or spoils quickly, the notes will show whether the salt level, temperature or handling was at fault.

## Checking Doneness

Judge hot-smoked fish by its flesh rather than its colour alone. The surface can look finished long before the centre has reached the target temperature. Press the thickest part gently with a clean finger; it should spring back and separate along the natural lines of the muscle. Cut into a test piece if you are unsure, and check that the centre is opaque all the way through.

## Storing Fish After Cooling

Once hot-smoked fish has cooled, wrap individual portions in clean paper or cloth and place them in a cool, dry container. Do not store them in airtight plastic while still warm, since trapped steam condenses and encourages growth. Keep portions separate so that a problem in one does not spread to the rest.

**Warning:** Never rely on smoke alone to preserve fish, and never treat cold-smoked fish as cooked. Botulism spores can survive in low-oxygen, low-salt, low-temperature conditions and produce a toxin that is not destroyed by smoking. Keep cold-smoked products strongly salted and chilled, and discard any fish that smells sour, looks slimy or has bulging packaging. When in doubt, throw it out.

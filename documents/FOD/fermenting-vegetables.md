# Fermenting Vegetables

Fermenting preserves vegetables by letting beneficial bacteria produce acid, which keeps spoilage organisms out. It needs salt, clean vegetables and patience, but no fuel or power. The method turns a seasonal glut into food that keeps for months and adds vitamins and flavour to plain meals.

![Cross-section: jar with leaf pieces packed low, line-solid brine level above them, weight on top, loosened lid, arrow-straight escaping upward; labels Brine, Weight, Loose lid, Gas out; the gas escape route keeps the jar from bursting. NEEDS: weight](assets/fermenting-vegetables-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Fermented vegetables are one of the few preserved foods that improve the diet without any cooking. They add sourness and texture to a plain meal of rice or beans, and they keep when fresh vegetables would be gone in a week. A household that ferments a surplus of cabbage, carrots or beans in autumn can eat greens in the middle of winter, when fresh produce is scarce.

## Ingredients

- Fresh vegetables such as cabbage, carrots, cucumber or beans.
- Non-iodised salt, about 2 per cent of the vegetable weight. For a kilo of cabbage, use about 20 grams.
- Clean water if the vegetables do not release enough juice.

Choose firm, unbruised vegetables. Damaged or soft pieces release their juice early and invite spoilage before the acid has formed.

## Equipment

- A clean glass jar or ceramic crock.
- A weight that fits inside the opening, such as a small clean stone in a bag or a smaller jar filled with water.
- A cloth or loose lid to cover the container.

> @image Clean glass jar, small stone in a cloth bag and a loose lid laid out on a kitchen counter

## Steps

1. Wash the vegetables and cut them into pieces.
2. Mix them with the salt and massage or pound them until they release juice.
3. Pack them tightly into a clean jar or crock, pressing down until the liquid covers them.
4. Weigh the vegetables down so they stay under the brine.
5. Cover loosely so gas can escape.
6. Keep at room temperature, away from sun, for one to four weeks, tasting from day five.

> @image Cabbage being pounded with salt in a wooden bowl until juice pools at the bottom

Pack in layers. Press each layer down with your fist or a wooden tamper, so that air pockets close and the juice rises above the top of the pieces. A fully packed jar ferments more evenly than a loosely filled one.

## Adjusting for conditions

Warm rooms ferment quickly, sometimes in under a week, while cold rooms can take a month or more. Taste the ferment every day from day five, and move it to a cool place once the flavour is right. Salt amounts stay the same in every season, but in a very hot room you may want the lower end of the one to four week range, since the ferment can turn too sour or too soft if left too long.

## Check the ferment

- Bubbles and a sour smell are normal.
- A white film on the surface is usually yeast and can be skimmed off.
- Fuzzy mould, pink or black colour, or a rotten smell means discard it.

## Common mistakes

- **Too little salt:** the ferment spoils before the acid forms.
- **Too much salt:** the vegetables stay soft and the ferment is slow.
- **Sealing the jar tight:** gas builds up and the jar can burst.
- **Leaving pieces above the liquid:** they grow mould.

## Checklist

- [ ] Salt is weighed, not guessed.
- [ ] Vegetables are fully covered by brine.
- [ ] The jar has room for gas to escape.
- [ ] The ferment is kept out of direct sun.

## Troubleshooting

A ferment that does not bubble within two or three days may be too cold, too salty or too dry. Move it to a warmer spot, check the brine level and add a little more salted water if needed. A ferment that smells sharply of vinegar has usually fermented too long or too warm. Use it in cooked dishes, where the flavour will mellow, and discard it if any mould appears.

## Practice before you need it

Ferment one small jar of a vegetable you already eat, in a normal week, before committing a large harvest. Keep notes on the room temperature, the day the flavour was right, and how the vegetables looked at the end. The next batch will be easier to judge.

## When it fails

If the brine is cloudy and smells pleasantly sour, the ferment is working. If it smells rotten or shows fuzzy mould, discard the whole batch and start again with cleaner equipment and more salt. If the vegetables float above the liquid, press them back under and add a little more salted water.

![Jar with line-dashed brine level, weight pressing vegetables below it, arrow-straight pressing down, caution above the line; labels Brine line, Weight, Press under; anything above the line is exposed to air. NEEDS: weight](assets/fermenting-vegetables-4.svg)

Once it tastes right, store the jars in a cool place. The ferment keeps for months.

**Warning:** keep all vegetables below the brine. Exposed pieces can spoil and carry mould into the batch. Discard any ferment that shows fuzzy mould, pink or black colour, or a rotten smell.

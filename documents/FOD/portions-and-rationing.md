# Portions and Rationing

Rationing divides the supply so it lasts as long as you need. The aim is to keep everyone healthy, not to make people go hungry. A fair, written plan prevents arguments over food and makes it easier to adjust when the supply changes or the number of people grows.

![Table: table-grid with table-header; columns Daily need, People, Days, arrow-straight to Daily ration; one total store becomes one daily ration. NEEDS: calorie-icon](assets/portions-and-rationing-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Without a plan, the supply is eaten in the first week and the household goes hungry in the last. A written ration also protects the people who cannot ask for more, such as young children and older relatives. Fair portions build trust, and trust makes a shortage easier to live through, because people accept limits that they can see are shared equally.

## Plan the calories

An adult needs roughly 2,000 kcal per day for light activity. Hard work needs more. Children, pregnant people and older people have their own needs, and these should be planned first rather than treated as an afterthought.

> @image Kitchen scale weighing dry rice into a small bowl beside a measuring cup

## Equipment and materials

- A kitchen scale, or a set of measuring cups and spoons that you use every time.
- A written stock list, with calories per item taken from the package labels.
- A portion bowl or plate for each person, marked with their name or colour.
- A notebook for the daily record of what was served.

Weigh the dry food before cooking rather than estimating by volume. Rice swells, beans double and porridge thickens, so a cupful can mean very different amounts from one meal to the next.

![Scale: bowl of dry rice on a weighing platform, arrow-straight to a second bowl with measured portion; labels Dry rice, Portion; weight, not volume, keeps portions consistent. NEEDS: kitchen-scale](assets/portions-and-rationing-2.svg)

## Calculate the supply

1. Add up the calories in what you have, using package labels.
2. Divide by the number of people.
3. Divide by the number of days you plan for.
4. Plan meals to match that daily amount.

![Worked example: step-box total store, arrow-straight, step-box divide by people, arrow-straight, step-box divide by days, arrow-straight to Daily ration; each step shows so the arithmetic can be checked.](assets/portions-and-rationing-3.svg)

Recalculate the plan after the first week. Actual use is almost always different from the plan, usually because cooking waste, spilt food and spoiled items were not counted at the start.

## Keep the basics in every meal

- Include a carbohydrate, a protein and some fat.
- Keep a daily amount of fruit or vegetables where possible, for vitamins.
- Give children and older people their portions first.

## Practical rules

- Eat at the same times each day.
- Give each person a bowl or plate, so portions are visible.
- Keep the stock in one place, and record it.

## Adjusting for conditions

In hot weather, people need more water and slightly more salt, but total calories may fall as appetite drops, so the ration can be trimmed a little without harm. In cold weather, the same people burn far more energy, so the calorie plan must be raised before the cold arrives. Heavy work, such as clearing debris or carrying water, adds calories for the people doing it, and the plan should reflect that openly.

## Common mistakes

- **Using guesswork:** portions drift upward without a measure, and the supply runs out early.
- **Forgetting the weight of the people:** a large adult needs more than a small one.
- **Changing the rules without telling people:** this creates distrust and disputes.
- **Forgetting the calories in cooking oil and sugar:** these add up quickly.

## Avoid mistakes

- Do not cut water to save food. Food needs water to digest.
- Do not hide the amounts from the group. Openness builds trust.
- Do not ration so hard that people cannot work or sleep.

## Checklist

- [ ] The total calories and days are written down.
- [ ] Children and older people are planned first.
- [ ] Each meal includes a carbohydrate, a protein and some fat.
- [ ] The plan is posted where everyone can see it.

## Troubleshooting

If the supply is falling faster than the plan, check the scale and the bowls first, since a heaped bowl is the most common cause of overuse. If one person keeps asking for more, check whether they are working harder or are unwell, rather than changing the whole plan. If a meal leaves people hungry an hour later, add more fat or a protein to the same portion instead of increasing the total.

## Practice before you need it

Run a three-day trial of the ration in a normal week, with the real scale and bowls. Record what was eaten, how people felt and where the arithmetic was off. A trial shows problems while the stakes are low, and it lets the household agree on the rules before they matter.

## When it fails

If the supply is running short faster than planned, reduce the portions for adults slightly before reducing those for children or older people. If someone is losing weight, becoming weak or confused, increase their portion and tell a medical professional as soon as possible.

Review the plan every few days, and change the portions if the supply or the needs change.

**Warning:** never ration water to the point of dehydration, and do not cut the portions of children, pregnant people or people with medical conditions without advice from a health professional.

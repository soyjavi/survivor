# Feeding Children and Older People

Children and older people are the first to suffer from poor food and water. They need food that is easy to eat and digest, and they need it on time. Small changes to meals, drinks and routine can prevent weakness, dehydration and illness before they become serious.

![Grid: table-grid with table-header; columns Children, Adults, Older people, rows Breakfast, Midday, Evening, bowl in each cell, step-box for protein sources; protein is spread across every group's meals, not added to one. NEEDS: protein-icon](assets/feeding-children-and-older-people-1.svg)

## Why it matters

A child who misses meals loses growth and energy quickly, and an older person who eats little may slip into confusion without anyone noticing. Both groups often cannot queue for food, carry water or cook for themselves, so the household has to build their needs into the plan from the start. Feeding them well is also one of the clearest signs that the household is coping.

## Children

- Children need regular meals, several small ones a day, rather than one large meal.
- Give them foods rich in protein and fat, such as beans, nuts, eggs and fish.
- Keep sugar and salt for cooking, and avoid giving them salty food when they are dehydrated.
- Breastfeeding mothers need more food and water. Keep them supplied first.

![Low table with bowl, person-sitting child and person-sitting adult beside it, arrow-both-ways between them; labels Porridge, Child, Adult; feeding is shared and slow, paced to the child. NEEDS: table, spoon](assets/feeding-children-and-older-people-2.svg)

Children grow in bursts, so their appetite changes from day to day. Offer the same food again rather than insisting on a new meal, and let them eat at their own pace. Mash or soften food for toddlers, and keep bowls and spoons for each child.

![Row of small bowl elements at low height on ground-line, person-sitting child at the end, leader to portion size; labels Small portions, Child height; portions are sized for small hands and small mouths. NEEDS: mat](assets/feeding-children-and-older-people-3.svg)

## Older people

- Older people may need softer food: soups, porridge and mashed beans.
- Many older people need regular medication. Keep it with the food so it is not forgotten.
- Watch for signs of poor intake: confusion, weakness and dark urine.

Older people often feel less thirsty than younger adults, so offer drinks even when they do not ask. Sit them upright when eating to reduce the risk of choking.

## Adjusting for conditions

In hot weather, children and older people lose water faster and need drinks more often, so plan an extra cup every few hours. In cold weather, warm meals matter more, and soups and porridge hold heat longer than dry foods. When water is scarce, give priority to the drinking needs of these groups before any other use of water.

## Practical steps

1. Ask about allergies and diets before cooking.
2. Serve small, frequent portions.
3. Offer water with every meal and between meals.
4. Keep food clean and warm, and keep infants' utensils separate.

## Common mistakes

- **Serving one large meal:** children and older people may not finish it, and they go hungry later.
- **Forgetting water:** dehydration builds quietly and affects both groups quickly.
- **Using the same bowl for everyone:** infant feeding tools must be cleaned after each use.
- **Ignoring medication schedules:** missed doses can be dangerous.

## Checklist

- [ ] Allergies and dietary needs are known.
- [ ] Each child and older person has a meal plan.
- [ ] Water is offered at each meal and between meals.
- [ ] Medication is stored with the food and given on time.

## Troubleshooting

If a child refuses food, check whether they are unwell before changing the menu. A child who is feverish or drowsy needs fluids and medical advice, not a new recipe. An older person who stops eating may be in pain, unable to chew or confused, so ask simple questions and watch for changes in behaviour. Keep a simple daily record of what each person ate and drank, so that changes are noticed early.

## Practice before you need it

Plan one day of meals for everyone in the household in a normal week, with soft versions of the food and a drink schedule. Practise cleaning infant feeding tools and checking medication times. A household that has rehearsed these routines will not have to invent them under stress.

## When it fails

If a child or older person refuses food for a day, offer small amounts of soft food and fluids and watch for dizziness or dark urine. If they become confused, very weak or stop passing urine, seek medical help at once.

**Warning:** do not give honey to babies under one year old, because it can carry bacteria that cause infant botulism. Seek urgent care for any child or older person with persistent vomiting, diarrhoea or confusion.

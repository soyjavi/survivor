# Food Spoilage and Safety

Bacteria grow fastest between about 5 °C and 60 °C. Keeping food out of that range, or cooking it through, is the core of food safety. Most food poisoning in a shortage comes from ordinary food left in warm conditions, not from exotic hazards, so the habits below matter more than any single product.

![Temperature scale: vertical line-solid with thermometer, threshold at 5 and at 60 degrees Celsius, bracket shading the zone between; labels Below, Danger zone, Cooked above; food is safest below the zone or cooked above it.](assets/food-spoilage-and-safety-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Food poisoning is one of the fastest ways for a household to lose its strength during a shortage. A single bout of vomiting or diarrhoea can dehydrate a child or an older person within a day, and it can take a healthy adult out of work for a week. Most of these illnesses are preventable with simple routines, which cost nothing but attention. Good habits protect the food you have, and they protect the people who would have to care for anyone who fell ill.

## The danger zone

- Keep cold food cold: below about 5 °C.
- Keep hot food hot: above about 60 °C.
- Do not leave perishable food in between for more than two hours. In hot weather, the limit is about one hour.

A simple rule is to ask how long the food has been out, not how it looks. Food can be harmful even when it appears fine.

## Equipment and materials

- A probe thermometer, or a thermometer that reads to at least 100 °C.
- A timer or a clock on the wall, used to log when food was cooked and when it was cooled.
- Clean cloths, separate cutting boards for raw and cooked food, and soap.
- Lidded containers for cooked food, and a clean space to cool it.

A thermometer is the only way to check food reliably. Judgement based on smell or appearance is useful for spotting spoilage, but it cannot tell you that a dish has stayed below the danger zone.

## Signs of spoilage

- A sour, rotten or unusual smell.
- Slime, mould or colour change.
- Bulging or leaking cans and packs.
- Foam or fizzing in liquids.

## Practical steps

1. Eat perishable food first: fresh meat, fish, dairy and cooked food.
2. Store dry goods in sealed containers.
3. Cook meat until it is steaming hot all the way through.
4. Reheat leftovers to steaming hot once, then eat them.
5. Wash hands before handling food and after using the toilet.

## Cool food quickly

Large pots of cooked food cool slowly at their centre, and that is where bacteria survive. Spread hot food into a shallow dish, stand the dish in cold water or a cool room, and stir it occasionally. Cover it once it is cool enough to stop steam from dripping back in.

## Adjusting for conditions

Hot seasons shorten every safe time in this guide, so reduce the limit to one hour in the heat. In cold seasons, food stays safe longer outdoors, but never leave it in a place where animals or pests can reach it. Cooking fuel is often used more heavily in the cold, which makes it tempting to reheat food several times. Reheat only the portion you will eat.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving cooked food on the table:** it spends hours in the danger zone.
- **Reheating several times:** each cycle lets bacteria grow again.
- **Using dirty hands or utensils:** contamination spreads from one dish to the next.
- **Judging by the date alone:** storage conditions matter more than printed dates.

## Checklist

- [ ] Perishable food is identified and eaten first.
- [ ] Cooked food is covered and not left out for long.
- [ ] Leftovers are reheated once, to steaming hot.
- [ ] Hands and surfaces are clean before cooking.

## Troubleshooting

If a dish tastes off but looks fine, do not eat it. If food has been out much longer than two hours and you cannot tell how long, treat it as unsafe. If a stew has been reheated several times and smells different, discard it. Keep a written note of each risky decision, since patterns in those notes show where the routine is failing.

## Practice before you need it

Walk through a day of cooking and storage in a normal week. Note when food is cooked, cooled, covered and reheated, and where each step happens. Hands, cloths and boards should be clean before the first dish, and the routine becomes automatic after a few days of practice.

## When in doubt

If you are not sure food is safe, throw it away. A lost meal is less costly than food poisoning in a crisis, when medical help may be far away. Keep a note of what was discarded so that the same risk is avoided next time.

## When it fails

If someone develops vomiting, diarrhoea or fever after eating, stop eating the suspect food, keep a sample if possible, drink fluids, and seek medical help if symptoms are severe or persist.

**Warning:** do not taste food to test it, because some dangerous toxins have no smell or taste. Never keep a container that is bulging, leaking or foaming, and discard any food that shows spoilage signs, even if only part of it looks bad.

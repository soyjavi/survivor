# Cooking Over a Wood Fire

A wood fire is the most dependable way to cook without gas, because wood is widely available and the fire can be built with simple tools. The key is to cook with coals, not flames. Flames blacken food, scorch pots and waste fuel, while a steady bed of coals gives even heat that you can control.

> @image Pot hung from a tripod over a wood fire and a grate over coals

## Why it matters

A fire that is built and managed well cooks several dishes at once, boils water for drinking and warms the people nearby. The same fire that heats a pot can also dry wet clothes and keep insects off stored food. Learning to run one fire well means that fuel is used only for the cooking that needs it, and a family can stretch a small pile of wood across many meals.

## Materials

- Small, dry wood for starting, and larger pieces for the bed of coals.
- A tripod, or two stones and a metal grate to hold the pot.
- A heavy pot with a lid, and a long-handled tool for moving coals.
- A bucket of water or a shovel of sand for putting the fire out.

Gloves made of leather or thick cloth protect your hands when you move hot pots or coals. A pair of tongs or a long stick lets you adjust the bed without leaning over the flames.

## Build the cooking fire

1. Burn a small fire until it forms a bed of hot coals. This usually takes 20 to 40 minutes.
2. Keep a supply of small, dry wood nearby, so you can feed the fire without disturbing the food.
3. Spread the coals in a layer, with more on one side for different heat levels.

![Top view: coals in three zones, line-dashed dividers, pan and pot-with-lid on the hot zone, food on medium and cool zones, arrow-both-ways across zones; labels Hot, Medium, Cool; moving food between zones controls heat without adding wood.](assets/cooking-over-a-wood-fire-1.svg)

Start with kindling, then add progressively thicker pieces as the flame takes hold. Wait until the flames have dropped and the wood glows orange under a grey ash skin before cooking. Coals that are still flaming will blacken the bottom of any pot.

> @image Hands feeding small sticks into a fire ring of stones, with a pile of kindling nearby

## Cook in a pot

- **Hang the pot** from a tripod over the fire, or set it on a grate on stones.
- **Set the pot on coals** directly, and add a few coals on the lid for even heat.
- **Keep the pot off the flames.** Direct flame scorches the bottom and cracks the seams of thin pots.

## Cook on a grill

Set a metal grate over the coals. Cook thin pieces first, then thicker pieces over lower heat. Turn food once, and avoid pressing it, because pressing squeezes out juices and makes smoke.

## Control the heat

- Move the pot to the cooler side to slow the cooking.
- Add small pieces of fuel rather than large logs.
- Wait for flames to die down before cooking delicate food.

Hold a hand above the coals at pot height and count how long you can keep it there. A few seconds means high heat, around five seconds means medium, and longer means low.

## Common mistakes

- **Cooking in the flames:** the outside burns before the inside is cooked.
- **Adding large logs:** the heat is uneven and hard to control.
- **Leaving the pot unattended:** food boils over or sticks to the base.
- **Using wet wood:** it smokes heavily and gives little heat.
- **Forgetting the wind:** a gust can blow sparks onto dry grass or onto the cook.

## Checklist

- [ ] The fire is outdoors, on bare ground or stones.
- [ ] Coals, not flames, are under the pot.
- [ ] Water or sand is ready before you light the fire.
- [ ] Someone watches the fire at all times.

## Troubleshooting

A fire that smokes without flame usually has wet or green wood in it. Move that wood to the edge to dry, and add dry kindling instead. A fire that dies after each meal has too few coals underneath. Build the bed deeper before you start cooking. Smoke that stings the eyes means the wind is blowing across the cook, so move the fire or shelter it with a stone wall.

## Practice before you need it

Light and cook on a fire at home, on a sunny day with no pressure to finish a meal. Time how long it takes to form a good bed of coals, practise moving pots on and off the heat, and learn how the fire behaves in wind and rain. Skills learned in calm conditions are the ones that work when the situation is difficult.

## When it fails

If the fire keeps going out, the wood is too damp or the coal bed is too thin. Split the wood smaller and build the bed again. If food is burning, lift the pot off the heat and move it to the cooler side.

**Warning:** wood smoke carries carbon monoxide in enclosed spaces. Cook outdoors only, keep the fire on a clear, flat area away from shelter and dry grass, and never cook with a fire under a roof or inside a room.

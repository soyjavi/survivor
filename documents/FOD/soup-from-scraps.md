# Soup from Scraps

Soup stretches a small amount of food and makes use of trimmings that would otherwise be wasted. It also adds water and warmth to the day, which matters when people are tired or cold. A pot that is always on the fire, fed with new scraps, becomes the backbone of a household's meals.

![Two panels joined by arrow-straight: pot-with-lid with leaf trimmings, then strained stock into bowl with solids left behind; labels Trimmings, Stock; solids are removed while flavour stays in the liquid. NEEDS: bone, strainer](assets/soup-from-scraps-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Scraps are the cheapest nutrition a household has, because they would otherwise be thrown away. A pot of soup turns a handful of peelings, a few beans and a little grain into a meal that fills the stomach and provides fluid. It is also a safe way to use older vegetables, as long as they are sound, and it spreads a small amount of protein across many bowls.

## Equipment and materials

- A large, lidded pot, and a second clean pot for the strained stock.
- A strainer and a cloth to catch the solids.
- A sharp knife and a chopping board for trimmings.
- A lidded container for storing scraps until they are used.

Keep a bowl of clean scraps on the side during the week, and cover it. The bowl should be emptied into the stock pot at least once a day, so that no scraps sit around long enough to spoil.

## Base stock

1. Save clean vegetable trimmings: onion skins, carrot ends, celery leaves, potato peelings.
2. Add bones from cooked meat or fish if you have them.
3. Cover with clean water and simmer for one to two hours.
4. Strain out the solids.

Keep the stock cool if you will not use it straight away, and use it within a day or two.

## Build the soup

- Add beans, lentils or grain for body and protein.
- Add any dried or fermented vegetables.
- Season with salt and dried herbs.
- Simmer until everything is soft and steaming hot.

![Layered pot-with-lid cutaway: water-drop stock at the base, beans and grain in the middle, leaf herbs on top, leader to each; labels Stock, Beans and grain, Herbs; stock is the base and herbs go in last. NEEDS: beans, grain](assets/soup-from-scraps-2.svg)

Add the grain or beans first, since they need the longest time to soften, and add the leafy herbs in the last few minutes. Fermented vegetables go in at the end, because their sourness fades with heat.

## Save fuel

- Keep a lidded pot on the heat and reuse the pot each day.
- Add new scraps to the stock each day to build flavour.

> @image Lidded pot of soup simmering gently on a small fire outdoors, with a wooden spoon resting on the rim

## Adjusting for conditions

In cold weather, make the soup thicker, with more beans or grain, since it gives warmth and energy for longer. In hot weather, make a lighter soup and eat it at room temperature within a few hours, rather than letting it sit warm in the sun. When water is scarce, make the stock from fewer pots of water and keep the solids for a second meal.

## Common mistakes

- **Saving scraps that are already spoiled:** the whole pot is ruined.
- **Leaving the stock to cool slowly:** it spends hours in the danger zone.
- **Adding too much salt early:** the soup becomes too salty as it reduces.
- **Using bones from unknown animals:** the stock may carry disease.

## Checklist

- [ ] Only clean, fresh trimmings are saved.
- [ ] Bones come from a healthy animal.
- [ ] The stock is strained and cooled quickly.
- [ ] The soup is reheated to steaming hot before serving.

## Troubleshooting

If the stock smells sour or turns cloudy and slimy, discard it and start again with a clean pot. If the soup tastes flat, add a little salt, a dried herb or a fermented vegetable. If it is too thin, add more beans or grain and simmer until thick.

## Practice before you need it

Make one batch of stock from scraps in a normal week, and keep a record of which trimmings were used and how the stock tasted the next day. A practice batch shows which scraps give the best flavour and how long the stock keeps in your kitchen.

## When it fails

If the soup is too salty, add more water and beans, not more food, and let it simmer again. If a soup has been left warm for hours, do not try to rescue it by adding fresh ingredients; discard it and start again.

**Warning:** boil the soup fully before serving, and do not keep it warm for hours without reheating. Do not use vegetables that are rotten, slimy or mouldy, or bones from an animal that was sick or found dead.

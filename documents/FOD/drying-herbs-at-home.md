# Drying Herbs at Home

Herbs keep their flavour and medicinal value far longer when most of their water is removed. Air drying needs no equipment beyond warmth, airflow and patience. The goal is to remove moisture faster than mould or rot can establish itself, so timing and conditions matter more than any special tool.

## Choosing and Preparing the Herbs

Harvest herbs in the late morning, after the dew has dried but before the afternoon heat drives off their oils. Cut healthy stems with no yellowing, insect damage or black spots. Woody herbs such as rosemary, thyme and sage dry well as whole stems. Soft herbs such as basil and mint are more fragile and often dry better when stripped into small leaf clusters.

1. Shake off dirt gently. Do not wash unless the leaves are visibly muddy.
2. If you must wash, pat each bunch dry with a clean cloth and let it rest for at least an hour.
3. Remove any damaged or discoloured leaves before drying begins.

## Air Drying Methods

Hanging is the classic approach. Tie small bundles, no thicker than a finger, with string and hang them upside down in a dry, dark, well-ventilated room. Light fades colour and reduces potency, so avoid direct sun. A warm, dry shelf works too. Lay the leaves in a single layer on a clean mesh screen or paper sheet and turn them every day.

- Keep the room between 20 and 30 degrees Celsius if possible.
- Aim for airflow from more than one direction, but avoid strong draughts that scatter leaves.
- Drying usually takes one to three weeks depending on humidity.
- Dense stems and thick leaves like sage or oregano need the longest.

## Testing for Dryness

Dry herbs crumble easily between the fingers and feel papery rather than limp. Stems should snap cleanly instead of bending. Leaves should not feel cool or damp when pressed.

1. Pick a stem and rub a few leaves between your palms.
2. If they powder with little effort, they are ready.
3. If a leaf folds without breaking, leave the bundle drying for several more days.
4. Check the underside of a stem for moisture before storing, since the thickest part dries last.

## Storage

Store dried herbs in clean, dry, airtight glass jars or tins. Strip leaves from stems once they are fully crisp, which keeps them crumbly and reduces the surface area for absorption. Label each container with the herb name and date. Keep jars away from heat, steam and light. Stored well, most herbs keep their quality for about a year, though they lose aroma gradually.

Inspect jars during the first two weeks. Condensation on the inside of the glass means the herbs were not dry enough and must be taken out and dried again.

## Common Problems

- **Mould** appears as fuzzy grey or white spots. Discard the affected material and improve airflow.
- **Browning** usually means the drying was too hot or too slow, or the herbs were exposed to sun.
- **Loss of flavour** often comes from drying too late in the season or storing in a bright place.

## Adjusting for Weather and Place

Humidity decides how long drying takes more than anything else. In a damp coastal climate, a bundle can take twice as long as the same herb in a dry inland room. When the weather turns wet for several days, bring the herbs indoors or move them to a warmer shelf. Never leave them outside overnight, because dew re-wets the leaves and restarts the problem.

Small spaces can work well if they are used carefully. A spare cupboard with the door left ajar, a loft that stays dry, or a corner of a kitchen away from the stove will all serve. Avoid rooms where cooking, washing or bathing produces steam, since moisture in the air slows drying dramatically and invites mould.

Some herbs need particular handling. Mint and lemon balm turn black quickly if they are not dried fast, so keep them in a warm, moving airflow and strip them early. Parsley dries more slowly and benefits from being laid flat on a screen. Bay leaves dry well on their own and can be spread on a tray for a few days without any tying.

## Tracking Progress

Keep a small notebook or write on the bundle tag the date you hung the herbs and the date you expect them to be ready. Check the bundles once a day, feeling the thickest stems and turning any that look damp. Note which herbs dried first, since this helps you plan next season's drying batches more accurately.

**Warning:** Discard any herb bundle that smells musty, sour or fermented, or shows fuzzy growth. Mould spores can cause respiratory irritation, and some moulds produce toxins that do not disappear when the herb is cooked. Never use herbs you are unsure about, and never burn dried herbs indoors.

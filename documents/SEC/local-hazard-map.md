# Local Hazard Map

A hazard map shows where danger is likely: rivers that flood, slopes that fall, woods that burn, roads that block. Make one before a crisis, keep it with your plans, and check it each season. A map made in a hurry is far less useful than one made calmly with the household.

## Why it matters

Most people know their own street well but have little sense of the wider area they would need to cross in an emergency. A map turns scattered memories into a single picture, so that the household can see which routes are at risk and which places offer safety. It also makes it easier to brief neighbours, relatives and helpers who have never been to your home.

![Neighbourhood sketch: map with river, slope, tree as three zones joined by bracket, waypoint-pin for home between; labels: river, slope, woodland, home; zones are judged one by one, not as general danger.](assets/local-hazard-map-2.svg)

## Make the map

1. Start with a paper map of the area around the home, at a scale that shows the streets within about two kilometres.
2. Mark the home, and the meeting points for the household and the street.
3. Mark the hazards you know: rivers and low ground, steep slopes, dry woodland, industrial sites, dams and main roads.
4. Mark the safe places: high ground, shelters, water sources and medical posts.
5. Use pencil first, so that you can correct the map as you learn more.

Walk the area at least once with the map in hand. Streets that look short on paper can be long in practice, and bridges or underpasses can be the only links between two safe zones.

![Paper map: waypoint-pin for home at centre, river, slope, tree zones, two line-solid routes leaving home with arrow-straight; labels: low ground, slope, woodland, route 1, route 2; each route must avoid every zone.](assets/local-hazard-map-3.svg)

## Use the map

- Plan evacuation routes that avoid the hazards, and mark a second route for each.
- Decide which hazards to prepare for first.
- Check the map against official risk information for your area, and treat any conflict as a reason to ask.

## Keep it current

- Update the map after any new event, flood, fire or building work.
- Copy the map and share it with the household and with trusted neighbours.
- Store one copy at home and one in the go bag, sealed in a plastic sleeve.

## Choose the priorities

Put the most likely and most serious hazards first. Floods, fires and storms are common in many places. Earthquakes and industrial accidents are less frequent but can be severe, and they deserve a plan even when they seem unlikely.

## Practice before you need it

1. Pick a route on the map and walk it with a timer.
2. Note any point where the route is narrow, flooded or overhung by trees or buildings.
3. Mark those points with a distinct symbol and write a short note beside each one.
4. Repeat the walk in the dark, once a year, to see what changes.

![Walking route: map, line-solid route, footprints, caution at narrow points, waypoint-pin at flooded dips, arrow-branch to second route; labels: narrow point, flooded dip, second route; which stretches need a note and where the second route begins.](assets/local-hazard-map-5.svg)

## Adjusting for conditions

Hazards change with the season. Rivers rise after heavy rain, dry woodland becomes a risk in hot months, and icy slopes can close a route in winter. Add a seasonal layer to the map, in a different colour, so that the household sees at a glance which risks apply now.

## Reading the terrain

Water runs downhill and collects in hollows, so an area that looks flat can still be a trap. Look for old flood marks on walls, the lines of debris left in fields after a previous event, and the places where the ground dips toward a stream. Slopes that have slipped before often show cracks, leaning fences or new gaps in the soil. Woods that have burned become hazardous again after heavy rain, when loose soil and fallen trees shift. Record these observations on the map, because they tell you more about the real risk than any single official line on a chart.

## Common mistakes

- Marking hazards from memory alone, without walking the area.
- Forgetting that low ground may flood faster than expected.
- Drawing only one evacuation route.
- Leaving the map at the office, where you cannot reach it at home.

## Checklist

- Home and meeting points are marked.
- Each main hazard has a symbol and a note.
- Two evacuation routes avoid the hazards.
- Copies are kept at home and in the go bag.

## When it fails

If a route on the map is blocked, use the second route, and mark the blockage on the map once it is safe to do so. If the map does not match what you see, trust what you see and update the map afterwards.

**Warning:** a hazard map is only a guide. It shows where risks are, not that they will not happen elsewhere.

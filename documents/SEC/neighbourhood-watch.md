# Neighbourhood Watch

Neighbours are often the first to help in a crisis, and a group that works together is safer than households alone. Start small, keep the system simple, and make sure everyone knows that the purpose is care, not control.

![Street map: map with several waypoint-pin for households, callout role labels, one central waypoint-pin; labels: household roles, meeting point; roles spread across the street so every block has a named contact. NEEDS: house](assets/neighbourhood-watch-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Emergency services may take time to arrive, and in widespread events they may be stretched thin. A street that knows who lives where, who needs help and who has which skills can respond within minutes. Trust built in calm times is what makes shared action possible when stress is high, so the group should be built before it is needed.

## Start

1. Meet the nearest households and list their names, phone numbers and any special needs, with their permission.
2. Agree on one or two contacts who keep the list up to date and hold a copy.
3. Choose a meeting point in the street or block, and check that it is safe to reach on foot.

Hold the first meeting in daylight, at a neutral location such as a shared garden or hall, and keep it short. Agree the first task before anyone leaves, so that the group has momentum.

## Share roles

Spread the work so that no single person is overloaded.

- Someone checks on older people and those living alone each day.
- Someone keeps the notice board current, with the latest official information.
- Someone knows the medical needs of the street and the nearest help.
- Someone records the group's water, food and fuel, and who has what.

Rotate the roles every few months, so that knowledge is shared and no one burns out.

## Patrol carefully

Patrols deter problems when they are visible, calm and predictable.

- Walk the street in pairs, at set times, and avoid confrontation.
- Carry a charged phone, and agree a simple signal for help.
- Note problems and report them to the authorities, rather than acting alone.
- Keep the group informed of any real threat, with facts rather than rumour.

## Keep it fair

Share resources openly, and do not ask for more than the household can give. Record what is lent and what is returned, so that trust stays high on both sides.

Agree in advance how shared supplies will be divided if they run low. A simple written rule, decided calmly, prevents resentment later.

## Practice before you need it

1. Hold a short drill once a season, with the contact list and the meeting point.
2. Time how long it takes to reach every household on the list.
3. Test the signal for help, and check that every phone holds a charge.
4. Review the list and the roles after each drill, and note what needs changing.

## Adjusting for conditions

At night, patrols should stay near lit areas and report rather than investigate. In heavy rain or heat, shorten the patrol and check on the most vulnerable first. If the street has many elderly or disabled residents, add a second checker for each one.

## Communicating with the street

A group is only as useful as its messages. Choose one channel for urgent updates, such as a single phone chain or a notice board in the hall, and keep it for facts only. Write each message in plain language: what happened, where, when and what the group should do. Avoid guesses, and mark anything unverified as unconfirmed. Ask residents how they prefer to receive news, because older people may rely on a printed notice rather than a message. Agree how often updates will be sent, so that the street does not drown in repetition and miss the one message that matters.

## Common mistakes

- Starting a patrol without a plan or a contact list.
- Spreading rumours about neighbours.
- Asking the same people to do every task.
- Keeping the list on one phone, which may be lost or broken.

## Checklist

- The contact list is current, and the contacts hold copies.
- The meeting point is agreed and known.
- Roles are shared, with a named person for each.
- Patrols run in pairs, at set times.

## When it fails

If the group splits or the list is lost, call a short meeting at the meeting point and rebuild the list on paper. If a problem is beyond the group, report it to the authorities and step back.

**Warning:** a watch group must not take the law into its own hands. Its job is to observe, help and report.

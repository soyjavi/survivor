# Travelling Safely in Unrest

In unrest, journeys are the most exposed moments. Plan carefully, travel light and keep your options open. A trip that can be turned back easily is far safer than one that depends on a single road and a fixed arrival time.

![Route map: map with waypoint-pin for home and destination, two line-solid routes, caution checkpoints, decision turn-back points; labels: route A, route B, checkpoint, turn back; a second route exists so turning back is planned.](assets/travelling-safely-in-unrest-1.svg)

## Why it matters

On the road you have little control. You cannot choose who stops you, how long a queue lasts or whether a road is open. Crowds gather at junctions, and the time between leaving and arriving gives problems room to develop. Good planning does not remove these risks, but it lets you change course early, before you are committed to a route that has become dangerous.

## Before you go

1. Check the latest official information on the areas you must pass through.
2. Plan two routes, and avoid crowds, demonstrations and checkpoints where possible.
3. Tell a trusted person your route, timing and expected arrival, and agree to call when you arrive.
4. Carry a charged phone, a paper map, water and a basic first aid kit.

Decide in advance the points at which you will turn back, such as a closed junction, a crowd that is growing or a road that is not clear. Write them on the paper map so that the decision is already made when you reach them.

![Paper route map: map with three decision at junctions, line-solid route, shelter symbol of hut, note callouts; labels: turn back, shelter; each turn-back decision is written in advance, so it is already made at the junction.](assets/travelling-safely-in-unrest-2.svg)

## On the way

- Travel in daylight, and in a group where you can.
- Keep your bag closed and your valuables out of sight.
- Do not stop in unfamiliar or deserted places.
- Keep your phone away from view, and keep it charged.

Keep the fuel tank at least half full, and check tyres and lights before you set out. A breakdown in an unsafe area is one of the most serious risks on a journey.

## Checkpoints

Checkpoints are tense for everyone, so make your behaviour predictable.

- Stay calm and polite. Show identification if asked.
- Keep your hands visible, and follow instructions.

![Checkpoint sequence: step-1, step-2, step-3, step-box with arrow-straight, person-standing; labels: stop calmly, keep hands visible, show identification, follow instructions; hands are seen first, before any movement. NEEDS: open-hand, id-card](assets/travelling-safely-in-unrest-3.svg)

- Do not take photos or argue. Wait for instructions before moving.

## Practice before you need it

1. Walk through the checkpoint routine with a family member, acting as the officer, using a clear sequence of steps.
2. Keep identification and the trip plan in the same pocket, so that both are ready at once.
3. Practise a calm sentence to explain your journey, such as "I am going to my family's home."
4. Review your routes each week, because roads close and reopen quickly.

## Adjusting for conditions

At night, visibility is poor and hazards are harder to see, so delay the journey until morning if you can. In rain, reduce speed and keep a wider gap to the vehicle ahead. If you travel on foot, stay on the side of the road that faces the traffic, and wear light-coloured or reflective clothing so that drivers can see you.

## Keeping options open

A journey is safer when you can change it without losing your way. Keep a second destination in mind, such as a relative's home, a shelter or a larger town, in case the first is unreachable. Fill the fuel tank before leaving, and carry a small reserve of water and snacks in the vehicle. Note the locations of hospitals, pharmacies and open shelters along each route. If you must stop, choose a well-lit, staffed place rather than an empty layby. Agree with the person you are travelling to that they will expect a message at each stage, so that a delay is noticed early.

## Common mistakes

- Setting out without a second route.
- Travelling late, when visibility and help are both poor.
- Stopping to watch a demonstration.
- Ignoring a feeling that the road ahead is wrong.

## Checklist

- Two routes are planned, with turn-back points.
- A trusted person knows the route and the expected arrival.
- A charged phone, a paper map, water and first aid are carried.
- Documents are ready to show at a checkpoint.

## When it fails

If the vehicle breaks down, stay with it in a safe, visible place, and call the person who knows your route. If you are stopped, comply calmly and keep the conversation short, then continue as soon as it is allowed.

**Warning:** if you feel unsafe at any point, leave the area by the most direct safe route.

# Search and Rescue for Households

When someone in the household is missing or trapped, the urge to act immediately is strong and understandable. Yet the most common cause of additional injuries in a disaster is a rescuer who enters an unstable area without a plan. A careful, organised search finds more people and keeps the searchers alive.

## When to search

A household search makes sense when a person was last known to be in a building, vehicle, or area that you can reach without serious risk. It does not make sense when the area is clearly beyond your skill and equipment.

1. Note the last time and place the person was seen, and who saw them.
2. Check whether the person is already known to be at a shelter, hospital, or meeting point before searching.
3. Assess whether the building or area is safe to enter at all.
4. If it is not safe, report the missing person to the authorities and wait for trained teams.

## Safe search methods

Search with a partner, never alone. Tell someone outside the area where you are going and when you expect to return.

1. Put on sturdy boots, gloves, a helmet or hard hat if one exists, and a dust mask or cloth over the nose and mouth.
2. Carry a torch, a whistle, a water bottle, and a charged phone or working radio.
3. Mark each area you search with a simple sign, such as a chalk X with the date and time, so others know it has been checked.
4. Move slowly through the space, calling the person's name and stopping every few steps to listen for a response, knocking, or movement.
5. Look at the structure before stepping. Avoid areas with sagging ceilings, leaning walls, exposed wires, gas smells, or water pooling in unexpected places.
6. Stay on the most solid surfaces, and keep one hand free for balance and support.
7. Take breaks, drink water, and stop when exhausted. A tired searcher makes dangerous mistakes.

## Searching in flood water

Floodwater hides hazards, can be contaminated, and moves faster than most people expect. Avoid entering it wherever possible. If you must enter, use a rope tied to a fixed point and keep low to the ground to resist the current.

- Never drive or walk into moving water.
- Stay away from downed power lines in or near water.
- Check depth with a pole before every step.

## Searching in rubble

Rubble can shift without warning, and any movement can bring down more material. Do not dig, pull, or lift heavy pieces unless you are trained to do so.

1. Look for voice sounds and tapping, and stop all nearby work while listening.
2. Shout the person's name, then wait a full minute in silence before shouting again.
3. Mark any spot where you hear a response, and report it to the nearest rescue team.
4. Do not remove large beams, slabs, or walls without support from qualified people.

## What not to do

- Do not enter a building with visible structural damage, a strong gas smell, or fire.
- Do not use open flames, including candles, for light inside damaged buildings.
- Do not run hoses, wires, or ropes across unstable areas without checking them.
- Do not move an injured person unless they are in immediate danger, such as fire or rising water.
- Do not try to cut or break through walls, gas pipes, or electrical lines.
- Do not give up searching the wrong place for too long. Re-check the last known location and the route the person would have taken.

## Calling professionals

Trained teams carry equipment, know how to shore up structures, and can work safely in dangerous conditions. Call them as soon as you have reason to believe someone is trapped.

1. Call the emergency number available in your area, or the local authority or aid post if no number works.
2. Give the exact address or landmark, the number of people missing, and the time they were last seen.
3. Describe the hazards you can see, such as smoke, gas, water, or unstable walls.
4. Stay at a safe distance and guide the arriving team to the search location.
5. Share everything you know, including what you have already searched and the places you have marked.

Keep the family informed and stay near a designated meeting point, so you can be reached when information changes.

## Looking after yourself

Rescuers in households often suffer injuries, heat stress, and emotional distress. Rest, drink water, eat when you can, and tell someone when you are struggling. Accept help from neighbours and trained staff, and allow them to take over when they arrive.

**Warning:** Never climb onto unstable rubble or lift a heavy slab to reach a trapped person. Collapses kill rescuers who were trying to help.

# Non-Violent Defensive Posture

The best defence is avoiding the situation. Awareness, routine and distance protect people far more than force, and they cost nothing to practise. This guide covers prevention only, and it is written for ordinary people going about daily life in a tense period.

![Concentric rings: survivor-mark at centre, three nested bracket rings; labels: awareness, routine, distance; each habit is a separate layer, and the outer rings protect the inner ones. NEEDS: ring](assets/non-violent-defensive-posture-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Most people who are targeted have shown signs in advance: they were alone, distracted, predictable or in a place that offered no escape. A posture built on awareness changes those signs. It makes you a harder target without making you a combatant, and it keeps you out of the fights that cause the most harm to bystanders and to the people who start them.

## Awareness

Most dangerous situations show signs before they start. Notice them early.

- Notice who is near you, and what they are doing.
- Pay attention to people who seem to be following or watching you, especially if they keep the same distance over several turns.
- Keep your phone and bags out of sight, and your hands free.

Use a simple habit: every time you enter a new space, note the exits, the people nearest to you and any object you could use to create distance. The habit takes seconds and becomes automatic with practice.

## Routine

Predictable routines are easy to exploit, so vary what you can.

- Vary your routes and times when possible, but let someone you trust know them.
- Avoid poorly lit, empty or isolated places.
- Walk in groups where you can.

## Distance

Distance gives you time to react, and time is the most valuable resource you have.

- Keep a comfortable distance from people you do not know.
- Move away from anyone who seems threatening, toward light and people.
- Cross the street or step into a shop if someone approaches too closely.

If someone closes distance quickly, turn your body, step back and say something firm and neutral, such as "Stay there, please." A clear boundary often ends the approach before it begins.

## Confidence

Stand upright and walk with purpose. This reduces the chance of being chosen as a target, and it helps you think clearly when you need to.

## Practice before you need it

1. Walk a familiar route and name the exits, lit spaces and busy points aloud.
2. Practise stepping back and turning the body with a friend acting as the approaching person.
3. Practise saying a short, firm boundary sentence until it comes out without hesitation.
4. Repeat the exercise on a different route each month, so that the habit works everywhere.

## Adjusting for conditions

In crowds, keep to the edge and keep your back near a wall or shopfront. At night, prefer the side of the street with more light and people, even if it is a longer route. In a tense period, reduce your time in public places and keep trips short, so that there are fewer chances for an encounter.

## Reading a situation early

Most incidents follow a recognisable sequence: an unusual interest in you, a move to reduce the distance, a request that is hard to refuse, and then a physical approach. Each stage offers a chance to step away. Notice when a stranger repeats a question, asks where you live, or starts to walk in step with you. When that happens, change direction, cross the road or enter a busy place. Do not wait to see whether the behaviour continues. A calm decision to leave early costs little, and it keeps the situation from reaching the point where choices are narrow.

## Common mistakes

- Staying in a place because you feel awkward about leaving.
- Taking the same route at the same time every day.
- Looking at a phone while walking through an unfamiliar area.
- Confronting someone to prove a point.

## Checklist

- Phone and bags are secured, with hands free.
- A route and arrival time were shared with someone.
- Well-lit, populated paths are preferred.
- Distance is kept from anyone who seems threatening.

## After an incident

Get to a safe place, call for help, and note what you remember as soon as you can. Write down times, descriptions and directions while they are fresh, and do not share unverified details publicly.

**Warning:** do not carry or use weapons in a crisis. They escalate situations and are often used against the person carrying them.

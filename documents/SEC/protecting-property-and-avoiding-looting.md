# Protecting Property and Avoiding Looting

When order breaks down, property crime rises. Protecting your home is mainly about making it less attractive and making sure people know you are there. Good neighbours and visible care do more than walls and bravado.

## Why it matters

Property crime tends to follow opportunity. A house that looks empty, dark or unattended is an easy choice for someone passing through, and the first house on a street that is breached often becomes the start of a wider problem. Protecting property also protects the people inside, because an intruder who finds an empty home has less reason to linger, and a household that stays calm is less likely to be drawn into a confrontation.

## Make the house less attractive

- Keep the front clear and tidy. Empty-looking houses invite trouble.
- Keep valuables out of sight, from windows and from the street.
- Store fuel and tools inside a locked space, or in a locked shed.

![Side-by-side scene: left tidy path with flashlight and do callout, right cluttered yard with do-not and caution; labels: tidy, cluttered; clutter signals an empty house to passers-by. NEEDS: house, gate](assets/protecting-property-and-avoiding-looting-2.svg)

A simple test helps: walk to the front gate and look back at the house. Note anything that is visible from the street and would be useful to a stranger, then move or cover it.

## Make it known you are present

- Keep lights on in the evening, at the front and back.
- Talk to neighbours and agree to watch each other's houses.
- Ask a neighbour to collect post and mark it when you are away.

## Protect yourself

- Do not show off supplies or food.
- Do not carry large amounts of cash or valuables in public.
- Keep your route home unpredictable, if you must travel after dark.
- Share your home address and travel plans only with people you trust.

## Do not take part

Do not take goods that do not belong to you, even if they are unattended or abandoned. Looting puts people in danger, it can get you arrested or hurt, and it harms the community you rely on. If goods are left in the street, leave them where they are and tell the authorities.

## Practice before you need it

1. Walk around the house once a month and check its outside as a stranger would.
2. Agree with a neighbour a simple signal for trouble, such as a light switched on and off.
3. Test the outside lights and replace bulbs that have failed.
4. Make a list of valuables and documents, with photographs, and keep a copy away from the house.

## Adjusting for conditions

When the power is out, use battery lights at the front and back rather than a candle, which can draw attention and start a fire. If you leave the house for several days, ask a neighbour to open curtains and move a bin, so that the house does not look abandoned. In a crowded street, keep your own belongings close and avoid standing near goods that have been left out.

## Dealing with pressure from others

Neighbours or passers-by may suggest that taking goods is normal, or that it is the only way to get what the household needs. Do not accept that reasoning. If you are short of something essential, ask the group, the shelter or the relevant service for help first, because they can often provide it through proper channels. If someone offers you goods that were obtained from a shop or a home, decline them. Accepting stolen goods can expose your household to the same risks as taking them, and it weakens the trust that keeps the street safe.

## Common mistakes

- Advertising supplies on social media.
- Storing cash and documents in an obvious place.
- Leaving a house dark and silent for days.
- Joining a crowd that is taking goods from a shop.

## Checklist

- Valuables are out of sight.
- Lights are kept on in the evening.
- A neighbour has agreed to watch the house.
- Fuel and tools are locked away.

## If it happens

Do not confront people. Note details safely from a distance, such as the time, vehicle colours and direction of travel, and report the incident to the authorities when you can. If someone is in the house, leave and call for help rather than defending the property.

**Warning:** do not stay to defend property if it puts people at risk. Property can be replaced. People cannot.

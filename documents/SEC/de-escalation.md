# De-escalation

Most dangerous situations begin as arguments, and many can be defused by a calm voice and a clear plan. Your goal is to lower the temperature, not to win the argument. A calm response also protects you, because a tense person who feels heard is far less likely to turn to force.

## Why it matters

Fear narrows attention. A frightened or angry person hears only part of what is said, and they read threats into neutral movements. Once a person feels cornered, every reply is interpreted as an attack, and the conversation becomes a contest. Lowering the emotional temperature first makes every later step easier, because reasoning returns only when the body has calmed.

![Two-panel contrast: left, person-standing inside a bracket narrowed to a threat; right, person-standing with arrow-branch fanning to two start-or-end exits; labels: narrow focus, wide view; fear shrinks what a person sees and hears. NEEDS: funnel](assets/de-escalation-2.svg)

## Stay calm

Your body sends signals before your words do. Adjust yourself first.

- Keep your voice low, slow and steady.
- Keep your body relaxed, with your hands visible and open.
- Stand at an angle, not square on, and keep an arm's length or more.
- Avoid pointing, folding your arms or staring fixedly.

Breathe slowly and let your shoulders drop. The other person often copies the pace of the people around them, so a steady rhythm is contagious.

![Seated scene: two person-sitting facing each other across table-grid, leader pointing to each; labels: eye level, hands visible, listening; calm posture sits level, not standing over the other person. NEEDS: open-hand](assets/de-escalation-3.svg)

## Listen

Listening is the most effective tool you have. Most upset people want to be heard before they can think clearly.

- Let the person speak without interrupting.
- Repeat back what they have said, in your own words, to show you understand.
- Acknowledge their feelings: "You are worried about the water."

Do not rush to fix the problem. A short summary of what you heard is often enough to change the mood, and it tells you whether you have understood the real concern.

## Offer options

People calm down more easily when they feel they have a choice.

1. Give a small, clear choice, such as "Let's sit and talk, or we can speak later."
2. Do not make threats, ultimatums or demands you cannot keep.
3. Offer something concrete when you can, such as information, a drink or a place to wait.

Use the table below to match the kind of tension you face with a suitable first response.

| Situation | First response | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Frustration about a queue | Explain the wait and the next step | Telling them to calm down |
| Fear for a relative | Ask what they need most right now | Dismissing the fear |
| Accusation against a neighbour | Ask for the facts, then offer to check together | Taking sides in public |

## Escalation signs

Watch for clenched fists, raised voices, pacing, a shrinking circle of space, or sudden silence. These mean the situation may be about to turn. Slow down, lower your voice further and create more distance.

![Escalation ladder: five step-box in vertical order joined by arrow-straight, threshold line-dashed above the third, arrow-curved stepping back from it; labels: calm talk, clenched fists, raised voice, shrinking space, walking away; step back at the warning signs, before the top.](assets/de-escalation-4.svg)

## Practice before you need it

1. Pair up with a family member and take turns playing the upset person.
2. Practise a calm opening line, such as "I can see this matters to you."
3. Practise a close-out line, such as "Let's pause and come back to this."
4. Swap roles and notice how much easier it is to stay calm when you have rehearsed.

## Adjusting for conditions

In a crowd, speak to one person at a time and keep your voice below the level of the group. In the dark or in a noisy place, move to a quieter spot if you can, and make sure the other person can see your hands. If you are already tired or hungry, say so and ask for a short break before you continue.

## Common mistakes

- Standing too close to show you are not afraid.
- Matching a loud voice with a louder one.
- Promising what you cannot deliver.
- Arguing about facts while the person still feels unheard.

## Checklist

- Voice low, hands open, distance kept.
- Listened first, then spoke.
- One or two clear options offered.
- Exit route known before you start.

## After

Check on the people involved, and tell the group what has been agreed. Write the agreement down in simple words, so that no one remembers it differently later.

**Warning:** do not put yourself between two people who are fighting. Get help.

# Dealing with Strangers

Most people in a crisis are looking for help, not trouble. A small number are not. Be courteous, be careful, and do not share more than is needed. Your aim is to protect your household while still treating the other person with respect.

## Why it matters

In a crisis, normal social checks disappear. Identification may be missing, phones may not work, and people who are desperate may behave in ways they would never choose in ordinary life. A stranger who gains entry or learns the layout of your home has an advantage you cannot easily undo. Caution at the first contact is the cheapest protection you have.

## At the door

A knock can come from a neighbour, a volunteer or someone with bad intentions. Follow these steps every time.

1. Do not open the door to someone you do not know.
2. Ask who they are and what they need, through the closed door.
3. Ask them to leave a message or come back later, if you are not sure.
4. If they are in need, offer help through the door, such as water in a container, rather than inviting them in.
5. Close and lock the door before you walk away from the conversation.

Keep children out of sight of the door while you talk, and have a second adult nearby if you can.

## On the road

- Do not get into a vehicle with a stranger.
- Keep your distance from people who are very close or who grab at you.
- Trust your instincts. If something feels wrong, move away and go to a public place.
- Walk on the side of the road facing traffic, so that you can see vehicles approach.

Pay attention to people who appear at your side without warning, or who ask the same question twice. Those patterns often signal that the conversation is a way of holding your attention while someone else acts.

## Sharing information

Do not give away the location of your home, your supplies or your routine. Share only what helps. A stranger who learns that you have water, fuel or medicine may return later, and a stranger who knows when the house is empty may come back for it.

Use a simple script when someone asks for information: "I can't share that, but I can point you to the shelter on the main road." A short, polite refusal avoids a debate and keeps the answer consistent across the household.

## Helping others

Helping is good, but keep it safe.

- Meet in a public place, with a second person, when you hand over supplies.
- Hand over a small amount first, and watch how the person responds.
- Give help through a group or a shelter rather than to one person in a dark place.

## Practice before you need it

1. Agree on a short phrase that the household uses to signal a stranger at the door.
2. Practise the door routine with a family member acting as the visitor.
3. Decide where each person stands during the conversation, and who calls for help.
4. Review the routine once a month, and after any local change.

## Adjusting for conditions

At night or in a crowded shelter, treat every unknown approach with more care, because you have less time to assess the situation. In a shelter, keep your belongings within sight, and ask staff rather than strangers for directions or information about your family members.

## Signs to watch for

Some behaviour is worth noticing before you act. A visitor who refuses to give a name, who pushes past the door or who asks about the household's numbers, supplies or timetable is showing a pattern you should not ignore. Someone who looks at your windows and doors more than at you, or who arrives in a vehicle with no visible purpose, also deserves a closer look from behind a locked door. Trust the discomfort you feel in your body, and treat it as enough reason to end the conversation.

## Common mistakes

- Opening the door out of politeness.
- Telling a stranger how much food is stored.
- Waiting until you feel threatened before leaving.
- Meeting someone alone in a quiet place.

## Checklist

- The door stays locked until you know who is there.
- Nothing about your supplies or routine is shared.
- Handovers take place in public, with a second person.
- Your exit route is known.

## When it fails

If someone forces entry or threatens you, move away from the door and into the safe room if you have one. Call for help. Do not argue or try to reason through the threat. Note what you remember afterwards, while it is fresh.

**Warning:** if someone threatens you, move away and call for help. Do not argue or fight.

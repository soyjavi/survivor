# Energy Budgeting

An energy budget tells you how long your stored power will last and what to switch off first. Work it out before you need it, because a budget made during a cut is usually made in a hurry and with the lights already dim. A simple sheet on paper is enough.

![Worksheet table: table-header row (device, watts, hours, Wh), table-grid rows below, arrow-straight from each row to a total row, step-box for the daily total; labels device, watts, hours, watt-hours, daily total; every row is one multiplication and the total is their sum.](assets/energy-budgeting-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Power in a cut is finite. Every watt-hour you spend today is one you cannot use tomorrow, so the budget turns a vague worry into a number you can act on. It also shows which device is quietly taking most of the energy, which is often not the one you expect.

## Equipment and materials

- A sheet of paper or a spreadsheet, with columns for device, watts, hours and watt-hours.
- The label or manual for each device, to read its power rating.
- A plug-in energy monitor, if you have one, to measure devices that have no label.
- The capacity figure from each battery and the output figure from each generator or solar panel.

## Measure the need

Start with the devices you actually use, not the ones you could use. Each device has a power rating printed on its label or in its manual, usually in watts.

1. List each device, its power in watts, and the hours it runs per day. The label shows the power.
2. Multiply watts by hours to get watt-hours (Wh) per day for each device.
3. Add the totals for all devices to get the daily need for the household.

Phone chargers, radios and torches use little energy each, but they run often. Fridges and pumps run for long periods and make up a large share of the total.

## Measure the supply

Write down what you have before you calculate how long it will last.

- Battery capacity in watt-hours, from the label.
- Solar output, which depends on the sun, weather and season. Assume about half the rated output as a planning figure.
- Fuel for a generator, and its output in watts.

## Work out the days

Divide the stored energy by the daily need. Add a margin of 20 per cent for losses in wiring, charging and inverters, which always waste some energy as heat.

**Example:** a household uses 600 Wh per day. A battery of 3,000 Wh gives about five days in theory. With the 20 per cent margin the usable energy falls to 2,400 Wh, which gives four days. If the same 20 per cent is added to the daily need as well, the need becomes 720 Wh per day, and the figure drops to about three and a third days.

## Adjusting for seasons

Winter changes the sums in two ways. Days are short, so solar output drops sharply, and cold lowers battery capacity. Summer brings the opposite problem, as fridges work harder and fans or cooling fans add load. Recalculate the supply each season, and do not reuse last year's figure.

## Cut the load

Reducing demand extends the supply more than any other step.

- Charge only what you need.
- Use LED lights instead of older bulbs.
- Use hand tools and manual methods where you can.
- Switch off standby power at the socket, because standby devices still draw energy all day.

## Set priorities

When the budget is tight, decide in advance which loads come first.

1. Medical devices.
2. Communication: radio and phone charging.
3. Lighting for safety.
4. Anything else.

## Common mistakes

- Using the rated battery capacity as if it were all usable. Batteries lose capacity as they age and as they get cold.
- Forgetting that appliances with motors draw a surge when they start.
- Counting solar output as steady. A cloudy day can produce a small fraction of the planning figure.

## Troubleshooting

- **The battery empties faster than the sheet predicts:** check for standby loads and forgotten chargers, then measure a few devices at the socket.
- **The solar figure looks too high:** reduce it again for shade, dust and the time of year.

## Checklist

- Daily need written down, device by device.
- Supply written down, with the margin applied.
- Priorities agreed by everyone in the household.
- Budget reviewed each day against what was used.

## When the budget fails

If the stored energy falls faster than planned, stop the lowest priority loads first, then reduce charging times. Recalculate with the real figures for the next day, rather than the ones you hoped for.

**Warning:** do not let the budget run down to zero. Keep a reserve for emergencies and for medical equipment.

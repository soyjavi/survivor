# Powering Devices from a Car

A car battery can charge phones, radios and torches for a useful period. It is a good backup, but it uses fuel, depends on the health of the battery, and must be handled carefully. The engine is the real hazard, not the charger.

## Why it matters

A car is often the only vehicle-sized energy store a household has, and it can charge several small devices a day. The same engine that makes it useful also makes it dangerous, because its exhaust is one of the most common causes of carbon monoxide deaths in cold weather. Treat the car as a small generator, and apply the same distance and air rules.

## Equipment and materials

- A 12-volt charger with a USB output, rated for the car's socket.
- A set of jump leads of adequate length and thickness.
- A tyre pump or battery tester, if the car has no battery monitor.
- A fuel reserve for the car, kept in an approved container outside.
- A printed note of the car's engine run times, so that everyone in the household follows the same routine.

## Safety first

The exhaust from a car engine contains carbon monoxide, which has no smell and no colour. It can fill a garage or a closed yard in minutes.

- **Never run the engine in a closed garage or enclosed space.** Carbon monoxide builds up very quickly.
- Park outside, facing away from the house, with the exhaust clear of people.
- Do not leave the engine running unattended.

## Charge the devices

Use a plan that charges devices in short bursts, so the battery keeps enough reserve to start the engine.

1. Use a 12-volt charger designed for car sockets.
2. Plug the device into the charger, and check it is charging.
3. Run the engine for short periods, about 15 to 20 minutes every hour, to limit fuel use and prevent the battery from draining.

## Protect the battery

A flat battery means the car will not start, which removes the backup entirely.

- Keep an eye on the battery. A flat battery means the car will not start.
- Do not drain the battery below the level needed to start the engine.
- Keep jump leads in the car.

## Adjusting for weather

Cold weather weakens a car battery sharply, so a battery that starts easily in autumn may fail on a frosty morning. In cold conditions, shorten the charging periods and check the battery more often. In hot weather, the charger and the device can overheat in a closed car, so leave the doors open while charging and keep the cable clear of the seats. In wet weather, keep the socket and the plug dry.

## Save fuel

Fuel is finite in a long outage, so treat it as part of the power budget.

- Charge only what you need.
- Turn off the engine as soon as the charging is done.

## Common mistakes

- Running the engine to charge a phone overnight, which uses a lot of fuel and may leave the car stuck.
- Using a cheap charger that does not match the device's voltage or current.
- Parking in a garage to keep warm while the engine runs.
- Charging a device while the car is moving, where a loose cable can pull free.

## Troubleshooting

- **The device will not charge:** check the socket fuse, the charger and the cable, in that order.
- **The engine will not start after charging:** the battery is too low. Use the jump leads, then recharge by driving rather than by idling.

## Checklist

1. Car parked outdoors, away from windows and vents.
2. Charger designed for car sockets.
3. Engine run in short periods.
4. Battery level checked before each run.
5. Jump leads in the car.

## When the battery is low

If the battery is low, stop charging and let it recover with a longer run later, when the car is outdoors and safe. If the car will not start, use the jump leads with a second vehicle, with the engines off while connecting.

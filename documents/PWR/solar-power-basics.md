# Solar Power Basics

A small solar system turns sunlight into electricity and stores it in batteries. It is quiet and needs little fuel, but its output depends on the sun and must be planned. The panel does not produce power at night, so the battery carries the household through the dark hours.

## Why it matters

Solar is the one source in a cut that keeps working without fuel deliveries, which makes it valuable in a long outage. It is also easy to oversell. A panel rated at a certain wattage rarely delivers that figure in practice, so the household must plan from realistic output and not from the label. A well-matched small system can carry lights, communication and a fridge for part of the day, which is more useful than a large system that is badly connected.

## Equipment and materials

- A folding or fixed panel with a stand, mounted securely.
- A charge controller matched to the panel's voltage and the battery's chemistry.
- A sealed storage battery, sized to the energy budget.
- An inverter only if mains-style devices must run, and cable of the correct thickness.
- Fuses or breakers for each connection, and a multimeter for checking voltage.

## Parts

Each part has one job, and the system fails when one of them is missing or badly matched.

- **Panel:** converts sunlight into direct current.
- **Charge controller:** protects the battery from overcharging and deep discharge.
- **Battery:** stores the energy for use after dark.
- **Inverter (if needed):** converts stored direct current into mains-style alternating current for some devices.

## Setup

Placement affects output more than almost any other factor, so take time over it.

1. Place the panel in full sun, facing the equator, at an angle near the local latitude.
2. Keep it clear of shade from trees, buildings and dirt.
3. Connect the controller first, then the battery, then the panel. Disconnect in the reverse order.
4. Fuse the connections, and keep wiring short and secure.

## Use

Run the system in a way that matches what the panel can supply.

- Run heavy loads in daylight, when the panel supplies them directly.
- Use the battery for lights, radio, phone and small devices after dark.
- Check the battery level each day.

## Adjusting for season and latitude

Output changes with the season. In winter, the sun is lower and the days are shorter, so the same panel may supply a fraction of its summer output. Plan the energy budget against the winter figure, and treat summer as a surplus. At high latitudes, tilt the panel more steeply in winter to catch the lower sun. Clean the panel more often in autumn, when leaves and dust build up.

## Troubleshooting

- **The battery is not charging on a clear day:** check the controller display, then the fuses, then the connections for heat or corrosion.
- **The panel warms and the controller shuts off:** the wiring may be undersized, or the panel is connected to a load it should not feed directly.
- **The battery drains before morning:** reduce the load after dark or add capacity, and check that no device is left switched on.

## Maintenance

Dirt and corrosion steadily reduce output, so a short routine keeps the system working.

- Clean the panel each month, and after dust or bird droppings.
- Check connections for corrosion and heat.

## Common mistakes

- Connecting the panel to the battery before the controller, which can damage the battery.
- Placing the panel in partial shade, which cuts output far more than the shaded area suggests.
- Assuming a sunny day means the battery will be full by evening.
- Using a panel with a rated wattage far above the controller's limit.

## Checklist

- Panel in full sun, angled and facing the right way.
- Controller connected first and disconnected last.
- Fuses in place.
- Battery level checked daily.
- Panel and connections cleaned and inspected.

## When output drops

If the battery does not charge, check the panel for shade and dirt, then check the connections for corrosion. A reading that stays low on a clear day points to a fault in the controller or the wiring, and that needs a qualified person.

**Warning:** do not connect a solar system to the home's wiring without a qualified electrician. It can endanger line workers.

# Fire Starters for Damp Wood

Rain makes fuel hard to light. The key is to find dry material inside the wet, and then to protect it while the fire grows. Most failed fires in wet weather fail for one of two reasons: the tinder was wet, or the fuel was too large too early.

> @image Splitting damp logs to reach dry wood, and building a tinder pile

## Why it matters

Wet wood is not useless, but it cannot take heat until its water has been driven off. A flame that is too small to boil that water simply goes out. The job in wet weather is to give the fire a dry core that burns on its own, then add the damp wood slowly once the core is hot. Understanding this stops most of the frustration, because it shows why big logs and fast fuel kill wet-weather fires.

## Find dry material

- **Under bark:** the dry inner wood of a dead standing tree, or the inside of a fallen log.
- **Dead branches still on a tree:** they stay drier than those on the ground.
- **Dead twigs at the bottom of conifers:** often dry under the branches.
- **Birch bark:** burns even when wet on the outside, because its oils keep it alight.

## Split the wood

Split damp logs to expose their dry centre. Use the fine shavings and the centre of the split as tinder. Feather the shavings so they stand up off the wood, which lets the flame reach them.

## Carry a fire starter

- Pack a small tin with cotton wool, petroleum jelly and dry tinder.
- Keep a ferro rod or lighter in a waterproof pouch.
- Bring a few dry candles or fire paste.

![kit layout: tin with cotton wool, jar of petroleum jelly, ferro-rod in bag; labels Cotton wool tin, Jelly, Ferro rod; the starter must be sealed to stay usable in rain. NEEDS: ferro-rod.](assets/fire-starters-for-damp-wood-3.svg)

## Shelter the fire

1. Build a windbreak with stones, bark or a tarp.
2. Place a flat rock or log under the fire to keep it off wet ground.
3. Build the fire in a small pile so the heat stays concentrated.
4. Add fuel only once the kindling is burning hard.

## Step-by-step in detail

Start with the dry core. Gather fine shavings, a handful of dry twigs and the dry centre of a split log, and place them on the flat base. Light the tinder with the lowest possible flame and let it climb through the twigs. Only when the twigs are crackling should you add the first split piece, laid across the top, not on the flame. Keep adding wood a little at a time, and let the flame dry each piece before the next goes on.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In heavy rain,** build the fire under an overhang or a tarp rig before you start looking for wood. Work in the shelter, not in the downpour.
- **In snow,** clear down to the ground, then build on a platform of logs or stones, so the fire does not sink into the snow.
- **In wind,** make the windbreak higher on the windward side, and keep the tinder low, close to the shelter.
- **After flooding,** expect every fuel to be damp. Use stored dry fire starter first and keep the first fire small.

## Common mistakes

- Using the wettest, largest log as the first fuel, then wondering why nothing burns.
- Stacking wood too tightly, which chokes the flame before it spreads.
- Blowing too hard, which cools the tinder and scatters it.
- Building on bare wet ground, so the heat is lost into the earth.
- Carrying a damp matchbox because the container was not sealed.

## Practice before you need it

Pick a damp afternoon and try the method with only the materials you carry. Find dry tinder in a wet wood, split a log, feather the shavings and light the fire under a tarp. Time how long it takes, and note which step failed. Repeat it until the sequence is automatic, because in the rain you will not have time to think it through.

## Troubleshooting

If the fire hisses and goes out, the fuel is still too wet. Stop adding large pieces, dry the tinder close to the flame, and rebuild on a smaller scale. If sparks land but the tinder will not catch, the shavings are probably too thick. Shave finer curls, and keep them stored in a dry pouch until the moment you need them.

## When it fails

If the fire hisses and goes out, the fuel is still too wet. Stop adding large pieces, dry the tinder close to the flame, and rebuild on a smaller scale.

**Warning:** never use a fuel accelerant to force a damp fire. It can flare up dangerously.

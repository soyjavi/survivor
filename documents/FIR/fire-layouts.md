# Fire Layouts

The shape of a fire decides what it is good for. Choose the layout for the job, not the other way round. A teepee that lights quickly is the wrong choice for a night of steady warmth, and a low trench is the wrong choice for signalling.

![Log windbreak acting as a reflector behind a fire](assets/fire-layouts-1.png)

## Why it matters

Most poor fires are not badly lit. They are badly shaped for the task. A cooking fire that throws its heat upward wastes fuel and burns pots unevenly. A warmth fire built as a narrow pile burns out in an hour. Matching the layout to the purpose saves wood, keeps people safer and makes the work of feeding the fire far lighter.

## Teepee

A cone of kindling over tinder. It lights fast and burns hot, which makes it ideal for starting a fire. It burns out quickly, so add fuel as soon as the kindling takes hold.

![side view: tinder-pile at centre, kindling teepee over it with a gap, arrow-straight for wind entering the gap, flame inside; labels Tinder, Kindling, Wind, Open side; the open side must face the wind so air reaches the flame.](assets/fire-layouts-1.svg)

## Log cabin

Logs stacked in layers around a central tinder pile. It burns long and steadily, which makes it good for warmth through the night. Build it on a flat base and keep each layer level so the structure does not collapse.

![top-down: log-stack as crossing layers each turned ninety degrees, tinder-pile centre, arrow-straight through the gaps; labels Layer 1, Layer 2, Air gaps; crossing layers keep the stack level and let air in.](assets/fire-layouts-2.svg)

## Star fire

Several logs laid like spokes from a central fire, pushed inward as they burn. It uses little wood and gives long, low heat. Keep the log ends together and push them in often, so the fire does not spread out and go cold.

![top-down: coals at centre, four log-stack spokes, arrow-straight pointing inward from each log end; labels Coal bed, Logs, Push inward; feeding the ends in as they burn keeps the fire compact.](assets/fire-layouts-3.svg)

## Trench fire

A narrow trench with a fire at one end, using the wind to draw air along it. It is good for cooking with pots, and the trench hides much of the light. Dig the trench so the wind blows along it, not across it.

![side view: stream-bed as trench on ground-line, coals and flame at the downwind end, tripod-pot over the coals, arrow-straight for wind along the trench; labels Trench, Wind, Downwind end; the trench must run with the wind.](assets/fire-layouts-4.svg)

## Reflector fire

A fire built against a wall of logs or stones, which bounces heat back toward the shelter. It gives good warmth in cold places. Build the wall close enough to reflect heat, but far enough back that it does not scorch.

## Materials for each layout

| Layout | Main use | Fuel size | Wind needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teepee | Lighting, short bursts of heat | Fine kindling first, then small logs | Light breeze to feed the flame |
| Log cabin | Long warmth, overnight | Medium to large logs | Little, shelter helps |
| Star fire | Low heat, cooking with few logs | Medium logs | None |
| Trench fire | Cooking, hidden light | Small to medium wood | Steady wind along the trench |
| Reflector fire | Warmth in open cold | Medium logs against a wall | Shelter from wind |

## Choose by need

- **Cooking:** trench or star fire, with coals spread evenly.
- **Warmth:** log cabin or reflector.
- **Signal:** a large teepee, built for visibility and kept bright.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In strong wind,** build a low layout such as a trench, and place a windbreak of stones on the windward side.
- **In rain,** raise the base on flat stones and cover the fuel stack with a tarp, leaving the flame side open.
- **In snow,** clear the ground to bare soil or a stone slab. Snow melts under the fire and makes the layout collapse.
- **On bare soil or sand,** dig a shallow ring to keep the heat in and the fire from spreading.
- **For a long watch,** build two small fires rather than one large one, so each can be fed without overheating.

## Common mistakes

- Building a teepee for overnight warmth, then waking to cold ash.
- Packing logs too tightly, so air cannot reach the flame.
- Placing the trench across the wind, which lets smoke blow back over the cook.
- Building a reflector wall so close that it scorches or cracks.
- Adding wet logs to a layout that was designed for dry fuel.

## Practice before you need it

Build each layout once in daylight on safe ground. Light it, feed it and let it burn down. Note how fast it catches, how much fuel it uses and which direction the smoke drifts. A layout you have built before is one you can trust in the dark.

## Troubleshooting

If the fire smokes heavily, the layout is usually too tight or the wood too wet. Open the structure, add dry kindling, and rebuild with the largest pieces at the base. If the fire keeps dying, check that air can reach the flame from at least one side, and remove a log that blocks the draw.

# A Winter Emergency Kit for the Car

A car stuck in snow or on a closed road can become a shelter, but only if it is equipped and handled properly. Cold builds quickly once the engine stops, and the most common winter vehicle emergencies come from running out of fuel, losing the way, or doing something unsafe with the exhaust. A small kit kept in the boot solves most of these problems before they become dangerous.

## Core Items to Carry

Pack these items in a container that stays dry and within reach of the rear seat.

- Two or three warm blankets or sleeping bags rated for low temperatures.
- Spare warm clothing, including a hat, gloves, a waterproof layer and thick socks.
- A shovel for clearing snow from the wheels and the exhaust pipe.
- A bag of sand, cat litter or salt for traction under the drive wheels.
- A torch with spare batteries and a hand-crank or small battery charger for a phone.
- Water, high-energy food that needs no cooking, and a small first aid kit.
- A reflective triangle or flares to warn other drivers, and a bright cloth to tie to the antenna.

## Fuel Care

Fuel is your heat source, so manage it deliberately.

1. Keep the tank at least half full throughout the winter months.
2. Check the fuel gauge before any long journey and plan refuelling stops on the route.
3. Ask the fuel supplier about winter diesel grades if you drive a diesel vehicle, because standard summer diesel can gel in extreme cold.
4. Keep a small amount of fuel in a sealed, approved container only if local rules allow it, and store it away from the cabin.

Running the engine uses fuel quickly. A fully fuelled car at idle can keep a cabin warm for many hours in the right conditions, but not indefinitely.

## Exhaust Safety

The exhaust is the most dangerous part of a snowed-in car.

- Clear snow away from the exhaust pipe every time you run the engine, and check it regularly while the car is idling.
- Keep the car's rear window cleared so you can see the exhaust area and check for blockage.
- Run the engine only in short, controlled periods, with a window cracked open on the side away from the exhaust.
- If the exhaust pipe becomes blocked by drifting snow, the gas can flow into the cabin.

If you feel drowsy, get a headache, or feel nauseous while sitting in the car, turn off the engine, open the doors, and get out into fresh air straight away.

## Staying With the Car

In most winter breakdowns the safest choice is to stay with the vehicle rather than walking through snow in poor visibility.

1. Tell someone your route and expected arrival time before you leave.
2. Stay in the car if the road is closed and help is known to be coming.
3. Use the hazard lights and tie a bright cloth to the antenna or a door handle so you are easy to find.
4. Keep moving your arms and legs and shiver if you are cold, which helps generate heat.
5. Only leave the car if you can see a building within a short, safe walking distance and you have dressed for the conditions.

## Making the Kit Work

Check the kit at the start of each winter, replace expired food and water, and test the torch and phone charger. Keep one person in the group responsible for watching the exhaust and the fuel level during any stay in the car.

# Heat Sources Compared

Choosing a heat source in a survival situation is less about which fuel burns hottest and more about which one you can run safely in the space you actually have. Every option trades off heat output, fuel weight, smoke, and the risk of poisoning yourself in an enclosed room. The sections below compare the five most common choices so you can match one to your situation.

## Wood

Wood is the most available fuel in most wild and rural settings, and it asks nothing of you except time and a safe place to burn it.

- **Efficiency:** moderate. Green wood wastes energy boiling its own moisture, so dry hardwood gives far more heat per load than soft, damp material.
- **Smoke:** high, especially with wet or resinous wood. Smoke carries fine particles and carbon monoxide.
- **Indoor safety:** poor unless the fire has a proper flue or a vent to the outside.
- **Best for:** open-air cooking, boiling water, and warmth in a ventilated shelter.

## Charcoal

Charcoal burns hotter and cleaner than wood, but it does so by producing a gas that is extremely dangerous indoors.

- **Efficiency:** high. It produces steady, intense heat for a long time.
- **Smoke:** low in visible smoke, but the burn releases large amounts of carbon monoxide that has no smell.
- **Indoor safety:** very poor. Charcoal fires have caused many deaths in closed rooms, tents and cars.
- **Best for:** outdoor grilling or a brazier in a well-ventilated open space.

## Gas

Bottled gas, such as propane or butane, is the easiest fuel to control, provided the equipment is in good condition.

- **Efficiency:** high, with fast response to adjustment.
- **Smoke:** almost none when the burner is working correctly.
- **Indoor safety:** acceptable only with a stove designed for indoor use and with a window or vent open. Incomplete combustion still produces carbon monoxide.
- **Best for:** cooking and boiling water with minimal fuss.

## Paraffin

Paraffin, also called kerosene in many regions, is a liquid fuel used in heaters and lamps. It stores well and is easy to carry in sealed containers.

- **Efficiency:** good in a properly designed heater.
- **Smoke:** moderate, and it leaves a sooty film on surfaces if the wick or burner is poorly adjusted.
- **Indoor safety:** only use a heater rated for indoor use, and never leave it unattended.
- **Best for:** longer-term room heating where a stove with a flue is not available.

## Solid Fuel Tablets

Compressed fuel tablets, often based on hexamine, are small, light and reliable for boiling water in a tiny stove or on a flat surface.

- **Efficiency:** low for heating a room, but good for a small pot of water.
- **Smoke:** low, though the fumes are unpleasant and can be toxic in quantity.
- **Indoor safety:** they should not be burned in a closed space, and the residue must never be eaten.
- **Best for:** brief cooking or hot drinks, especially when weight matters.

## What to Choose

Match the fuel to the room and the purpose:

1. Assess whether the space has any way for smoke and gases to escape.
2. If it does not, choose only solid fuel tablets for very small tasks, and accept that room heating is off the table.
3. If you can ventilate, a dry hardwood fire or a gas stove rated for indoor use gives the best balance.
4. For sustained warmth in a building with a chimney, a correctly maintained wood stove is the most reliable choice.
5. Store at least two days of fuel and keep the fire source separate from sleeping areas.

Plan around the worst case rather than the best one. A heat source that works only when conditions are perfect will fail when you need it most.

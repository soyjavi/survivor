# Fire Watch and Night Safety

A fire that is safe at dusk can become dangerous by midnight. Embers settle, wind shifts, and a log can roll out of the ring while everyone sleeps. Keeping a fire under control overnight means preparing it before dark, setting a clear watch schedule, and checking it on a regular rhythm rather than relying on memory.

## Preparing the Fire Before Dark

Set the fire up for a long, stable burn rather than a large, roaring blaze.

1. Choose a site that is clear of overhead branches, dry leaves and any shelter material.
2. Build a low, compact fire with larger logs that burn slowly.
3. Place a ring of stones or a metal barrier around the fire to keep logs from rolling out.
4. Stack a supply of dry wood close by, enough to last until morning, under cover if possible.
5. Set out at least two full containers of water, a shovel and a bucket within a few steps of the fire.

## The Watch Rotation

A watch works only if people know when it is theirs and what to do during it.

- Assign shifts of one to two hours, depending on how tired people are and how many are awake.
- Use a simple written schedule that everyone can see, with names and times.
- Make sure the person on watch is fully awake, dressed for the cold, and not cooking, drinking alcohol or using a phone screen that reduces their awareness.
- Hand over clearly. The outgoing person tells the incoming one the state of the fire, any problems, and what fuel is left.
- Wake a backup person if the watch is going to be disrupted or if the weather changes.

## What to Check During Each Round

Every round should follow the same checks so nothing is missed.

1. Look at the fire from a safe distance and note whether it is burning steadily, smouldering or dying.
2. Look for logs that have shifted toward the edge of the ring and move them back with a stick or shovel.
3. Check the ground around the fire for glowing embers, especially at the edges and downwind.
4. Feel the air and the ground for heat, and listen for crackling that suggests sparks escaping.
5. Check the spread of any smoke and whether wind has picked up.
6. Top up the water and move any wood or gear that has drifted too close.

## Handling Embers

Embers are the real risk overnight, not the flames.

- Spread out any large glowing coals so they can cool faster.
- Pour water evenly across the embers, stirring with a stick until they stop glowing.
- Do not rely on a thin layer of ash to hide a live ember, because a warm ember can reignite when air reaches it.
- Keep a check on the nearest shelter, and move bedding or tarpaulins well away if sparks are landing near them.

## Night Safety for Everyone Else

People asleep cannot respond to a problem, so plan for them.

- Place sleeping areas upwind and at least several metres from the fire.
- Keep a torch and a whistle at each sleeping place so anyone can signal the watch.
- Agree on a simple call for help, such as three blasts on a whistle, so the watch knows to wake the group.
- Keep children and pets inside the sleeping area, away from the fire ring.

## Putting the Fire Out at Night

If the fire must be extinguished before dawn, do it fully rather than letting it smoulder. Pour water in stages, stir, and test with the back of a hand until the ash is cold. A fire left smouldering in dry ground can flare up hours later.

# Fire in Wind and Storm

Wind is the single biggest factor in whether a small fire stays small. It feeds oxygen to the flames, pushes sparks into dry material, and can flip a calm camp into a grass fire in minutes. Burning in strong wind is often a bad decision, but if you must keep a fire going, the goal is to limit how far its embers can travel and to know when to stop.

## How Wind Changes a Fire

Understanding wind helps you predict where trouble will start.

- Gusts push flames sideways and lift burning fragments several metres away.
- Dry grass, leaf litter and resinous wood catch fire easily when hit by sparks.
- A fire that seems stable can flare suddenly when a gust hits the coals from below.
- Wind can shift direction quickly in storms, so the safe side of a fire can become the dangerous one within a few minutes.

## Choosing a Sheltered Site

The best defence is a place where the wind cannot reach the fire.

1. Look for a hollow, a rock face or a dense stand of bare trees that blocks the wind without creating an overhead fire risk.
2. Stay well away from dry grass, bracken and the base of trees with loose bark.
3. Put the fire on bare mineral soil or stone, with a clear circle at least three metres across.
4. Build a windbreak from stones, logs or a metal sheet on the upwind side, set just far enough away that it does not heat up.

## Sheltering the Fire

A windbreak helps, but it has to be built and maintained correctly.

- Keep the fire low and compact, because tall flames catch the wind more easily.
- Use larger, dense logs that burn slowly rather than many small sticks that flare.
- Keep the ring tight and check that embers stay inside it.
- Stack spare wood on the downwind side, not the upwind side, so you can reach it without leaning into the smoke.
- Do not build the fire against a tree, a shelter wall or a tent fabric, because heat and sparks will reach them.

## Watching for Spread

Check the fire and its surroundings frequently while the wind is strong.

1. Look for sparks landing beyond the ring and put them out at once with water or soil.
2. Watch the grass and leaves downwind for smoke, which is the first sign of a spot fire.
3. Listen for roaring, which means the wind is feeding the fire faster than you can control it.
4. Keep at least two buckets of water and a shovel within reach at all times.

## When to Stop Burning

Stopping is often the right decision, and it should happen earlier than you think.

- **Stop immediately** if embers are landing outside the cleared area and you cannot put them out fast enough.
- **Stop** if the wind has increased to the point where you cannot safely stand near the fire.
- **Stop** if the surrounding ground is dry grass, peat or deep leaf litter that can carry fire underground.
- **Stop** if you cannot keep the fire within the ring, or if a shelter, tree or building is at risk.

To stop, let the fire burn down, spread the coals, and drown them in stages with water until nothing is hot. Use soil only as a supplement, because wet earth cools embers better than dry sand.

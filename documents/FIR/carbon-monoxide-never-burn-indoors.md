# Carbon Monoxide: Never Burn Indoors

Carbon monoxide has no colour and no smell. It is produced by any fire, charcoal, gas stove, heater or generator that burns fuel. In a closed room it can build up to a deadly level within minutes, and many people die in their sleep without warning. This is the most important rule in any guide about fire.

## Why it matters

Carbon monoxide binds to the blood more readily than oxygen does, so the body takes it in and carries less and less oxygen to the brain and heart. The early symptoms feel like a cold, a hangover or simple tiredness, which is why people often miss them. Children, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with heart or lung disease are affected sooner. Because you cannot smell or see the gas, your senses cannot warn you.
## Why it builds up

Carbon monoxide forms when fuel burns without enough air. Closed rooms, sealed houses and tents trap it. It spreads through the air evenly, so the whole room is affected, not only the area near the fire.

## Never use indoors

- Charcoal burners, barbecues or braziers, even with a window open.
- Wood or gas cooking stoves not designed for indoor use.
- Petrol or diesel generators, including those in a garage or shed attached to the house.
- Camping gas heaters and lamps.

## Ventilation is not enough

Opening a window a little does not make an unsafe room safe. Carbon monoxide can still build up faster than air moves out. Use these fuels only outdoors, at least several metres from windows, doors and vents, and keep them upwind of the building.

## Alarms and where to fit them

A carbon monoxide alarm is the only way to notice the gas when your senses cannot. Choose the type that fits your situation, and place it with care.

| Alarm type | Power source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Battery-only | Replaceable battery | Easy to fit anywhere, but batteries must be checked regularly |
| Mains-wired | Household power with battery backup | Stays on through short cuts only if the backup works |
| Combined smoke and carbon monoxide | Battery or mains | One unit, but test both functions |

Fit one alarm on each floor, outside each sleeping area, at head height or as the maker directs. Keep it away from cookers and fuel-burning appliances, where normal cooking gases can trigger false alarms.

## Signs of poisoning

- Headache, dizziness and nausea.
- Weakness and confusion.
- Drowsiness and difficulty waking.
- Several people in the house feeling unwell at once. This is a strong clue to look for carbon monoxide, especially if a fire or generator is running.

![row of four boxes: step-box each, in order Headache, Dizziness, Nausea, Drowsiness, line-solid to one note beneath, warning beside it; label Several people unwell together; shared symptoms are the clue to poisoning, so get to fresh air.](assets/carbon-monoxide-never-burn-indoors-4.svg)

## What to do

1. Move everyone outside into fresh air at once. Do not stop to switch off appliances if that puts you at risk.
2. Call emergency help from outside the building.
3. Do not go back inside until it has been declared safe.
4. Seek medical care for anyone who was affected, even if they seem better.

If someone is unconscious, not breathing normally or cannot be woken, treat it as an emergency and call for help immediately.
## Adjusting for conditions

- **During a power cut,** the temptation to run a generator or light a stove rises sharply. Plan the indoor lighting and heating you need before the power fails, so you do not improvise.
- **In cold weather,** sealing a room for warmth also seals in the gas. Keep one small vent or window slightly open only when no fuel is burning.
- **In tents and vehicles,** never run a stove or heater inside, even under an awning. A car engine must never run in a closed garage, even with the door open.

## Common mistakes

- Placing a generator under a window or beside a door, even if the room is open.
- Using a barbecue in a porch or tent entrance because the wind seems to clear the smoke.- Ignoring an alarm that has been chirping for days because the battery is low.

## Troubleshooting

If an alarm sounds and you cannot see a cause, treat it as real. Leave, call for help and do not return until it is safe. A chirp every minute or so usually means a low battery or an end-of-life warning, which is different from the continuous alarm tone. Replace the unit or battery as the maker directs, then test it. If symptoms ease once you are outside, you still need medical care, because the gas may have been high enough to harm you.

## Checklist

- [ ] Fuel-burning appliances used only outdoors
- [ ] Generators and braziers kept several metres from openings
- [ ] Working carbon monoxide alarm fitted, if available
- [ ] Household knows the signs and the way out

## When it fails

If an alarm sounds, treat it as a real emergency. Leave, call for help and do not simply reset the alarm to silence it.

**Warning:** a carbon monoxide alarm is a useful extra, but it is not a reason to burn fuel inside.

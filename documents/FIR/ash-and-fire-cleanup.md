# Ash and Fire Cleanup

Ash can hold hot embers for many hours after the flames have gone. Cleaning up properly prevents fires, protects people and makes the ash easier to reuse. Treat every fire as still dangerous until the ash is cold to the touch.

![cross-section: ground-line across; coals buried under a crust below, pool of water-drop above, arrow-straight down into the bed, bracket marking the cooled layer; labels Grey crust, Embers, Water, Cooled layer; a grey surface does not mean cold underneath.](assets/ash-and-fire-cleanup-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Many repeat fires do not start during the night. They start the next morning, when someone stirs the bed, a gust of wind lifts the grey crust, or a child pokes a stick into what looks like finished ash. Embers hide under that crust, and they can stay alive far longer than anyone expects. Ash is also a poor thing to carry. A shovel of warm ash keeps its heat for hours and will scorch a bucket lid, a bag or the floor of a tent. Cleaning up is part of the fire, not an afterthought.

## Equipment and materials

- A bucket of water, or a bag of sand or dry soil
- A long-handled shovel or a sturdy stick for stirring
- A metal scoop and a metal bin with a tight lid
- Heat-resistant gloves

![labelled row on ground-line: bucket, shovel, tin, pot-with-lid, gloves; leaders to labels Water, Long shovel, Scoop, Lidded metal bin, Heat gloves; everything warm goes into metal, never plastic or paper. NEEDS: shovel, gloves.](assets/ash-and-fire-cleanup-2.svg)

## Cool it fully

1. Put the fire out with water or sand. See Putting Out Fires.
2. Stir the ash and pour more water on it until the hissing stops.
3. Hold the back of your hand near the surface of the ash. It must feel cold before you touch it.
4. Wait at least several hours, then check again before you leave the site.

## Step-by-step in detail

Work in order rather than rushing. First, make sure nobody is standing downwind of the pit, because steam and sparks travel with the wind. Second, pour water slowly from the outside edge toward the centre, so the ring of embers around the bed is soaked before the middle. Third, stir with the shovel and pour again, lifting the bottom of the bed so water reaches the hidden coals underneath. Fourth, stop when the hissing stops, then wait. The hissing only means the surface is wet. It does not mean the ash is cold.

## Collect the ash

- Scoop cold ash into a metal container with a lid.
- Store the container outside, away from the house and from any combustible material.
- Never put hot or warm ash into a plastic or paper bag. Embers can survive for hours and ignite the bag.

![site plan: pot-with-lid outside on ground-line, hut at left, shrub at right, arrow-both-ways to each; labels Lidded metal bin, House wall, Dry grass; warm ash must sit well away from anything that can burn.](assets/ash-and-fire-cleanup-4.svg)

## Reuse ash

- Wood ash can be spread on the garden in small amounts. It adds potassium but raises the pH of the soil, so keep it away from acid-loving plants.
- Ash can help control moisture in a latrine or toilet pit. Add a thin layer after each use.
- Ash mixed with a little water makes a simple scrub for blackened pots.

Do not reuse ash from charcoal, briquettes, treated timber, painted wood or plastic. Those residues can be harmful to soil and to anyone who handles the ash afterwards.

## Clean the fire area

Remove scorched stones or debris, and keep the spot clear of dry grass, leaves and overhanging branches. A bare, clean ring makes the next fire easier to build and safer to light.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In wind,** cover the whole bed, especially the downwind edge where sparks land first. Shield the pit with a few stones so the wind cannot scatter the ash.
- **On dry grass or leaves,** wet the ground around the ring before you start, not after. Widen the cooling zone.
- **After a large bonfire,** plan for a much longer cool-down, often a full night. Assign someone to check the site at intervals.

## Common mistakes

- Covering the pit with soil while it is still warm. Trapped heat can smoulder for a long time.
- Judging the ash by its colour. Grey ash can be hot underneath.
- Leaving after a quick splash of water. The hissing stops before the ash is cold.
## Troubleshooting

If the ash smells smoky hours later, it is still hot. Re-wet it, stir thoroughly and test again. If the ash looks wet but stays dark, heat may still be hiding in the centre. Probe with a stick rather than your hand, and treat any smoke as a signal to keep soaking.

## Checklist

- [ ] Water or sand applied to all of the ash
- [ ] Ash cold to the back of the hand
- [ ] Ash in a metal container with a lid
- [ ] Container stored outside and away from buildings
- [ ] Site checked again before leaving

## When it fails

If the ash is still warm after several hours, or smoke rises from it again, pour more water on it, stir thoroughly and repeat. Do not cover the pit with soil until it is fully cold, because trapped heat can smoulder for a long time.

**Warning:** ash from charcoal or briquettes can contain carbon monoxide-producing residue and must not be left in a closed space. See Carbon Monoxide: Never Burn Indoors.

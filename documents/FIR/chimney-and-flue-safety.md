# Chimney and Flue Safety

A blocked or cracked flue can fill a home with smoke and carbon monoxide. The flue is the passage that carries smoke and gases up and out of the building. Check it before the cold season and whenever the appliance is used heavily. Most problems are visible if you look.

## Why it matters

A flue works because warm gases rise and pull fresh air in through the appliance. When the passage narrows, cracks or cools, that pull weakens, and smoke spills back into the room instead. Soot and creosote build up gradually, so a flue that worked well last winter can fail this winter without any obvious change. Regular checks catch the problem while it is small and cheap to fix.

## Inspect

1. Look at the flue from outside for cracks, loose joints or damage at the top.
2. Use a torch and look up the flue from the base, if it is safe to do so. Never climb onto a roof in poor weather or onto a weak surface.
3. Check for birds' nests, debris and soot build-up.
4. Confirm the cap and chimney terminal are in place, undamaged and clear.

## Tools and equipment

- A stiff flue brush sized to the flue, not smaller
- Extension rods that join securely, with a rod to pull the brush back
- A drop sheet or old sheet to cover the appliance opening
- A torch, a dust mask and eye protection
- Thick gloves and, if needed, a helper to foot the ladder

## Clean

- Sweep the flue before each heating season, and more often if burning damp or unseasoned wood.
- Remove creosote, a black, tar-like deposit that can ignite and cause a chimney fire.
- Use a brush sized to the flue, pushed with rods from the base.
- Cover the appliance opening while sweeping so soot does not fall into the room.

## Seasonal routine

| When | What to check |
|---|---|
| Before the heating season | Full inspection from outside and from the base, then a sweep |
| Each week in use | Smoke stays up the flue, the fire draws well, no smell of smoke |
| After heavy use or a cold snap | Soot near the appliance, staining on the wall |
| In spring | Cap and terminal clear, damper and seals in good condition |

## Adjusting for conditions

- **In wind,** a flue can draw badly or push smoke back down. Keep the cap clear and watch for smoke entering the room on gusty days.
- **In rain or damp weather,** wet wood makes more creosote. Sweep sooner if you have burned damp fuel for several weeks.
- **In a cold snap,** the stove may be run hard for days. Check the flue more often and look for soot on the appliance and the room walls.
- **With a new appliance,** ask for the flue to be checked for the correct size and height before the first fire.

## Common mistakes

- Sweeping while the appliance is still hot, or without covering the opening.
- Burning softwood, wet wood or household waste, which deposit more creosote.
- Ignoring staining on the outside wall, which often signals a leak from the flue.
- Closing the damper before the fire is fully out, which traps gases and heat in the flue.
- Continuing to use the appliance after a chimney fire without a professional inspection.

## Troubleshooting

If smoke comes into the room when you open the door, the flue may be cold and not drawing. Check that the cap and terminal are clear and that no extractor fan is pulling air out of the room. If the problem continues, stop using the appliance and arrange an inspection. A fire that smokes heavily or keeps going out often points to the same underlying fault. Keep a record of each sweep, including the date and what was found, so the next inspection can compare.

## Checklist

- [ ] Flue inspected from outside and from the base
- [ ] Cap and terminal clear
- [ ] Flue swept this season
- [ ] Appliance connection sealed

## Stop using it

If you see any of these signs, stop using the appliance until the flue has been inspected and repaired. A poor draw is not something to work around by opening doors or windows. Once the repair is made, light a small test fire and confirm that smoke rises steadily before returning to normal use.

## When it fails

If a chimney fire starts, leave the building and call for help. Do not try to fight it from inside the house, and do not open the appliance to check on it.

**Warning:** a blocked flue can cause carbon monoxide poisoning. See Carbon Monoxide: Never Burn Indoors.

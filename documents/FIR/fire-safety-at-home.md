# Fire Safety at Home

In a crisis, more people use open flames, unfamiliar heaters and makeshift stoves. Most household fires start in the kitchen, from heaters or from candles left alone. The risk rises when power is out and normal routines break down, so safety habits matter more, not less.

## Why it matters

Normal homes are designed around electricity, gas and a steady routine. During a crisis those systems fail or become unreliable, and people improvise. A candle replaces a light, a camping stove replaces a cooker, and a heater is moved into a bedroom. Each change adds a new ignition source in a place that was never planned for it. Prevention is cheaper than any rescue, and most of it costs nothing but attention.

## Prevent

- Never leave a flame, candle or stove unattended.
- Keep flammable items, including cloth and paper, at least one metre from heat sources.
- Keep a bucket of sand or water and a fire blanket close to the cooking area.
- Keep a fire extinguisher where it can be reached quickly, and learn how to use it before you need it.

## Candles and lamps

- Place candles on a stable, non-flammable holder, away from curtains and bedding.
- Blow out candles before leaving the room or going to sleep.
- Refuel lamps outdoors, and never fill them while they are lit or hot.

## Smoke alarms

- Test smoke alarms each week if they are battery powered.
- Replace the batteries when the alarm chirps, and keep spare batteries in a known place.
- Check the batteries after every power cut, because they may have drained.

## Cooking in a power cut

Use a stove only in a ventilated room, never inside a tent or a closed bedroom. Put the stove on a stable, flat surface away from curtains and cloth. Keep children and pets out of the cooking zone, and never leave a pan that is heating oil unattended. Turn off the stove before you carry a pot away, and keep the lid nearby so you can smother a flare-up straight away.

## Escape plan

1. Agree two ways out of every room.
2. Choose a meeting point outside the house.
3. Practise the plan with children, and make sure they can open the doors and windows.
4. Keep bedroom doors closed at night, to slow the spread of fire and smoke.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **With young children,** keep matches and lighters out of reach, and practise the escape with them at night.
- **With older adults or people who need help,** agree who will check on them and how they will leave, before any fire.
- **In flats or upper floors,** check that windows open and that a rope or ladder is available where a stairway may be blocked.
- **In cold weather,** keep heaters at least one metre from beds, sofas and curtains, and turn them off when leaving the room.

## Common mistakes

- Using a barbecue or camping stove indoors because it is the only heat source left.
- Hanging clothes to dry over a heater or stove.
- Testing the smoke alarm and forgetting to check the batteries after a power cut.
- Keeping the extinguisher in a locked cupboard or a corner where nobody can reach it.
- Never practising the escape plan, so that nobody knows which window to use.

## Practice before you need it

Twice a year, run a short drill. Sound the alarm, count down from thirty and have everyone reach the meeting point. Ask each person to name both exits from their room, and to open the door and window without help. Practise using the extinguisher once on a safe test fire outdoors, so the first squeeze is not the first time you have held it.

## Troubleshooting

If an alarm chirps and you cannot find the source, replace the battery first. If it keeps chirping, or if the alarm sounds with no smoke, check for steam, cooking fumes or dust near the unit. A stove that smokes unexpectedly should be shut off and inspected before it is used again.

## Checklist

- [ ] Smoke alarms working
- [ ] Extinguisher and fire blanket in place
- [ ] Escape plan practised with everyone
- [ ] Candles and lamps stable and attended

## When it fails

If an alarm sounds and you cannot find the source, treat it as a real fire. Get everyone to the meeting point first, then investigate from outside if it is safe.

**Warning:** do not use water on a grease or cooking oil fire. Smother it with a lid or a fire blanket.

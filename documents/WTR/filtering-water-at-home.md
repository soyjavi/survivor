# Filtering Water at Home

Filtering removes dirt, leaves and particles. It does not make water safe on its own, but it makes boiling and disinfection far more effective, because microbes hide inside particles and cloudy water blocks heat and light.

## Simple layered filter

Build the filter in a clean container with a hole in the bottom.

1. **Bottom layer:** a clean cloth over the hole.
2. **Second layer:** fine sand, about 10 cm.
3. **Third layer:** coarse sand or small stones, about 10 cm.
4. **Top layer:** larger stones or gravel, to catch leaves and debris.

Pour the water slowly through the layers. The first litre will often look cloudy, so pour it through again if it is still dirty.

## Steps

1. Gather the materials and wash your hands and containers.
2. Let heavy particles settle in a bucket for an hour if you can.
3. Pour the clear top water slowly through the layers.
4. Collect the output in a clean container, then disinfect it.

## Commercial filters

- Choose a filter rated for **bacteria and viruses**, not only for sediment.
- Follow the maker's instructions for flow rate and replacement.
- Clean it as instructed. A blocked filter can harbour bacteria.

## Common mistakes

- Pouring too fast, so the water passes the layers without being cleaned.
- Using dirty sand or cloth that has been stored wet and smells sour.
- Assuming the output is safe to drink without further treatment.

## Checklist

- [ ] Layers clean and in the right order
- [ ] Water poured slowly
- [ ] Output collected in a clean container
- [ ] Output disinfected before drinking

## Replacing the materials

- Rinse or replace the cloth when it is very dirty.
- Replace the sand and gravel when the water flows very slowly or starts to smell.

## When it fails

If the water still looks cloudy after two passes, add more fine sand or let the water settle for longer before pouring. If the filter smells sour or rotten, rebuild it with fresh materials.

## Why it matters

Particles are the reason disinfection fails. Microbes cling to grit and organic matter, and cloudy water absorbs heat and blocks chemicals. A filter does not need to be perfect; it needs to remove enough material that boiling or bleach can finish the job.

## Equipment and materials

- A clean bucket or plastic bottle with the bottom cut away or pierced.
- A clean cloth, a coffee filter or a tightly woven T-shirt.
- Fine sand, coarse sand and small stones, all washed in clean water before use.
- A second container to catch the output.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Muddy river water:** let it settle overnight in a bucket, then pour off the top water. Filtering mud alone clogs the layers within minutes.
- **Cold weather:** water flows more slowly through sand when it is cold. Expect a longer run and do not force it.
- **Limited sand:** fine soil with the stones washed out can replace sand in a pinch, but check that the output runs clear.

## Filter for a household

A larger setup uses a wide bucket with the layers built in its upper half and a clean tap or cloth-covered outlet near the bottom. Fill it in batches, not all at once, so that each pour has time to pass through the layers. Label the outlet container so that filtered and unfiltered water never mix.

## Practice before you need it

Build the filter at home using dirty water from a garden or a puddle. Time the run, note how clear the output looks after each pass and record how many passes are needed. Knowing this in advance tells you how much water one filter can produce in a day.

## Maintaining the filter

After each use, rinse the sand and gravel with clean water and let them drain. Store the layers dry in a sealed bag if you plan to keep them. A filter left wet between uses grows odour and slime, which then pass into the next batch of water.

## Choosing sand

Use sand from a clean, dry source, washed well before use. Beach sand contains salt, which passes through the layers and into the water, so avoid it. Coarse sand from a builder's yard is often the best choice when it is free of clay and organic matter.

**Warning:** do not use ash or charcoal from a fire as a drinking water filter. It does not remove microbes. Unless the filter is rated for bacteria and viruses, the water still needs boiling or bleach.

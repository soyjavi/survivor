# Water After a Flood

Floodwater carries sewage, chemicals and dead animals. Treat all of it as unsafe, including water from wells and taps, until you have checked them. The first days after a flood are when people most often drink the wrong water, so slow down and follow the order below.

![Decision tree: decision at top, arrow-branch to four branches, rain or river for floodwater, bottle for sealed, ending in do, start-or-end and do-not; labels: Floodwater, Tap, Well, Sealed bottles, Drink, Boil, Disinfect, Discard; floodwater never reaches drinking and only sealed bottles skip treatment.](assets/water-after-a-flood-1.svg)

## Do not drink floodwater

- Never drink water that has been standing in or running through a flood.
- Do not wash food or dishes with it, and keep children out of it.
- Do not wade through it if you can avoid it. Cuts can become infected quickly.

> @image Photo of a flooded street with debris and a waterline on a garden wall

## Check the tap and the well

1. Do not use tap water until authorities say it is safe, or boil it first.
2. Wells can be contaminated by floodwater. Disinfect the well and test it before drinking.
3. If there is a boil-water notice, follow it for the full period.

![Well sequence: step-1 to step-3 step-box chain with arrow-straight, arrow-loop back to pump, decision at the test; labels: Dose, Wait, Pump until smell fades, Test, Drink; the test is the last gate before drinking, not the pump.](assets/water-after-a-flood-2.svg)

## Treat what you collect

- Use water from sealed bottles or containers that were never in the flood.
- Let water settle, then boil it or dose it with bleach. See Boiling Water and Disinfecting Water with Bleach.
- Use the cleanest water first for drinking, then for cooking, then for cleaning.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting a tap because the water runs clear.
- Filling containers from a tank or pipe that was submerged.
- Cleaning up debris with bare hands and skipping handwashing afterwards.

![Cleaning steps: bucket with four step-box stages joined by arrow-straight, warning tag on first container; labels: Scrub, Rinse, Disinfect, Dry, Flood contact; a flood-contact container is cleaned in full before rejoining the drinking store. NEEDS: red-tag](assets/water-after-a-flood-3.svg)

## Checklist

- [ ] No floodwater used for drinking, cooking or washing food
- [ ] Tap water boiled or declared safe
- [ ] Well disinfected and tested before use
- [ ] Containers cleaned before refilling

## Limits

Clear water can still carry microbes, and testing kits may not detect every chemical. Floodwater often leaves silt inside tanks, which hides contamination until the water is tested. Treat every source as suspect until it is confirmed.

## When it fails

If you cannot get clean water after a flood, look for sealed bottles and ask local services for supply points. If anyone drinks floodwater by mistake, seek medical advice and watch for diarrhoea or vomiting.

## Why it matters

Floods do not end when the water recedes. Mud, silt and standing water remain for days, and they carry the same risks as the flood itself. Clean water after a flood is a supply to be rebuilt, not a tap to be restarted, so the first week needs a deliberate plan.

## Protect your health

- Wash your hands with clean water and soap after any contact with floodwater or mud.
- Cover cuts with waterproof dressings, and clean them with clean water before covering.
- Watch children closely, because they are more likely to put their hands in their mouths.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Shallow flooding:** water may look less dangerous, but it still carries contamination. Treat it the same as deep water.
- **Cold weather:** wet clothing increases the risk of hypothermia. Change into dry clothes after wading.
- **Rural areas:** wells and livestock tanks often suffer most. Check them before the animals drink.

## Keeping track of the water

Mark every container that was in contact with flood water with a red tag, and keep it away from drinking supplies until it has been cleaned and tested. Write down the source of each new batch and the date it was treated. This record helps when authorities announce a new safety notice, because you can check at once whether a batch is affected.

## Troubleshooting

- **Tap water runs clear but smells wrong:** keep it for cleaning only, and wait for an official announcement before drinking.
- **Batch of treated water looks cloudy:** the silt has not settled. Let it stand longer, pour off the top, then treat again.

## Practice before you need it

Write down the steps for the first forty-eight hours and keep them with your emergency kit. Include where sealed water is stored, how to boil and how to dose the well. Practising the steps once with the household reduces mistakes when everyone is tired and anxious.

## Clearing debris safely

Wear gloves, boots and a mask when moving wet debris, because mould and sewage can be present in mud. Lift items carefully, rather than dragging them through the mud, and bag contaminated material for disposal. Keep children and animals out of the area until it has been cleared and the ground has dried.

## Working with neighbours

Neighbours often share wells, pipes and drainage. Agree a plan for who tests what, who cleans which tank and how results are shared. A single household treating its own water while the neighbours drink untreated water does not protect the street, so coordinate early.

**Warning:** flood-damaged containers, tanks and pipes can hold contaminated water for weeks. Clean them before reuse.

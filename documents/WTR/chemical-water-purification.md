# Chemical Water Purification

Chemical tablets and drops are light, cheap and need no fuel. They suit travel, emergency kits and situations where boiling is impossible. They work only when used correctly, and they do not remove everything, so treat them as a backup to boiling rather than a full replacement.

![Contact timeline: timeline-axis with timeline-marker at zero, step-box for shake, bracket over a wait bar, then do symbol at the end, bottle at left; labels: Dose, Shake, 30 min or more, Drink; drinking waits for the full contact time and cold water stretches it.](assets/chemical-water-purification-1.svg)

## Types

- **Iodine tablets or solution:** kill most bacteria and viruses. Not recommended for long-term daily use, or for pregnant people without medical advice.
- **Chlorine dioxide tablets:** kill most microbes, including some parasites. Read the label for contact time.
- **Chlorine (household bleach):** see Disinfecting Water with Bleach.

> @image Photo of a foil strip of iodine or chlorine dioxide tablets beside a clear water bottle

## Steps

1. Filter cloudy water first, because particles shield microbes from the chemical.
2. Read the package for the dose per litre and the water temperature. Cold water needs a longer contact time.
3. Add the dose, close the container and shake or stir as the label directs.
4. Wait the full contact time, usually 30 minutes or more, before drinking.
5. Keep the tablets dry and sealed until use.

![Dose strip: bottle left with line-dashed fill line, arrow-straight to pills in the middle, arrow-straight to table-grid cell on the right; labels: Litres measured, Dose per litre, Tablets needed; the tablet count follows the measured volume, not a guess.](assets/chemical-water-purification-2.svg)

## Common mistakes

- Adding extra tablets because the water still looks cloudy. More chemical does not speed up the process and can make the water taste unpleasant.
- Drinking before the contact time has passed.
- Using tablets that are past their expiry date, or that have been left damp.
- Treating very cloudy water without filtering it first.

## Checklist

- [ ] Water filtered if cloudy
- [ ] Correct dose for the volume and temperature
- [ ] Full contact time observed
- [ ] Tablets dry, in date and sealed

## Limits

- Chemical treatment does not remove heavy metals, salt or most chemical pollution.
- Cryptosporidium resists many chemicals. Use a filter rated for it, or boil.
- Expired tablets lose strength, so the dose on the label may no longer work.

## When it fails

If the water tastes or smells strongly of chemical after the contact time, do not add more. Pour it out, treat a fresh batch from the same source, or change source. If clean water still cannot be obtained, boil it instead.

## Why it matters

Chemical treatment is the fallback when boiling is impossible. A tablet in a pocket can turn a river into drinking water, which makes it valuable for walkers, travellers and anyone who may need to leave home quickly. The advantage is weight and simplicity; the cost is strict discipline with dose and time.

## Equipment and materials

- The tablets or drops, in their original packaging with the label intact.
- A clean bottle or container with a wide neck and a tight lid, marked with measurement lines if possible.
- A small clean cloth or coffee filter for pre-filtering.
- A timer or watch, since contact time is the most common thing people forget.
- A marker pen for writing the treatment time on the bottle.

> @image Hand holding a small foil packet of tablets next to a marked bottle and a wristwatch

## Step-by-step in detail

1. **Choose the cleanest source you can reach,** and filter it if there is any sediment.
2. **Fill the container** to the measured line. Guessing the volume leads to wrong doses.
3. **Check the label for the dose** for your water temperature. Some tablets need two doses for cold water.
4. **Drop in the tablet or measure the drops,** then close the lid and shake for the time the label says.
5. **Write the time on the bottle,** then leave it untouched for the full contact time.
6. **Smell and taste before drinking.** A faint chemical taste is normal; a strong one is not.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Cold water:** the chemical works more slowly. Use the longer contact time on the label or wait an extra thirty minutes.
- **Turbid water:** chemicals can be used up on particles before they reach microbes, so filter first, and only increase the dose if the label allows it.
- **Hot weather:** store the treated water in the shade, because heat speeds the loss of chemical strength.

## Troubleshooting

- **Strong chemical taste:** pour the water out and treat a fresh batch. Do not add more tablets.
- **Water still looks cloudy after treatment:** the chemical did not act on the particles. Filter again, then treat.
- **Stomach upset after drinking:** stop using the product, switch to boiled water and watch for signs of dehydration.

## Practice before you need it

Treat a litre of tap water at home with the same tablets, time the wait, then taste it. Do it once before a trip or an emergency, so that you know what the expected taste and timing look like.

**Warning:** never exceed the dose on the label. Excess chemicals can make people ill.

# Finding Water in an Emergency

When taps stop, look for water before you need it. Start with what you already have, then move to sources that need treatment. Searching takes energy and time, so plan the route and rest often.

## Water you already have

- **Water heater tank.** Turn off the power or gas first, then drain it from the bottom valve.
- **Toilet cistern.** The tank holds clean water. Do not use it if you added cleaning products or blue dye.
- **Canned fruit and vegetables.** The liquid in the can is safe to drink.

> @image Photo of a hot water heater drain valve with a hose attached

## Sources that need treatment

- **Rain.** Collect it from clean roofs into clean containers. Let the first flush run off before you collect.
- **Rivers, lakes and streams.** Collect upstream of people, animals and camps. Filter and disinfect before drinking.
- **Dew.** Wipe it off grass with a clean cloth and wring the cloth into a container.

![Dew collection: grass blades as shrub, water-drop on blades, arrow-curved dragging a cloth, then arrow-straight to cup; labels: Dew, Drag cloth, Wring, Cup; dew comes in small amounts, so wring the cloth often to fill a cup. NEEDS: cloth](assets/finding-water-in-an-emergency-2.svg)

## Sources to avoid

- Water near waste, livestock or industrial sites.
- Water with an oily film, a bad smell or dead animals nearby.
- **Sea water.** It dehydrates you faster. Only distillation makes it safe.

## Practical order

1. Collect from the cleanest source you can reach.
2. Let it settle, or filter it through a clean cloth.
3. Boil it for one minute, or dose it with bleach.
4. Store it in clean, covered containers.

## Common mistakes

- Drinking the first sample without treatment because you are thirsty.
- Using a container that held fuel, chemicals or milk.
- Drinking from a heater tank that has been turned on without first checking that the water is cool and clear.

## Checklist

- [ ] Power or gas off before draining a heater
- [ ] Source checked for smell, film and nearby waste
- [ ] Water settled, filtered and treated
- [ ] Containers clean, covered and labelled

## When it fails

If you find no clean source, collect rain or dew, and treat everything before drinking. If the only water is seawater or visibly polluted, do not drink it. Keep moving toward help, and ration what you have.

## Why it matters

Finding water early saves energy. Once thirst starts, decisions become rushed and sources get chosen for convenience rather than safety. A short search with clear rules keeps the energy for moving.

## Equipment and materials

- Clean containers with lids, at least two.
- A cloth for wiping dew and for filtering.
- A bottle or bucket with a handle, for carrying.
- A small pot for treatment, if you have fuel.

## Searching safely

1. Look for signs of water first: green vegetation, animal tracks leading to a low point, and the sound of running water.
2. Follow the low ground rather than the steep ground, since water collects in hollows.
3. Stop at the first clean source you find, rather than searching for the perfect one.
4. Mark the location so you can return to it.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Desert:** water collects in dry riverbeds after rain. Dig a shallow hole on the outside of a bend.
- **Winter:** melt snow only after boiling it, and do not eat snow directly.
- **Urban areas:** check pipes and taps for flow before assuming they are safe.

## Carrying water

Carry water in lidded containers, and use a strap or a handle rather than the arms, so that the load does not exhaust you. Fill containers only three-quarters full to reduce spills on uneven ground. Rest in shade when possible, and drink small amounts regularly on the way.

## Troubleshooting

- **Source looks clear but smells wrong:** move on. Smell is a better guide than appearance.
- **No safe source after a long search:** rest, conserve energy, and collect rain or dew.

## Practice before you need it

Walk the local area with a bottle and a cloth, and note where water could be found: a tap that can be reached, a local stream, or a rain gutter. Mark those places on a map and keep a copy with your kit.

## Protecting yourself while searching

Wear sturdy shoes, cover your skin against insects and sun, and carry a hat. Keep your route short and tell someone where you are going. Stop before you become exhausted, because a rest in shade is often more useful than another hour of searching.

## Mapping nearby water

Note each water source on a simple sketch of the area, with the distance and direction from your home. Write down which sources are reliable in each season. A map made in a calm moment is far more useful than a memory formed in a crisis.

**Warning:** do not drink sea water, even in small amounts. It increases dehydration and can cause serious illness.

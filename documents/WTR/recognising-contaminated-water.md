# Recognising Contaminated Water

You cannot see or smell most dangerous microbes. Some signs, however, mean the water must not be used, even after treatment. Learn to read those signs before you need to, so that a rushed decision does not become an illness.

![Checklist: five step-box rows each with do or do-not marker; labels: Smell, Colour, Surface, Life, Location; one failed sign rules the source out, even when the water looks clear.](assets/recognising-contaminated-water-1.svg)

## Warning signs

- **Smell:** rotten eggs, sewage, petrol, chemical or strong chlorine smells.
- **Colour:** brown, yellow, green or black water.
- **Surface:** an oily film, foam or floating debris.
- **Life:** dead insects, fish, or algae that forms a green mat.
- **Location:** water near waste, dead animals or industrial sites.

> @image Photo of three glass samples: clear water, brown water and water with an oily surface film

## When signs appear

1. Do not drink the water.
2. Move to another source if you can.
3. If you have no choice, filter and treat it, but use it only for washing unless it is the only option.

## Quick check before use

1. Look at a sample in a clear glass against white paper.
2. Smell it, holding the glass away from your face.
3. Let it settle for an hour and check the bottom for silt.

![Glass test: cup in centre, do marker above clear water, do-not above cloudy water, note about smelling after settling; labels: Clear, Cloudy or film, Smell after settling; judge in a clear glass, not a bottle. NEEDS: white-paper](assets/recognising-contaminated-water-2.svg)

## Common mistakes

- Trusting clear water because it looks clean.
- Drinking because the water was boiled, when the smell is petrol or chemical.
- Using a container that once held chemicals as a sampling vessel.

## Checklist

- [ ] No strong or unusual smell
- [ ] No oily film, foam or floating debris
- [ ] Colour normal for a clear source
- [ ] Source not near waste, carcasses or industry

## Limits

Clear water can still carry microbes, so treat all untested water. Heavy metals and chemicals may not be visible at all, and a test kit is the only way to find them. Visual checks cannot find dissolved lead or nitrates, so use a laboratory test when one is available.

## When it fails

If the water fails any check and no other source exists, use it only for washing or cleaning. Keep it away from drinking containers, and label it clearly.

## Why it matters

Most people judge water by looking at it. That habit fails with microbes, which are invisible, and with dissolved chemicals, which can leave water looking perfect. Learning the signs in advance makes the first decision in a crisis a calm one.

## Equipment and materials

- A clear glass or jar for viewing samples.
- A sheet of white paper for background.
- A clean, labelled sample bottle with a lid.
- A notebook for recording what you saw and smelled.

> @image Clear glass jar, white paper sheet and a labelled sample bottle arranged on a table

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Rain or snowmelt:** a sudden change in colour after heavy rain usually means runoff. Wait for the water to clear before using it.
- **Stagnant water:** still pools can look clear but contain algae or insect larvae. Treat them with the same caution as dirty water.
- **Night checks:** use a torch carefully, and check colour in daylight whenever possible.

## Choosing the sample

Take the sample from the middle of the source, below the surface, using a clean bottle that you rinse with the water first. Avoid the edges, where sediment and debris collect, and avoid the surface film. Label the sample with the place and the time.

## Troubleshooting

- **Sample smells of sewage after settling:** the source is compromised. Find another source, even if it is further away.
- **Sample is clear but a film appears on the glass:** this is an oily or chemical film. Do not drink it.

## Practice before you need it

Collect a sample from a known clean tap and a known stream, and compare them side by side. Record their colour, smell and settling pattern in your notebook. The comparison gives you a baseline that makes unusual water easier to spot later.

## Sensory traps

People adapt quickly to a smell, so a sewage odour that is strong at first can seem to fade after a few minutes of sniffing. Cold water also hides odour, so warm the sample in your hand before testing. Compare the sample with a known clean water, because your memory of clean water is often less precise than you expect.

## Reporting and sharing findings

Tell everyone in the household which source is unsafe, and mark it clearly with a sign or a stake. Write the date, the observed signs and the action taken. If a neighbour or a local service asks for information, share the facts you recorded, rather than your impression of the water.

**Warning:** fuel or chemical smells mean the water may be unsafe even after boiling. Do not drink it.

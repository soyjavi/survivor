# Water from Ice and Snow

Snow and ice can supply drinking water in cold places, but they must be melted and treated first. Eating snow lowers body temperature and wastes energy, and it can leave you more thirsty than before.

![Snow melting: pot-with-lid on flame, water-drop ring at the base, ice-cube added gradually, do-not on grey snow; labels: Starter water, Snow added slowly, Reject roadside snow; the starter water prevents scorching as snow melts.](assets/water-from-ice-and-snow-1.svg)

## Choose the right snow and ice

- Use clean, fresh snow from the surface of an open area.
- Avoid yellow or grey snow, snow near roads, and snow from roofs.
- Clear ice is better than cloudy ice. Sea ice must not be used, because it contains salt.

> @image Photo comparing fresh white snow with grey roadside snow and clear lake ice

## Melt it

1. Start with a small amount of water in the pot, then add snow gradually.
2. Melt it over low heat. Stir to prevent burning.
3. Once it has melted, bring the water to a rolling boil for one minute.

## Common mistakes

- Packing the pot with snow at once, which scorches the base and wastes fuel.
- Using melted snow that was left uncovered and collected dust or ash.
- Melting snow on a high flame, so the pot burns.
- Drinking the water while it is still lukewarm, without boiling it.

![Layered melt: pot with ice-cube layers stacked, water-drop ring growing at the base, arrow-straight upward; labels: Layer 1, Layer 2, Meltwater; a thin layer melts into little water, so small batches melt faster and waste less fuel.](assets/water-from-ice-and-snow-2.svg)

## Protect your body

- Do not eat snow directly. It takes energy to melt inside the body and can cause hypothermia.
- Keep the melted water warm in a covered container inside your clothes or sleeping bag so it does not freeze.

## Checklist

- [ ] Snow taken from a clean, open area
- [ ] Yellow, grey and roadside snow rejected
- [ ] Melted slowly, with a starter of water
- [ ] Boiled for one minute before drinking

## Limits

Melting snow over a fire needs a lot of fuel. Plan the water supply before the cold sets in, and keep a reserve. Melting in small batches uses less fuel than a full pot and keeps the water cleaner.

## When it fails

If the melted water tastes or looks wrong, discard it and choose a different snow source. If the fuel runs out, store melted snow in a lidded container and keep it insulated until you can boil it.

## Why it matters

Snow is common in cold regions but rarely a dependable source by itself. It takes a large volume of snow to make a small amount of water, and melting costs fuel and time. Knowing how much snow yields how much water helps you decide whether it is worth the effort.

## Equipment and materials

- A large pot with a lid and a stable base.
- A second pot or clean container for storing melted water.
- A spoon or stirring stick to prevent burning.
- Insulating material, such as a sleeping bag or thick cloth, for the stored water.

> @image Large pot filled with clean snow on a camp stove beside a spoon and a lidded bottle

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Very cold air:** snow is dry and fluffy. Melt more than you think you need, because dry snow yields less water.
- **Wind:** collected snow loses moisture and gathers dust. Collect from the leeward side of a hill or a sheltered area.
- **Ice:** clear lake ice yields more water per volume. Melt it in chunks, and cut off the cloudy parts first.

## Troubleshooting

- **Pot scorches while melting:** the heat is too high or there is no water at the base. Add a little water first and reduce the flame.
- **Melted water tastes of smoke:** let it cool and pour it through a clean cloth before boiling it.
- **Melted water freezes overnight:** keep it in an insulated container with a lid, and bring it to a boil in the morning.

## Carrying melted water

Once the water is boiled, let it cool and pour it into a lidded bottle. Wrap the bottle in cloth and keep it close to the body, inside a jacket or sleeping bag. Use the water within a day, because it loses its warmth and can become stale.

## Practice before you need it

Melt a pot of snow at home or on a camping trip. Measure the water you get from a set volume of snow, note the fuel used and check the taste. This simple test gives you a planning figure for a cold-weather trip or emergency.

**Warning:** do not melt ice or snow in a sealed container. It can burst.

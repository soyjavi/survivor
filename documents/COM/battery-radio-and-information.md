# Battery Radio and Information

A radio is often the only way to hear official instructions in a crisis. Phones and the internet depend on power and networks that may fail within hours, while a radio can run for days on a small battery or a few minutes of cranking. Choose a radio that does not depend on the grid, and learn the stations that broadcast emergency information.

## Why it matters

Official instructions are the most valuable information in a crisis: where to go, what to boil, which roads are closed and when the next update will come. Those instructions are usually broadcast first on radio, because a transmitter needs far less infrastructure than a website or a mobile network. A household that cannot hear the bulletins is working from rumour.

## Choose a radio

- A hand-crank or solar radio with a built-in torch is the most reliable, because it needs no spare cells.
- A battery radio works well if you keep spare batteries sealed and dry, and store them outside the radio.
- Check that it receives AM and FM bands. Short wave helps when local stations are off air, if you can use it.
- Look for a weather band if your country broadcasts one. Alerts can then sound even when the set is switched off.

## Equipment and materials

Buy one radio for the household, plus a simple backup that uses standard batteries. Gather these items in one place:

| Item | Why you need it | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Primary radio | Main source of bulletins | Works on both AM and FM |
| Backup radio | Covers a failure of the primary | Charged or fitted with fresh cells |
| Spare batteries | Power for the battery radio | Stored outside the radio, dated |
| Paper station list | Works without the radio | Written in pencil, kept dry |

## Find the stations

1. Write down the frequencies of the national and local public broadcasters before a crisis.
2. Note the station that gives emergency bulletins in your area, and the backup station if that one goes off air.
3. Learn the times of the regular news and weather broadcasts, so you can tune in on schedule.
4. Keep the list on paper, in the same place as the radio.

## Step-by-step in detail: a listening routine

1. Switch on the radio and tune to the emergency station, not the nearest music channel.
2. Listen to the full bulletin, from the opening date and time to the closing words.
3. Write down the authority named, the area covered and the instruction given.
4. Switch the radio off, and note when the next bulletin is due.

## Use it sensibly

- Listen at set times to save batteries, and keep the volume low.
- Turn the radio off between broadcasts unless you need it.
- Write down important instructions as you hear them, including times, places and named authorities.
- Share the bulletin with people who could not hear it, such as elderly neighbours.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Cold rooms:** alkaline cells lose power faster in the cold, so keep spares close to your body until needed.
- **Inside concrete buildings:** the signal may be weak, so place the radio near a window or on a higher floor.

## Keep it working

- Test the radio every few months, and rotate the batteries.
- Store the radio in a dry place, separate from the batteries, so a leak does not damage it.
- Keep a second, simpler radio if you can, in case the first one breaks.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the radio on all day.** It drains the cells before the important bulletins.
- **Using old batteries.** Test them before a crisis, not during one.
- **Trusting one station only.** Stations can go off air, so keep a backup frequency.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Silence | Dead cells or switch set to off | Replace cells, check the switch |
| Crackle or hiss | Weak signal or poor antenna position | Extend the antenna and move near a window |
| Station drifts off | Tuning knob loose or batteries low | Retune, then change the cells |
| Crank feels stiff | Dirt in the gears | Clean the gears and check the housing seal |

## Checklist

- Primary and backup radios located and tested.
- Station list written on paper and placed with the radio.
- Spare cells dated and stored outside the radio.
- Listening times agreed with the household.
- Notebook kept beside the radio for recording instructions.

## What to do when it fails

If the radio gives no sound, check the batteries, the power switch and the antenna. If it still fails, crank it for a minute to recharge the internal cell, or move to a window and try a different band. A printed copy of the official instructions remains useful.

**Warning:** a radio is only as useful as the information it brings. Check what you hear against what neighbours say, and see Verifying Rumours.

# Passwords and Accounts

When you cannot access an account, you cannot pay for food, prove identity or receive help. Keep access safe and recoverable. The aim is to make sure that someone in your household can get into each important account, even if the usual phone or device is lost.

![Recovery sheet: table-grid with table-header column; labels: account name, username, recovery contact, codes location, sealed envelope; the sheet holds routes back into accounts, never passwords. NEEDS: envelope](assets/passwords-and-accounts-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Accounts are now the keys to almost everything. Banking apps release cash, email resets other passwords, and government portals issue benefits, permits and medical records. In a crisis, a locked account can mean days without money, and a hijacked email can let a stranger reset every other login you own.

Recovery is slower than most people expect. Providers may need days to verify identity, and during a disaster their support lines are overloaded. Preparing the recovery route in advance is the only way to shorten that wait.

## Equipment and materials

- A sealed envelope for the recovery sheet, marked with the household name.
- A fireproof box for the envelope, passports and insurance papers.
- A password manager, or a notebook for passphrases kept in a separate locked place.
- Printed backup codes, generated while you are still able to log in.
- A spare phone number for recovery, held by a trusted relative.

## Make a recovery sheet

1. List each important account: bank, email, government services, phone, insurance.
2. Note the username, the recovery email or phone number, and where the security codes are sent.
3. Record which device or number receives the codes, and how to reach the provider's support.
4. Store the sheet in a sealed envelope, in a place known to the family.

## Step-by-step in detail: setting up recovery

1. Log in to each account from your usual device, and open the security settings.
2. Confirm that the recovery email and phone number are yours and still work.
3. Generate backup codes, and copy them onto paper in pencil.
4. Note the date of the check beside each account on the sheet.
5. Sign out of shared devices and remove any stored login you no longer need.
6. Place the sealed envelope in the fireproof box, and tell one trusted adult where it is.

## Passwords

- Use a long passphrase for each important account, made of several unrelated words.
- Do not reuse passwords across accounts, especially for email and banking.
- Use a password manager if you have one, and keep its master password written down in a safe place.

## Two-factor codes

- Keep backup codes on paper, not only on a phone.
- Keep the recovery phone number up to date, and check that it still works.
- Note which authenticator app or hardware key belongs to which account.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **No signal:** generate backup codes before a crisis, when a connection is available, and keep them offline.
- **Lost phone:** the paper codes become the only route in, so keep them separate from the phone case.
- **Shared household:** agree which adult handles each account, and record that choice on the sheet.
- **Older relatives:** store their codes with them, not with the younger family members, and check the sheet with them each year.

## Losing access

- Contact the provider through its official number or branch, not a number from a message.
- Keep proof of identity ready: a passport or national ID, and a utility bill with your address.
- Ask for the account recovery route the provider offers, and write down each reference number.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the password beside the account name.** Anyone who finds the note gets the account.
- **Letting the recovery number expire.** An old number cannot receive the code you need.
- **Keeping backup codes only on the phone.** A lost phone then locks you out.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Code never arrives | Wrong number or poor signal | Check the number and the spam folder |
| Login refused | Password changed on another device | Use the password manager or the sheet |
| Account locked | Too many failed attempts | Wait, then use the official recovery route |
| Backup code rejected | Code already used | Try the next code on the paper list |

## Checklist

- Recovery sheet written and sealed in the envelope.
- Backup codes generated and stored on paper.
- Recovery email and phone number tested this year.
- Trusted adult told where the envelope is kept.
- Provider support numbers written from official websites.

## What to do when it fails

If the codes do not arrive, check the number, the signal and the spam folder, then request a fresh code once. If that fails, use the paper backup codes or the provider's identity check. Do not keep trying, because repeated failures can lock the account.

**Warning:** never write passwords on a note with the account name next to them. Keep the sheet separate.

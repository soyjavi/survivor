# Amateur Radio Basics

Amateur radio, also called ham radio, lets licensed operators send messages over long distances without the internet or phone networks. It is one of the few systems that can keep working in a large-scale crisis, because it needs only a set, a battery and an antenna.

![Cycle diagram: radio, step-1 listen, step-2 transmit, step-3 listen again, arrow-loop back to step-1; labels: listen, transmit, listen again; the operator listens before every transmission. NEEDS: antenna, battery](assets/amateur-radio-basics-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Mobile towers fail when power or backhaul lines are cut. A radio operator with a battery set can still reach a club member across town, a relay station, or a distant operator who can pass a message on. Amateur networks have carried health and welfare traffic after storms and earthquakes for decades, which is why emergency planners value them.

## Get licensed

1. Find the authority that issues licences in your country and read its rules.
2. Study for the entry-level exam. Most countries grade licences, so start with the basic one.
3. Join a local amateur radio club. Clubs run courses, practice exams and mock drills.
4. Sit the exam, then choose a call sign as your licence allows.

Amateur radio requires a licence in almost every country. Never transmit before the licence is issued to you.

## Equipment

- A handheld transceiver for local contact, with at least one spare battery.
- A small base station that runs on a battery bank or solar panel.
- An antenna suited to the frequency band you use. A poorly tuned antenna wastes more power than a weak set.
- Coaxial cable, connectors and a simple power meter, so you can check the set works.

## Step-by-step in detail: setting up a base station

1. Choose a site with clear sky above it. A roof, a windowsill or an open garden works better than a cellar.
2. Mount the antenna as high as you safely can, and keep it away from metal gutters and power lines.
3. Run the coaxial lead in a single, gentle route, with no tight bends.
4. Connect the power source, and switch on the set at low output first.
5. Tune to the agreed frequency, and listen for a full minute before you transmit.
6. Make a short test call to a club member, and ask for a signal report.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Cold weather:** batteries lose capacity quickly, so keep spares inside a coat or a warm bag until you need them.
- **Rain and damp:** seal every connector with a weatherproof tape, and check the lead after each storm.
- **Hilly terrain:** a higher antenna position usually gives a wider reach, and a valley can block the signal entirely.

## Use in a crisis

1. Listen first, then transmit only when there is a gap in the traffic.
2. Keep messages short and clear, using standard procedures and phonetic spelling for names.
3. Use the frequencies your local club agreed for emergencies.
4. Repeat important details back to the sender, to confirm them.
5. Keep the radio and antenna protected from water, dust and physical damage.

![Flow chart: start-or-end, step-box listen, decision gap found, step-box transmit, step-box confirm, arrow-loop back to listen, arrow-branch no to listen; labels: listen, gap found, transmit, confirm, no; a busy channel means waiting.](assets/amateur-radio-basics-4.svg)

## Common mistakes

- **Talking over other operators.** Wait for a clear channel before pressing the key.
- **Skipping the antenna check.** A set with a broken lead appears to work, but it will not reach anyone.
- **Using a single battery.** Keep a spare charged and labelled.
- **Forgetting the procedures.** Practise the message format before it is needed.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | First action |
|---|---|---|
| No sound from the speaker | Volume low or squelch too tight | Adjust volume, then loosen squelch slightly |
| Transmit light, no reply | Antenna lead loose or cut | Check both ends of the coaxial lead |
| Weak received signal | Antenna poorly placed | Raise or reposition the antenna |
| Set cuts out under load | Battery voltage dropping | Swap in a charged spare |

## Practise now

Join a local club, listen to the regular nets, and practise sending short messages before a crisis. Drills show weak points in the equipment and in the operators.

![Form: table-header call sign, table-grid rows time, message, confirmation, do check beside confirmation; labels: call sign, time, message, confirmation; the message is complete only when the confirmation row is filled.](assets/amateur-radio-basics-5.svg)

## Checklist

- Licence confirmed and call sign noted.
- Set, antenna and lead tested on a local net this month.
- At least one spare battery charged and labelled.
- Log book, pencil and frequency list kept in a sealed bag.
- Club contact and meeting time written on the family plan.

## What to do when it fails

If you cannot reach anyone, check the power, the antenna lead and the frequency. Try a different band, move to higher ground, and listen before you transmit again.

**Warning:** only transmit when you are licensed to do so, and never send messages that could be used to harm people or property.

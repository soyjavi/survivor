# Digital Safety in a Crisis

Crises bring scams, fake messages and stolen accounts. Criminals know that people are anxious, short of information and eager for help, so they send convincing messages. Protect your devices and identity before the problems start, because it is much harder to recover an account in the middle of an emergency.

## Why it matters

In a disaster, normal checks disappear. Officials are overloaded, shops are closed and people are willing to pay for a seat on an evacuation bus or a bottle of water. Scammers exploit exactly that gap. A stolen account can also be used to message your contacts in your name, so one compromised login can harm an entire family or street.

Digital safety is cheap to prepare and expensive to repair. The steps below take an afternoon at home, and they save days of stress later.

## Equipment and materials

- A paper notebook for account names, recovery contacts and backup codes.
- A small sealed envelope and a fireproof box for the most sensitive papers.
- A second phone or a spare SIM for recovery codes, if your provider allows it.
- A printed list of official numbers, checked through the authority's own website.
- A screen lock and a backup charger for the device you use most.

> @image Person checking a phone beside a paper list of verified official numbers

## Protect your accounts

1. Use a strong, unique password for each important account.
2. Turn on two-factor authentication where you can, and keep the backup codes on paper.
3. Write down important account names and recovery details, and keep them in a sealed envelope.
4. Check which email address and phone number each account uses for recovery, and keep both current.

## Step-by-step in detail: a one-afternoon audit

1. List every account that holds money, identity or private messages.
2. For each one, confirm the recovery email and phone number still work.
3. Generate a long, unique password, and store it in a password manager or a sealed note.
4. Enable two-factor authentication, and save the backup codes on paper.
5. Log out of old devices and remove any you no longer use.
6. Date the audit and place the paper copy with the family plan.

## Recognise scams

- Messages that create urgency, such as a deadline, a fine or a threat.
- Requests for money, bank details or passwords, even from people you know. Their account may have been taken.
- Links to unknown sites, especially those offering help, aid or refunds.
- Calls or messages claiming to be from officials. Verify them through a known number, not the number they give you.

## Protect devices

- Keep devices updated when a connection allows, so security fixes install.
- Use a screen lock and keep the device with you, not in a shared bag.
- Do not connect to unknown Wi-Fi networks, or open unexpected attachments.
- Remove saved payment cards from devices you may lose.

## Keep data private

- Do not post your location, travel plans or family details publicly.
- Keep copies of important documents on paper and on a device, in separate places.
- Share only what a verified contact needs to know.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Intermittent connection:** update devices in short bursts when a signal appears, then switch to airplane mode.
- **Shared devices:** use a separate profile or guest account, and log out after each use.
- **Children and older relatives:** explain the scam patterns in plain words, and agree a simple rule that no one shares a code.
- **Stolen phone:** use the provider's lock or erase function, then change the passwords for the accounts it held.

## Common mistakes

- **Sharing codes.** A one-time code sent to you is never a reason to share it with anyone who asks.
- **Reusing passwords.** One leaked password then opens several accounts.
- **Trusting the caller ID.** Numbers can be faked, so check through a known channel.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Code never arrives | Wrong number or poor signal | Check the number, then request one fresh code |
| Account locked after failures | Too many attempts | Wait, then use the provider's recovery route |
| Unknown login alert | Someone else has the password | Change the password and sign out of all sessions |
| Strange messages sent from your account | Account taken over | Warn contacts and start recovery |

## Checklist

- Recovery email and phone number confirmed for each important account.
- Backup codes stored on paper, away from the phone.
- Screen lock enabled on every device.
- Official numbers written down from an official website.
- Audit date noted in the crisis log.

## What to do when it fails

If an account is taken over, use the provider's official recovery route, change the password on a clean device, and warn your contacts not to act on messages from that account. Keep a written note of the time and what happened.

**Warning:** official bodies do not ask for passwords or payments by message. If in doubt, stop and check with the authority directly.

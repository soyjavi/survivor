# Verifying Rumours

In a crisis, rumours spread faster than facts and can cause panic, bad decisions and harm. Check before you act, and before you share. A single message that says a bridge has collapsed or a shop is giving out free fuel can send a whole street in the wrong direction.

![Checklist: four step-box in a row, arrow-straight between, decision at each; labels: who said it, how do they know, confirmed elsewhere, useful; a report must pass every box before it is acted on or shared.](assets/verifying-rumours-1.svg)

## Why it matters

Uncertainty creates a vacuum, and rumours rush to fill it. People who are frightened or exhausted accept simple stories, especially when the story explains their fear. The same mechanism that makes a rumour convincing also makes it dangerous, because a family may leave a safe house, queue for fuel that does not exist or avoid a road that is actually open.

Rumours also damage trust. Once a neighbourhood learns that a warning was false, it is slower to believe the next true one. Careful checking protects the credibility of the people who give accurate information.

## Equipment and materials

- A notebook for recording each report, with its source and time.
- A printed list of official contacts and their numbers, checked from official websites.
- A battery radio tuned to the public station, for confirmed bulletins.
- A pencil, and a set of labels or stickers for marking unconfirmed items.
- A community notice board, where confirmed updates can be posted.

> @image Two neighbours comparing a handwritten message with a printed official notice on a community board

## Ask four questions

1. **Who said it?** Is the source official, or someone who saw it second-hand?
2. **How do they know?** Did they see it themselves, or were they told?
3. **Is it confirmed elsewhere?** Compare with the radio, official notices and people you trust.
4. **Is it useful?** If it is not confirmed, do not act on it or pass it on.

## Step-by-step in detail: checking a report

1. Write the report word for word, with the time and the person who gave it.
2. Trace the chain back: ask who told that person, and so on, until you reach a first-hand witness.
3. Check the report against the most recent official bulletin you have heard.
4. Look for a second, independent source that describes the same event in different words.
5. Decide, and label the report as confirmed, unconfirmed or false.
6. Pass on only the confirmed items, with the source named.

## Warning signs of a false rumour

- It is very dramatic, urgent, or designed to make you pass it on.
- It names no source, or the source is a friend of a friend.
- It promises something that official sources have not mentioned.
- It asks for money, personal details or a visit to an unknown place.

## What to do

- Act on official instructions first.
- Keep a note of unconfirmed reports, and label them as such, with the time you heard them.
- Ask the person who gave the report to check it with an official source.
- Wait for a second, independent confirmation before you change a plan.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Fast-moving events:** expect the picture to change every hour, and recheck older reports before acting on them.
- **Small communities:** a rumour can travel across a whole village in minutes, so slow the chain by asking for sources aloud.
- **Language barriers:** ask a trusted bilingual neighbour to translate the original wording, not only the summary.
- **Children in the group:** explain that a frightening story is not the same as a fact, and check it together.

## Common mistakes

- **Forwarding to be helpful.** A warning passed on without a check spreads the error further.
- **Trusting a familiar voice.** Friends repeat rumours in good faith, so check the claim, not the messenger.
- **Dropping the label.** Once an unconfirmed report loses its label, it looks like fact.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Two official sources disagree | One bulletin is out of date | Wait for the next bulletin and note both times |
| No official source available | Radio or phone down | Hold the report as unconfirmed and act cautiously |
| Rumour returns after correction | Old copy still in circulation | Post a dated correction where the rumour started |
| Panic spreading in a group | Unverified news shared quickly | Stop the chain, state what is confirmed, and set a time for the next update |

## Calm the group

Share what is confirmed, say what is not known, and tell people when you will next have news. A calm, factual tone protects everyone, and it stops people from making decisions out of fear.

## Checklist

- Four questions asked before any report is acted on.
- Each unconfirmed report labelled with a time.
- Official numbers checked from an official website.
- Confirmed updates posted on the community board.
- Corrections noted in the crisis log the same day.

## What to do when it fails

If you have already acted on a false report, correct it quickly and say what the confirmed position is. Tell the people who received it, and note the correction in your crisis log.

**Warning:** never share a rumour about a named person. It can cause real harm.

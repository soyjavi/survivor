# Two-Way Radio Basics

A two-way radio lets a group coordinate quickly over distance without relying on phones or networks. Radios are simple to use, but they are shared resources: every transmission blocks others, and sloppy procedure wastes time. Agreeing on channels, a short call format and a battery routine before you need them makes communication dependable when conditions are poor.

## Channels and Settings

Choose one channel for the group and set every radio to it before separating. Write down the channel number, the squelch setting and any privacy code, and keep the list with each radio. Keep the volume at a level that can be heard in noise, but not so loud that it drowns out incoming messages. Learn how to lock the keypad to prevent accidental transmissions in a bag or pocket.

1. Confirm each radio is on the same channel before leaving the base.
2. Test with a short check call while everyone can hear the others.
3. Note any radio that is weak or static-heavy and replace or relocate it.

## Call Procedure

Use a simple, consistent structure. Identify who you are calling, identify yourself, then give the message. Keep each transmission brief, then release the button and wait for a reply. Speak slowly and clearly, about a few centimetres from the microphone, and avoid shouting, which distorts the signal.

1. Say the name of the station you are calling, then "this is" followed by your name.
2. Pause briefly, then give the message in short, complete sentences.
3. End with "over" when you expect a reply, or "out" when the conversation is finished.
4. Confirm important information by repeating it back.

Use phonetic spelling for names and places, and say numbers digit by digit when accuracy matters. Avoid chatter that blocks others from transmitting.

## Battery Use

Batteries drain faster in cold weather, when transmitting constantly, and when the radio is searching for a signal. Turn radios off when not needed, and use the lowest power setting that reaches the group. Carry spare batteries in a dry, insulated pouch, and charge them fully when power is available. Mark each battery with its charge date so you know which are fresh.

- Keep a battery in a pocket close to the body in cold conditions.
- Replace any battery that gets hot, swells or loses capacity quickly.

## Range Limits

Range depends on height, terrain and the power and antenna of the radio. Hills, buildings, trees and metal structures reduce range sharply. Two people on flat open ground might talk over several kilometres, but in a valley or urban area the same radios may fail at a few hundred metres. Test the range in the actual conditions where you will use them, and plan a relay or a higher position if needed.

1. Move to higher ground for a wider signal path.
2. Hold the radio upright with the antenna vertical.
3. Keep metal objects away from the antenna when transmitting.

## Keeping Radio Discipline

Listen before you speak. Wait for a clear channel before pressing the button, and keep transmissions short so that others can respond. If two people transmit at once, both signals are lost, so stop, wait a few seconds and try again. Agree on a priority rule for urgent messages in advance, so that a critical call can break in without confusion.

Keep a written log of important transmissions, including the time, the caller, the message and the response. This simple habit makes it easier to check details later and to spot patterns in equipment problems.

## Maintaining the Radios

Clean the contacts and the antenna connector regularly with a dry cloth, and check the antenna for damage. Store radios in a padded case when not in use, and keep the battery compartment closed to protect the seals. If a radio gets wet, switch it off immediately, remove the battery, dry it fully and let it stand before testing.

**Warning:** Do not transmit near a person working with explosives, electrical lines or blasting equipment, since radio energy can trigger some devices. Keep the antenna away from your face when transmitting for long periods. Never assume a radio channel is private, since anyone with a compatible radio can listen. Avoid sending sensitive personal information, and keep emergency frequencies clear for genuine calls.

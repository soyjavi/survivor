# Signalling for Help

Rescuers are more likely to find you if you signal clearly and repeat it. Use signals that work in the conditions you are in, and make them visible from the air as well as from the ground. A signal that nobody sees is only wasted effort, so think about where you would be seen from.

## Why it matters

Searchers work from a plan, and a plan is shaped by likely locations and visible clues. A distress signal narrows a search from a whole valley to one clearing, and it can shorten the time before help arrives by hours. Signals also tell a rescuer that you are conscious and able to respond, which changes how they prioritise the search.

Signalling costs little energy when it is planned well. The aim is to be seen during the windows when searchers are most likely to pass, rather than to shout constantly and exhaust yourself.

## Equipment and materials

- A loud whistle on a cord, kept on the outside of your clothing.
- A signal mirror, with a sighting hole in the centre.
- A torch with a spare set of cells, and a red or white filter if you have one.
- Bright cloth in orange, yellow or red, folded small enough to carry.
- Dry tinder, matches or a lighter in a waterproof case.

## Sound

- Three sharp blasts of a whistle are an internationally recognised distress signal.
- Repeat it every minute or so, and pause to listen for an answer.
- Shout only if a whistle or horn is not available, because shouting tires you quickly.
- Keep the whistle on a cord around your neck, so you can reach it without searching.

## Light

- Three flashes followed by a pause is a common distress signal, repeated.
- Use a torch or a signal mirror. A mirror can be seen for many kilometres in sunlight, so aim the flash at the aircraft or vehicle.
- At night, a fire or a bright lamp attracts attention, but keep it under control.

## Ground signals

- Lay out large shapes in contrasting colours, such as a bright cloth on dark ground.
- Use a large SOS in snow or on bare earth, at least three metres high, with lines deep enough to show from above.
- Build three fires in a triangle as a distress sign, if it is safe to do so and the fires are well spaced.

## Step-by-step in detail: a mirror signal

1. Hold the mirror near your face, with one eye looking through the sighting hole.
2. Place your other hand in front of you, with the thumb raised toward the target.
3. Angle the mirror until the light spot falls on your thumb.
4. Without moving your head, bring the mirror up so the spot lands on the target.
5. Flash in a short rhythm, pausing between each set of flashes.
6. Watch the target for any sign of a response, such as a wing wobble or a change of direction.

## Conserve energy

- Signal from a visible, open spot, not from inside a shelter or under trees.
- Rest between signals, and keep a fire ready to light when aircraft or vehicles are heard.
- Plan signalling in short periods, so that batteries and fuel last.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Fog or rain:** sound carries further than sight, so favour whistle signals.
- **Snow:** bright cloth and dark ground contrast sharply, so lay out a large SOS with trodden lines.
- **Night and cold:** build a fire in a safe place, and rotate short signals with rest.

## Common mistakes

- **Signalling from a hidden spot.** Move to open ground before you start.
- **Stopping too early.** Rescue can take hours, so keep the pattern going.
- **Using a signal the rescuers do not know.** Stick to recognised patterns.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| No response to whistle | Wind or distance | Move to a higher point and repeat |
| Mirror spot not visible | Angle off the target | Shift the mirror slightly and re-aim |
| Ground sign hard to see | Low contrast | Add more cloth or dark stones |
| Fire smoke invisible | Fire too small | Add green material to make the smoke thicker |

## Checklist

- Whistle on a cord and within reach.
- Mirror cleaned and the sighting hole clear.
- Bright cloth folded in an easy pocket.
- Signal pattern agreed with the household before any trip.
- Fire kit kept dry and checked each season.

## What to do when it fails

If nobody responds, check the signal is visible from the direction of the search, then change position. If the mirror is not catching the light, adjust the angle until you see a bright spot on the target.

**Warning:** do not waste fuel or batteries signalling when there is no chance of being seen or heard.

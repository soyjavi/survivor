# Emergency Broadcasts

Emergency broadcasts warn of floods, fires, storms and other dangers. They are most useful when you know how to hear them, understand them and act on them. Many people miss a warning because they were not listening at the time, or because they assumed it applied to someone else.

![Ladder: ladder with three rungs, caution at bottom, warning middle, do-not mark top; labels: watch, warning, evacuation order; rising urgency, prepare then act now then leave. NEEDS: evacuation-order](assets/emergency-broadcasts-1.svg)

## Why it matters

A broadcast can reach thousands of people in seconds, which no door-to-door effort can match. The minutes between a warning and a flood or fire are often the only time you have to move, to gather the family and to take the medicines you need. A household that knows how to receive an alert gains that time. A household that does not may find out only when water reaches the doorstep.

Broadcasts also carry the authority of the official body that issues them. They name the area, the hazard and the action, which lets you decide quickly without guessing.

## Where alerts come from

- Public radio and television broadcasts, which often repeat an alert every few minutes.
- Official sirens or loudspeakers in some areas, usually with a recorded voice.
- Text alerts from the authorities, where mobile networks work.
- Local leaflets or door-to-door messages from officials.

## Understand the levels

- **Watch or advisory:** conditions could develop. Prepare now, and check your supplies and route.
- **Warning:** the danger is happening or about to happen. Act now.
- **Evacuation order:** leave immediately, following the route named in the alert.

Use the exact wording of the alert. It tells you what to do, which area it covers and when it ends.

## Step-by-step in detail: receiving an alert

1. Hear the opening tone or siren, and stop any task that makes listening hard.
2. Turn up the volume on the radio or the television, and move closer to it.
3. Listen for the issuing authority, the hazard type and the named area.
4. Wait for the full message, including the time it takes effect and the time it ends.
5. Repeat the instruction aloud, so the whole household has it.
6. Write the alert on paper, with the time you heard it.

## Act on alerts

1. Stop what you are doing and listen to the full message, not only the first sentence.
2. Note the area, the time and the instruction. Write them down if you can.
3. Follow the instruction in the alert, starting with any evacuation.
4. Tell others in the household, and check on neighbours who may not have heard.
5. Keep listening for updates, because the situation can change quickly.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **At night:** keep a battery radio on the bedside table, set to low volume, so the sound wakes you.
- **Hearing difficulties:** choose a text or vibrating alert, and agree a visual signal such as a light switched on and off.
- **Remote homes:** a weather band radio or a satellite messenger may be the only route, so test it before the season starts.
- **Multiple languages:** learn the key words for evacuate, shelter and danger in each household language.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for confirmation.** If the alert names your area and an action, act first and check after.
- **Listening only once.** Alerts repeat, and updates often change the instruction.
- **Assuming the warning is for someone else.** Check the named area against your address.

## Recognise fake alerts

Official alerts name a clear authority and a clear action. Be cautious of messages that ask for money, personal details or unverified links. Check with a second official source, such as a radio station or a known official number.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Phone gives no alert | Notifications switched off | Turn on emergency alerts in settings |
| Siren heard, message unclear | Distance or wind | Tune the radio to the public station |
| Alert repeats with a new area | Warning widened | Recheck your address and act on the update |
| Cannot tell if real | Message unfamiliar | Confirm through a second official source |

## Checklist

- Radio tuned to the public station and kept charged.
- Phone notifications for official alerts enabled.
- Named areas checked against your home and workplace addresses.
- Evacuation route written on the family plan.
- Neighbours who may not hear alerts identified in advance.

## What to do when it fails

If the alert system is silent, switch to the radio, then to neighbours who can hear it. If you cannot confirm an alert, follow the most cautious instruction, such as leaving a low-lying area, until you hear otherwise.

**Warning:** do not ignore an alert because you think it is a false alarm. Check it, but act first if there is any doubt.

# Message Relays and Runners

When radios fail or distances are too great, people on foot can carry written messages between points. A well-organised relay is slower than a radio but far more reliable than word of mouth, and it can continue in conditions that stop other methods. The keys are a clear message format, a designated route for each runner, written receipts and a plan for when a runner does not return.

> @image Two young runners in weatherproof jackets receiving a folded note from an adult at a rural crossroads, with a map on a board nearby.

## Organising the Relay

Start by deciding which points need regular contact, such as a base, a water source and a second group. Assign one runner per route and name a backup for each. Keep the number of runners small enough that they can be briefed in person, and keep the number of steps in each route short. Long routes are harder to manage and more dangerous.

1. Draw a simple map showing the points and routes.
2. Assign runners by age, fitness and familiarity with the terrain.
3. Brief each runner in person, repeating the route back to you.
4. Agree on the time window for each leg and the place where the next runner collects messages.

## Writing and Carrying Messages

Write every message on paper, using a standard form that includes the sender, recipient, time, place and a short text. Keep the text clear and use simple words. Fold the paper so the message is not visible from the outside, and write the recipient's name on the outside. Use a waterproof pouch or sealed bag where rain is likely.

- Number each message so that gaps can be spotted.
- Keep a copy of every message at the sending point.
- Never carry more than one message per runner at a time unless the route is safe and short.

## Routes and Timing

Choose routes that avoid hazards such as deep water, unstable ground, traffic and unknown people. Prefer paths the runner already knows, and mark the route with simple landmarks. Estimate travel time generously, then add a margin for delays. Have the runner report at fixed intervals, and treat a missed report as a reason to check, not as an emergency in itself.

1. Walk the route in daylight before relying on it at night.
2. Note turning points, crossings and any shelter along the way.
3. Set a return time and a point to report back if the route is blocked.

## Receipts and Records

A message is not complete until the recipient confirms it. Give each recipient a slip to sign with the time received, and have the runner bring that slip back. Keep a log at the sending point showing each message, the runner, the departure time and the receipt time. Unanswered messages should be followed up by a second runner after a set period.

- Write the receipt time as well as the date.
- Compare the log each evening to find overdue messages.
- Destroy sensitive messages once they have been delivered and logged, if the situation requires it.

## Runner Safety

Runners are at risk from weather, injury, fatigue and people who may seek their supplies or messages. Each runner should carry water, a whistle, a small first aid kit, a torch and a copy of the route. Limit the hours a runner works in heat or cold, and rotate duties so that no one becomes exhausted. A runner who feels unsafe must be allowed to stop and report.

## Handling Dropped or Delayed Messages

Messages get lost. Plan for it by sending a copy by a second route when the content is important, or by asking the recipient to confirm receipt through another runner. Mark each message with its sending time, so that a delay can be measured and reported. If a message is not confirmed within the agreed window, send a short note asking whether it was received, rather than resending the full text.

Keep runners informed about changes. When a route is closed or a point has moved, send the updated instructions in writing and confirm that each runner has them. A runner who sets out on an old route wastes time and may be exposed to danger.

## Rotating and Resting Runners

Limit each runner to a fixed number of trips per day and make sure they rest between them. Rotate routes so that no runner always takes the most demanding path. Provide food and water at the start and end of each shift, and check on runners who return looking unwell or exhausted.

**Warning:** Never send a runner alone into unknown areas, across fast water, or onto roads at night without a light and reflective material. Do not let children run long or hazardous routes. If a runner fails to return, send a search party only after a clear plan is in place, and do not put more people at risk than the situation requires.

# Burns

Burns cause pain, infection and fluid loss. Cool the burn quickly and cover it. The deeper and larger the burn, the more fluid the body loses, so early cooling and careful covering both matter.

## Why it matters

A burn keeps damaging tissue for as long as heat remains in it, so cooling is the single most useful step you can take in the first minutes. Burns also open the skin to bacteria, and large burns can make a person cold and weak through fluid loss. Acting early reduces both the depth of the injury and the chance of a complication, and it often lessens the pain significantly.

## Burn depth

- **Superficial:** red and painful, with no blisters. Usually heals in a few days.
- **Partial thickness:** blistered, wet and very painful. Heals in weeks and may scar.
- **Full thickness:** white, leathery or charred, and sometimes numb. Needs medical care.

## First steps

1. Remove the person from the heat source. Protect yourself from smoke or hot substances.
2. Cool the burn with cool running water for at least 20 minutes. If running water is not available, use clean, cool water.
3. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn, unless they stick to it.
4. Cover the burn loosely with a clean non-fluffy dressing or cling film.

## Step-by-step in detail

Hold the burned area under the running water, or lower it into a basin of cool water, without rubbing. Keep it there for the full period even if the pain eases, because the heat may still be in the deeper layers. Once cooled, lift the dressing material onto the burn from the edge, so it does not drag across the surface. Check the colour and the blistering again after cooling, and note the time the burn happened.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** their skin is thinner and they lose heat more easily, so keep them warm after cooling and seek care sooner.
- **Older adults:** thin skin and slower healing raise the risk, so lower your threshold for seeking care.
- **Chemical burns:** flush with plenty of water and keep flushing while you prepare the person for transport.
- **Electrical burns:** make sure the power is off before touching the person, and seek care even if the burn looks small.

## Do not

- Do not use ice or very cold water. It can cause more damage and drop body temperature.
- Do not apply butter, oil, toothpaste or other home remedies.
- Do not burst blisters.
- Do not remove stuck clothing.

## Materials

- Clean running water or a clean container of cool water.
- Non-fluffy dressings, cling film or a clean sheet.
- Gloves, if available, for your own protection.
- A blanket to keep the person warm after cooling.

## Common mistakes

- Cooling for only a few seconds instead of about 20 minutes.
- Wrapping the burn tightly, which causes swelling to press on the skin.
- Covering with fluffy cotton that sticks to the wound.
- Forgetting that the person may be cold or in shock.
- Lifting a cooled dressing so quickly that it tears the blisters.

## Troubleshooting

If the water runs out, switch to any clean water you can find and keep cooling. If the dressing sticks, soak it gently in clean water before removing it, and do not pull. If the skin turns dark or the person says they cannot feel the burn, seek care at once, because deeper damage may be present.

## Checklist

- Source of heat removed.
- Burn cooled for at least 20 minutes.
- Jewellery and tight clothing removed where safe.
- Loose, clean dressing in place.
- Person warm and watched.

## Practice before you need it

Know where the nearest tap or clean water supply is in your home, and keep a clean sheet or cling film in the kitchen. Teach each adult in the household how long twenty minutes feels when standing at a sink, so that nobody stops early.

## When it fails

If the burn gets worse, becomes white or black, or the person becomes confused or pale, seek help at once. If cooling water runs out, use whatever clean water you have and keep cooling for as long as possible. If blisters burst on their own, clean gently and cover again.

## Serious burns

Seek medical care for burns that are:

- Larger than the palm of the hand.
- On the face, hands, feet, genitals or a major joint.
- Deep, white, leathery or charred.
- Caused by electricity or chemicals.
- In a child or an older person.

## Fluids

Large burns cause fluid loss. Give small sips of clean water if the person is awake and not vomiting. Keep them warm.

**Warning:** burns on the airway from smoke or steam can close the throat quickly. Seek help at once if breathing is hard or the voice is hoarse.

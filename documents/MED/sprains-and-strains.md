# Sprains and Strains

A sprain stretches or tears a ligament. A strain affects a muscle or tendon. Both are common after falls, lifting and long walks. Most heal well with simple care in the first two days, followed by gradual return to movement.

## Why it matters

Sprains and strains are among the most common injuries in households, and they are easy to underestimate. A joint that is left to swell, or a muscle that is pushed too early, can take much longer to recover. The first two days set the pace of healing: controlled swelling and gentle movement help tissues repair, while heavy loading too soon can reopen the injury. Because a sprain can feel like a fracture, it is important to check carefully before assuming it is only soft tissue.

## RICE

1. **Rest.** Stop using the joint or muscle.
2. **Ice.** Apply a cold pack or cold water wrapped in cloth for 15 to 20 minutes, several times a day for the first two days. Never place ice directly on the skin.
3. **Compression.** Wrap the area with a soft bandage, firm but not tight.
4. **Elevation.** Raise the limb above the heart when resting.

## Step-by-step in detail

Begin by sitting the person down in a comfortable position, with the injured area supported on a pillow. Remove shoes or tight jewellery before swelling begins. Wrap the cold pack in a thin cloth, place it over the swollen area, and set a timer. Remove it when the time is up, and let the skin return to normal before reapplying. Start the bandage at the far end of the limb and work toward the heart, overlapping each turn by about half its width.

## Check for a fracture

If the person cannot bear weight, the joint looks deformed, or there is severe pain over a bone, treat it as a fracture. See Fractures and Splinting.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** growth plates can be injured in a way that looks like a sprain, so seek review for any significant injury.
- **Older adults:** falls can cause hidden injuries, so check the whole body and the head.
- **People on blood thinners:** bruising can be extensive, so watch for growing swelling and seek advice.
- **Ankles and knees:** if the joint feels unstable or gives way, protect it and seek medical review.

## Return to activity

- Start gentle movement once pain allows.
- Increase activity slowly over several days.
- Stop if pain returns.

## Materials

- A soft elastic bandage.
- A cold pack, or a bag of cold water wrapped in cloth.
- A pillow or folded blanket for elevation.
- A notebook to record pain and swelling each day.

## Common mistakes

- Putting ice directly on the skin, which can cause frostbite.
- Wrapping the bandage so tightly that the foot or hand loses colour.
- Returning to heavy activity too soon.
- Ignoring the swelling that keeps growing.
- Walking on an injured ankle to "test" it repeatedly.

## Troubleshooting

If the bandage slips, rewrap it from the far end, keeping the same firm but comfortable tension. If the skin under the bandage itches or feels tight, loosen it slightly. If the pain returns during gentle movement, go back a step and rest for a day before trying again.

## Checklist

- Rest started at once.
- Ice applied for 15 to 20 minutes at a time.
- Bandage firm but not tight.
- Limb raised when resting.
- Pain and swelling noted each day.

## Practice before you need it

Learn to apply a figure-of-eight wrap on a family member's ankle while they are well, so you know how much tension is comfortable. Keep a cold pack in the freezer, and check that the elastic bandage is not perished.

## When it fails

If the swelling keeps growing after the first day, or pain gets worse rather than better, seek medical review. If the person cannot walk, or the joint feels unstable, treat it as a possible fracture. If the bandage causes numbness or colour change, loosen it at once.

## Watch for

- Numbness, tingling, or a cold or blue foot or hand.
- Swelling that keeps growing after the first day.

**Warning:** check the bandage. If the toes or fingers become pale, blue, numb or cold, loosen it straight away.

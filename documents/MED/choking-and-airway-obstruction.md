# Choking and Airway Obstruction

A blocked airway can stop breathing in minutes. Act quickly, but avoid harming the person. Most choking can be cleared with a few firm, well-placed blows if the person is still conscious.

## Why it matters

A partly blocked airway still lets some air pass, and the person may be able to cough. A fully blocked airway lets none pass, and oxygen runs out quickly. Telling these two situations apart is the key skill. Overconfident interventions can push an object further in, while hesitation lets a complete blockage become a crisis. Calm, clear steps help you move between the two without losing time.

## Signs

- Unable to speak, cough or breathe.
- Hands clutching the throat.
- Blue lips or face.

If the person can cough forcefully or speak, encourage them to keep coughing. A strong cough clears the airway better than any manoeuvre.

![Decision flow: decision, arrow-branch to do (encourage cough) and to warning (act now); labels: coughing and speaking, silent and unable to breathe; a strong cough is always the first option.](assets/choking-and-airway-obstruction-2.svg)

## Adult or child over one year

1. Give up to five sharp back blows between the shoulder blades, with the heel of one hand.
2. If the object does not come out, give up to five abdominal thrusts. Stand behind the person, place a fist above the navel, and pull inwards and upwards.
3. Alternate back blows and thrusts until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive.

![Schematic: person-standing torso outline, fist icon with other hand over it, leader to navel, do-not mark on ribs; labels: above navel, not ribs; the thrust point is above the navel. NEEDS: fist](assets/choking-and-airway-obstruction-3.svg)

## Step-by-step in detail

Lean the person forward so that gravity helps the object move, and support their chest with your non-striking hand. Aim the blows at the space between the shoulder blades, not at the spine or neck. For abdominal thrusts, keep your arms wrapped around the upper abdomen, pull in a quick, firm movement, and release the pressure between thrusts.

## Infant under one year

- Lay the infant face down along your forearm, with the head lower than the chest.
- Give up to five firm back blows.
- Turn the infant face up and give up to five chest thrusts with two fingers in the centre of the chest.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Pregnancy or obesity:** chest thrusts replace abdominal thrusts, placed on the lower half of the breastbone.
- **Adults who cannot stand:** support them as they sit or lie, keep following the same steps, and start CPR if they become unresponsive.
- **Children:** the older-child method applies from one year of age. Stay with them and keep the rhythm steady.

## Materials

- None required. Your hands and a firm surface are enough.
- Keep a torch or light nearby if the person is in a dark place, so you can see the mouth.

## Common mistakes

- Giving abdominal thrusts to an infant.
- Pulling the person forward in the wrong direction when giving back blows.
- Hitting the back too gently, which does not dislodge the object.
- Pausing too long between cycles.
- Reaching into the mouth without a clear view of the object.

## Troubleshooting

If the blows produce a cough but nothing else, encourage more coughing and keep watching. If the person gasps and can speak again, stop the manoeuvres and keep them sitting, then arrange a medical check, because the airway may still be irritated. If nothing changes after several cycles, call for help and keep the cycle going while help arrives.

## Checklist

- Cough encouraged while the person can still cough.
- Five back blows, then five thrusts.
- Infant handled with head lower than chest.
- Mouth checked after each cycle, but no blind sweeps.
- Help called if the object does not move quickly.

## Practice before you need it

Rehearse the positions on a cushion or with a willing adult, using gentle, controlled movements only. Learn the difference between an infant and a child position so that you do not stop to think during a real event.

## When it fails

If the object does not come out and the person becomes unresponsive, lower them to the ground and start CPR. Check the mouth before each set of breaths, and remove the object only if you can see it clearly.

## Unresponsive

If the person becomes unconscious, lower them to the ground and start CPR. See Basic CPR.

**Warning:** never do blind finger sweeps in the mouth. You may push the object further in.

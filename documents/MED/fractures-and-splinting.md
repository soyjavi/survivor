# Fractures and Splinting

A broken bone must be kept still. Moving it can damage nerves, blood vessels and muscles. A good splint is not about perfect alignment; it is about stopping movement and keeping the limb warm and pink.

## Why it matters

Movement at a fracture grinds bone ends against soft tissue, causes pain that makes the person tense, and can turn a closed break into an open one. Splinting before transport reduces both the injury and the distress. Because many fractures are hard to tell from a bad sprain, treat any suspected break as a fracture until a clinician has examined it. Splinting is a temporary measure, and the aim is to hold the limb steady, not to fix the bone.

## Signs of a fracture

- Pain, swelling and bruising at the site.
- An unnatural angle or shape.
- Difficulty moving the limb or bearing weight.
- A grating sensation when moved, which you should not try to provoke.

## Splint the limb

1. Support the injured limb in the position it is found. Do not straighten it by force.
2. Pad the limb with cloth or padding.
3. Place a rigid support, such as a board, rolled newspaper or a stick, on each side of the joint above and below the break.
4. Bind the splint with bandages above and below the break. Tie the knot on the side of the splint, not over the break.
5. Check the fingers or toes after binding. They should be warm, pink and able to move.

## Step-by-step in detail

Ask the person to stay still and tell you where it hurts most. Support the limb above and below the break with your hands while another helper places the padding. Slide the padding into position rather than lifting the limb, so that the bone is not jolted. Place the rigid supports, then bind the bandages one at a time, starting above the break and working downward, keeping each turn snug but not tight. Lower the limb gently onto a pillow or folded blanket once bound.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** their bones can bend rather than snap, so a deformity may be less obvious. Treat any suspected break as a fracture.
- **Older adults:** brittle bones break more easily, and a fall may hide other injuries, so check the whole body.
- **Limbs with swelling:** bandages may need loosening as swelling develops over the next hours.
- **Hands and feet:** check the colour and movement of each digit more often, because swelling builds quickly in these areas.

## Materials

- Rigid supports: a flat board, a folded blanket, a rolled magazine or a stick.
- Padding such as cloth, towels or cotton.
- Bandages or strips of clean cloth to bind the splint.
- Scissors to cut clothing, if needed.

## Open fractures

If the bone has broken through the skin, cover the wound with a clean dressing and control the bleeding first. Do not push the bone back in.

## Spine

If a neck or back injury is possible, keep the person still and do not move them unless they are in danger.

## Common mistakes

- Binding the splint so tightly that circulation is cut off.
- Moving the joint above or below the break before splinting.
- Using a splint that is too short to stop movement.
- Tying the knot over the fracture site.
- Letting the person walk on the limb to test it.

## Troubleshooting

If the fingers or toes feel cold or look grey, loosen the bandages first, then reassess. If the splint slides, add padding in the gap and rebind firmly enough to hold. If the person feels a new numbness or pain that spreads, check the bandages again and seek urgent care.

## Checklist

- Limb supported in its natural position.
- Padding in place.
- Splint covers the joints above and below the break.
- Bandages firm, not tight.
- Fingers or toes warm and moving.

## Practice before you need it

Practise splinting a pillow or a rolled blanket with a partner, so that you become used to supporting the limb while someone else binds. Keep the rigid supports and bandages together in one place, so they can be grabbed quickly.

## When it fails

If the fingers or toes turn pale, blue or numb after splinting, loosen the bandages straight away and check again. If the bandages cannot be loosened enough, remove the splint and rebind it more loosely. If the person has an open fracture and bleeding that does not stop, follow Controlling Bleeding and seek urgent care.

**Warning:** if the fingers or toes turn pale, blue or numb after splinting, loosen the bandages.

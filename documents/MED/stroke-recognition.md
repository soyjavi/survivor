# Stroke: Recognising the Signs

A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is interrupted, either by a blocked artery or by bleeding. Brain cells begin to die within minutes, and the sooner blood flow is restored, the more function can be saved. The difficulty is that a stroke can look like many other conditions, such as fainting, a migraine, or intoxication. A simple recognition test lets you act quickly without needing to diagnose the cause.

## The recognition test

Use a short sequence of checks. Each one takes only a few seconds, and any single abnormal result is enough to call for help.

1. **Face:** Ask the person to smile. Look for drooping on one side, where the smile is lopsided or one side does not move.
2. **Arms:** Ask the person to raise both arms and hold them out. Look for one arm that drifts downward or cannot be lifted.
3. **Speech:** Ask the person to repeat a simple sentence. Listen for slurred, garbled, or missing words, or an inability to speak at all.
4. **Time:** If any of the above is abnormal, call emergency services at once and note the exact time the symptoms started or the time the person was last seen well.

**Warning:** Do not wait to see whether the symptoms improve. Stroke treatment works best when it starts early, and symptoms that disappear quickly may still need urgent assessment.

## Other signs to notice

Stroke does not always produce the classic three signs. Also watch for:

- Sudden numbness or weakness in one leg or one side of the body.
- Sudden loss of vision in one or both eyes, or double vision.
- Sudden confusion, difficulty understanding what is said, or unusual drowsiness.
- Sudden loss of balance, dizziness, or difficulty walking.
- A sudden, severe headache that is unlike any previous headache.
- Loss of consciousness or seizure.

Any of these, especially when they come on suddenly, is a reason to call for help, even if the person seems to recover.

## What to do while waiting for help

1. Call emergency services immediately and say that you suspect a stroke.
2. Note the time the symptoms began, or the last time the person was known to be normal. This information matters greatly to the treating team.
3. Keep the person sitting or lying with the head slightly raised, unless they are unconscious.
4. If the person is unconscious but breathing, place them in the recovery position.
5. Loosen tight clothing around the neck and chest.
6. Keep the person calm and reassure them while watching for changes.
7. Stay with the person and watch their breathing and level of response.

**Warning:** Do not give food, drink, or any medicine by mouth to a person who may have had a stroke, because swallowing may be impaired and the person may choke.

## What not to do

- Do not drive the person to hospital yourself unless emergency services advise it and no other option exists.
- Do not give aspirin or other blood-thinning medicines unless emergency operators specifically recommend them.
- Do not give the person a drink to test their swallowing.
- Do not wait for the person to decide whether they need help.
- Do not assume that a person who is drowsy is simply tired.

## Recognising a stroke in others

People who have had a stroke may not realise what has happened. They may deny weakness, fail to notice a drooping face, or insist that they are fine. Trust the observations of other people present, and do not let the person's denial delay the call.

## Lowering the risk

Many strokes can be prevented. Reduce the risk by:

- Controlling blood pressure, which is the single largest modifiable risk.
- Stopping smoking, and avoiding exposure to tobacco smoke.
- Keeping blood sugar and cholesterol under review where medical care is available.
- Maintaining regular physical activity and a healthy diet.
- Limiting alcohol, and avoiding binge drinking.
- Seeking prompt advice for irregular heartbeat, since it can cause clots to form in the heart.

## After the emergency

A person who has had a stroke may need long-term support. They may have difficulty with movement, speech, memory, or swallowing. Recovery is often gradual, and rehabilitation makes a real difference. Caregivers should watch for repeat episodes, which often occur within days or weeks of a first stroke, and should treat each new episode as an emergency.

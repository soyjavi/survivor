# Seizures: First Aid

A seizure is a sudden burst of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. It can cause convulsions, staring spells, confusion, or loss of awareness, and it usually ends on its own within a few minutes. Most seizures in people who already have epilepsy are not dangerous in themselves, but the injuries that happen during them are. Your job is not to stop the seizure. It is to protect the person from harm, time the event, and help them recover safely afterward.

## During the seizure: protect, do not restrain

1. Stay calm and stay with the person.
2. Move hard or sharp objects away from them, and clear the area of furniture where possible.
3. Cushion the head with something soft, such as a folded jacket, if it is available.
4. Loosen tight clothing around the neck.
5. Let the convulsion run its course. Do not try to hold the person down.
6. Note the time the seizure started, and watch for the features that describe it.

**Warning:** Never put anything into the person's mouth during a seizure. Objects can break teeth, block the airway, or injure the person's hands and your own.

## Timing the seizure

Timing is the most useful information you can give to emergency staff. Note when the seizure began and when it stopped.

- Most seizures stop within two to three minutes.
- Call emergency services if the seizure lasts longer than five minutes.
- Call emergency services if the person has repeated seizures without regaining consciousness between them.
- Call emergency services if the person is injured, is pregnant, has diabetes, or has never had a seizure before.
- Call emergency services if the person does not begin breathing normally after the seizure ends.

**Warning:** A seizure lasting longer than five minutes, or one after another without recovery in between, is a medical emergency. Call for help immediately.

## Recovery position

Once the convulsions have stopped, the person may be drowsy, confused, or unconscious. Place them in the recovery position if they are breathing but not responding normally, so that fluid or vomit can drain from the mouth and the airway stays open.

1. Kneel beside the person and straighten the legs.
2. Place the arm nearest you out at a right angle to the body, elbow bent, palm up.
3. Bring the far arm across the chest, with the back of the hand resting against the cheek on your side.
4. Bend the far knee and lift it so the foot rests flat on the ground.
5. Gently roll the person toward you by pulling on the raised knee, keeping the head tilted back slightly so the airway stays open.
6. Check breathing frequently, and reposition if needed.

## After the seizure

- Speak calmly and reassure the person. Many people feel embarrassed or disoriented afterward.
- Stay with the person until they are fully awake and able to understand where they are.
- Do not give food or drink until the person is fully alert and able to swallow safely.
- Check for injuries, particularly to the head, mouth, tongue, and limbs.
- Keep the person lying down for a while, even after they seem to recover.
- Do not leave a person alone until they are back to their usual state.

## What not to do

- Do not hold the person down or restrain their movements.
- Do not put anything into the mouth, including fingers, a spoon, or a bottle.
- Do not give water or food during or immediately after a seizure.
- Do not try to pull the tongue out. The tongue cannot be swallowed.
- Do not throw water on the person.
- Do not attempt to revive the person by slapping them.
- Do not leave the person alone in the water, near heights, or near traffic.

## Special circumstances

Some situations require extra care:

- **Seizure in water:** Keep the head above water and get the person out as soon as it is safe, because a seizure in water can cause drowning.
- **Seizure while driving or operating machinery:** Stop the vehicle or machine safely and turn off the engine.
- **Seizure on heights or stairs:** Move the person away from the edge only if it can be done safely.
- **Seizure with breathing difficulty afterward:** Begin the steps for an unresponsive but breathing person, and call for help without delay.

## Recognising a first seizure

A first seizure, or a seizure with unusual features, should always be assessed by a clinician. Look for the following features to report:

- Whether the person fell, injured themselves, or bit their tongue.
- Whether there was loss of bladder or bowel control.
- Whether the person was confused or had trouble speaking afterward.
- Whether there was a warning sign beforehand, such as a strange smell or sensation.
- Whether the person has a known medical condition, or has missed medicine recently.

Record these details and pass them to emergency staff. They help determine the cause and the appropriate follow-up.

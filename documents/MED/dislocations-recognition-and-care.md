# Dislocations: Recognition and Care

A dislocation happens when the ends of the bones that form a joint are forced out of their normal position. It is usually caused by a fall, a sports injury, or a sudden twist. Dislocations are painful and disabling, and they can damage the surrounding nerves, blood vessels, and ligaments. The shoulder, finger, elbow, hip, and knee are among the most commonly dislocated joints. Your role is to recognise the injury, support the joint in the position you find it, protect the circulation, and arrange professional treatment. Putting the joint back into place is a skilled task, and it should not be attempted without training.

## Recognising a dislocation

A dislocated joint usually looks different from its partner on the other side of the body.

- The joint looks misshapen, flattened, or has a visible bump or hollow where the bone should be.
- The limb is held in an unusual position and the person resists movement.
- Pain is severe and worsens with any attempt to move the joint.
- The joint cannot be moved through its normal range.
- Swelling develops quickly around the joint.
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness appears in the limb.

**Warning:** A dislocated joint with a pale, cold, blue, or numb limb is an emergency. Blood supply or nerve function may be compromised, and the person needs immediate professional help.

## Immediate first aid

1. Ask the person to stop moving and to keep the joint still.
2. Support the injured limb in the position in which you found it, using your hands or a pillow.
3. Do not try to move the joint back into place.
4. Remove rings, watches, or tight clothing from the affected limb before swelling develops.
5. Apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth to ease pain and swelling, keeping it away from direct contact with the skin.
6. Check circulation and sensation in the fingers or toes beyond the injury every few minutes, looking for warmth, colour, and movement.

**Warning:** Do not attempt to push the bone back into place, twist the joint, or pull the limb to reposition it. Doing so can damage the nerves, blood vessels, and surrounding tissue.

## Supporting the joint

1. Place padding or a folded cloth under the joint to keep it comfortable.
2. If the arm is affected, support it in a sling or by tucking it against the chest, using a triangular bandage or a piece of cloth.
3. If the leg is affected, keep it still and support it with padding on both sides.
4. Use a splint to immobilise the joint in the position it is held, if you have one and it does not require moving the limb to fit.
5. Keep the person warm and comfortable, and offer reassurance.

## Checking circulation and sensation

Check these at regular intervals, and record the results.

- Colour of the skin beyond the injury, comparing it with the uninjured side.
- Temperature of the fingers or toes, which should feel warm.
- Ability to feel light touch, which indicates nerve function.
- Ability to move the fingers or toes, which indicates nerve and tendon function.
- Capillary refill, by pressing a fingernail or toenail until it blanches and checking how quickly the colour returns.

**Warning:** Numbness, cold skin, pale or blue colour, or inability to move the fingers or toes means circulation or nerve function may be compromised. Get professional help at once.

## What not to do

- Do not attempt to reduce the dislocation unless you have been trained and have the skills to do so safely.
- Do not force the joint into a new position.
- Do not give the person food or drink if they may need surgery or sedation soon.
- Do not let the person continue to use the injured joint.
- Do not apply heat to the joint, because it can increase swelling.
- Do not delay getting help while trying to fix the problem yourself.

## When to seek professional care

Seek professional care as soon as possible for any dislocation. Recovery is best when the joint is reduced promptly, and a clinician should check for associated fractures, which are common with dislocations. The following signs require urgent attention:

- Loss of circulation or sensation in the limb.
- Severe, unrelenting pain that does not ease with rest.
- Open wound near the joint, which may indicate the bone has come through the skin.
- Dislocation of the hip or shoulder after a major impact.
- Dislocation of a joint in a child, where a growth plate may be involved.
- A dislocation that recurs or that the person cannot move back on their own.

## After the injury

- Keep the joint supported and protected until a clinician has examined it.
- Do not allow the person to return to activity until assessed.
- Note how long the joint has been out of position, since this affects treatment.
- Pass on details of the injury, the mechanism, and any previous dislocations to the treating team.

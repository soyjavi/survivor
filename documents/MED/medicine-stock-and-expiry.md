# Medicine Stock and Expiry

Medicines run out fast when pharmacies close. Keep a stock of the ones your household uses, and check it regularly. A written inventory with dates turns a vague supply into one you can actually manage.

## Why it matters

A medicine cabinet that has not been checked for a year can hide several problems at once: expired items, damaged packaging, missing doses and medicines stored in the wrong place. Because stock is rarely checked until someone needs it, these problems usually surface during an emergency. A regular, short review keeps the supply honest and gives you time to order replacements before a shortage forces a difficult choice.

## What to keep

- Any prescription medicine you take daily. Ask your doctor about extra supply.
- Pain relief and fever medicine suitable for adults and children.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Antiseptic, bandages and basic first aid items.
- Any medicine specific to a household member, such as insulin or asthma inhalers.

## Expiry

- Check the date on every item every six months.
- Replace medicines that have expired. Do not use liquid medicines that are cloudy or changed colour.
- Keep a list of stock with dates on the inside of a cupboard door.

![Log: table-header, table-grid rows; labels: medicine, quantity, expiry date, storage location; each item is tracked by where it is kept.](assets/medicine-stock-and-expiry-2.svg)

## Storage

1. Keep medicines dry, in their original packaging, in a cool, dark place.
2. Keep them away from heat, sunlight and humidity. A bathroom is often too humid.
3. Keep them out of reach of children.
4. Keep refrigerated medicines in the fridge, and keep them cold if the power fails. Use an insulated box with ice.

## Step-by-step in detail

Once every six months, take everything out of the cupboard and place it on a table. Read each label and compare the expiry date with your list. Remove anything expired, damaged or unlabelled, and write down what you removed. Put the newest packs at the back and the oldest at the front. Update the inventory with the quantity and date, then return each group to its storage zone.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children's medicines:** keep the child-specific products separate, with the dose chart beside them.
- **Humid or hot climates:** check packaging more often, and consider a sealed box with a desiccant for tablets.
- **Medicines with a short life after opening:** write the open date on the bottle as soon as you start using it.
- **Shared households:** agree on one person to manage the inventory, so that items are not duplicated or forgotten.

## Using medicines in a crisis

- Follow the label and never exceed the dose.
- Do not share prescription medicines.
- Use the oldest stock first.

## Materials

- A notebook or card for the inventory.
- A marker to write the open date on bottles.
- A cupboard with a door that stays closed.
- An insulated box and ice packs for refrigerated items.

## Common mistakes

- Keeping medicines in their original boxes but never checking dates.
- Storing tablets in a damp bathroom cabinet.
- Mixing medicines from different containers into one bag.
- Forgetting that some medicines need to be used within a set time after opening.
- Keeping the inventory in one person's head rather than on paper.

## Troubleshooting

If a tablet has crumbled, stuck together or smells unusual, set it aside and ask a pharmacist before using it. If a liquid has separated, check the label for shaking instructions, and discard it if it does not return to a uniform appearance. If a refrigerated item has been out of the fridge for longer than the label allows, do not use it.

## Checklist

- Inventory written and up to date.
- Expiry dates checked every six months.
- Storage places dry, cool and out of reach of children.
- Refrigerated items cold.
- Oldest stock used first.

## Practice before you need it

Set a calendar reminder for the six-monthly check and make it a family routine. Practise reading an expiry date on a bottle in poor light, because some labels are hard to see in an emergency.

## When it fails

If a medicine has expired, or its colour, smell or texture has changed, do not use it. Take it to a pharmacy or clinic for safe disposal. If the stock is too low to last, contact a prescriber early rather than waiting until it runs out.

**Warning:** do not take medicines that are expired, discoloured or unlabelled.

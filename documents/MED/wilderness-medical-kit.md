# A Wilderness Medical Kit

A wilderness medical kit should fit the length of the trip, the number of people, the distance from help, and the skills of the people carrying it. The best kit is the one you can use under stress, with your hands cold and light failing. Keep it small enough that you actually carry it, and organise it so you can find any item in the dark.

## Core contents and why each one matters

1. **Gloves:** Protect you and the casualty from blood, fluid, and contamination.
2. **Gauze pads and roller gauze:** Dress wounds and apply pressure to bleeding.
3. **Pressure dressing:** A larger pad with a bandage that holds firm pressure on a limb wound.
4. **Adhesive strips and tape:** Close small wounds and secure dressings.
5. **Non-stick dressings:** Protect healing wounds when changed.
6. **Blister dressings:** Cover and cushion friction blisters on the feet and hands.
7. **Irrigation syringe:** Flushes debris from wounds with clean water under pressure.
8. **Tweezers and small scissors:** Remove splinters, ticks, and debris, and cut tape and dressings.
9. **Thermometer:** Tracks fever, which is one of the most useful measures of infection.
10. **Emergency blanket:** Retains body heat for someone who is injured, cold, or in shock.
11. **Splint material:** A padded or rigid splint for suspected fractures or sprains.
12. **Written notes and a pen:** Record times, vital signs, and treatments.

## Personal and group medicines

Carry medicine for the group, and personal prescriptions for each person who needs them, with a written list of names, purposes, and doses.

- Approved pain relief and fever reducer, with the label information kept with it.
- Anti-diarrhoea medicine, used only when the illness is not bloody and not accompanied by high fever.
- Medication for allergies, including an adrenaline auto-injector if a member of the group has been prescribed one.
- Any regular prescription medicine, with a few days of extra supply if the trip is long.

**Warning:** Medicines lose effect or become unsafe over time and with heat. Check expiry dates before departure and keep them in a sealed bag away from direct sun and freezing.

## Tools for specific emergencies

- A small pocket knife or trauma shears for cutting clothing and bandages.
- A whistle, to signal for help if the group becomes separated.
- A headlamp, so both hands remain free while working.
- Water purification method, because dirty water is a common cause of illness.

## What to leave out

Many items seem useful but add risk, weight, or false confidence.

- **Scalpels and surgical instruments:** Only trained users should carry these, and misuse causes harm.
- **Sutures and needles:** Stitching requires training and sterile technique. Without them, the material is more likely to cause infection.
- **Antibiotics without guidance:** Taking them without a diagnosis leads to misuse, resistance, and unnecessary side effects. Carry them only if a clinician has prescribed them for the trip.
- **Injectables without training:** Unlabelled needles and drugs do not belong in a basic kit.
- **Large volumes of ointment and cream:** They take space and rarely change outcomes.
- **Unlabelled or unidentified medicines:** If you cannot name the drug, its dose, and its purpose, do not carry it.
- **Outdated items:** A kit that contains expired material gives a false sense of security.

## Organising and checking the kit

1. Put items in clearly labelled, waterproof bags, grouped by purpose: wounds, medicines, tools, and documentation.
2. Keep the kit in one place in the pack so it can be reached quickly.
3. Inspect it before each trip, replace anything used, and check dates.
4. Make sure at least one member of the group has practised the skills the kit requires.
5. Keep a copy of the emergency plan, including how to call for help where phone coverage exists.

## Using the kit wisely

A kit is a tool, not a replacement for judgement. Use it to stabilise, to make comfortable, and to buy time. Decide early whether the situation needs evacuation, and do not let the kit persuade you that a serious injury is minor. The most important items in any kit are the calm person who knows how to use them, and the decision to turn back or seek help when a problem is larger than the kit can handle.

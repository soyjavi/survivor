# Removing a Tick

A tick attached for many hours can transmit disease. Remove it as soon as you find it. Most ticks do not transmit infection in the first hours, so calm, careful removal is more important than speed alone.

## Why it matters

Ticks attach by cementing their mouthparts into the skin, and the longer they feed, the greater the chance that they transmit an infection. Hasty or rough removal is worse than careful removal, because squeezing or twisting can push infected fluid into the bite and leave mouthparts behind. A tick removed carefully within the first day is a far lower risk than one left in place for days.

## Steps

1. Use fine tweezers or a tick removal tool.
2. Grip the tick as close to the skin as possible.
3. Pull straight upward with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk.
4. Clean the bite and your hands with soap and water, or antiseptic.
5. Put the tick in a sealed container if you want to keep it.

## Step-by-step in detail

Find a good light and a stable position, ideally with a second person to hold the skin taut. Place the tweezers as close to the skin as you can see, without pinching the surrounding skin. Pull at a steady, even speed in a straight line. Once the tick is out, check that the head is intact and that nothing is left in the skin. Wash the tweezers with soap and water or alcohol afterwards, and write down the date.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** check the scalp, behind the ears and the armpits, where ticks often hide.
- **Pets:** ticks can be carried into the house, so check animals after they have been outside.
- **Skin folds:** check the groin, the waistband and under the arms after walking in long grass.
- **Heavy infestations:** if many ticks are found, seek medical advice rather than removing them all at home.

## Do not

- Do not use heat, petrol, nail varnish or other home remedies. They can make the tick release more saliva.
- Do not squeeze the body of the tick.
- Do not leave the mouthparts in the skin. If they break off, try to remove them with tweezers. Otherwise leave them to come out naturally.

## Materials

- Fine-pointed tweezers or a tick removal hook.
- Soap and water, or antiseptic wipes.
- A small sealed container or sticky tape if you want to keep the tick for identification.
- A notebook to record the date of the bite and where it happened.

## Common mistakes

- Squeezing the body, which pushes fluid back into the wound.
- Pulling sideways or twisting, which can snap the mouthparts.
- Forgetting to check the rest of the body for more ticks.
- Ignoring the bite because the tick came off easily.
- Dropping the tick in the bin with no record of when the bite happened.

## Troubleshooting

If the tick breaks and the head stays in the skin, leave it alone if you cannot remove it easily, and keep the area clean. If the bite area becomes very itchy or sore, clean it again and watch closely. If you are unsure whether the tick was fully removed, ask a clinician to check the site.

## Checklist

- Tick removed with fine tweezers.
- Bite and hands cleaned.
- Date and place of the bite noted.
- Body checked for other ticks.
- Symptoms watched for the next few weeks.

## Practice before you need it

Keep fine tweezers with the first aid kit and check that they are clean and sharp. Practise on a pet or a cushion first, so that the grip and pull are steady when you need them.

## Watch for

Look out for the following over the next few weeks:

- A spreading red rash, especially a bull's-eye pattern.
- Fever, headache, joint pain or muscle aches.

Seek medical care if these appear.

## When it fails

If the mouthparts remain in the skin and you cannot remove them, leave them alone so that the skin can push them out. Keep the area clean. If the bite becomes infected, seek care.

**Warning:** wash the bite area and check the body for further ticks after walking in long grass or woods.

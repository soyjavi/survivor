# Allergies and Anaphylaxis

Most allergies are mild. Anaphylaxis is a severe reaction that can close the airway and cause shock within minutes. It is a true emergency, and waiting to see whether it passes is the most common mistake people make.

## Why it matters

Anaphylaxis can start within minutes of contact with a trigger, but it can also begin a long time after exposure, so a person may not connect the reaction to the cause. The speed is what makes it dangerous. Shock can develop before the person has time to describe what happened, and the airway can narrow before anyone has finished reading the situation. Acting on the first clear signs, rather than on a confirmed diagnosis, is the safe default.

Anaphylaxis happens when the immune system reacts strongly to a substance such as a food, an insect sting, a medicine or a plant. The body releases chemicals that widen the blood vessels, leak fluid from them and tighten the muscles of the airways. These changes can happen in a few minutes.

## Signs of anaphylaxis

- Swelling of the lips, tongue, throat or face.
- Difficulty breathing, wheezing or a hoarse voice.
- Hives, flushing or itching all over the body.
- Vomiting, belly pain or diarrhoea.
- Dizziness, collapse or a weak, fast pulse.

Two or more of these signs together, especially after a known trigger, should be treated as anaphylaxis.

## Act at once

1. If the person has an adrenaline auto-injector, use it on the outer thigh straight away, through clothing if needed.
2. Call for emergency help and say that the person is having a severe allergic reaction.
3. Lay the person flat with the legs raised if they are dizzy or faint. If they are struggling to breathe, let them sit up.
4. Give a second dose after five to fifteen minutes if there is no improvement and a second injector is available.
5. Keep watching. Symptoms can return after the first dose wears off.

## Using an auto-injector

- Remove the safety cap, press the tip firmly against the outer thigh and hold it in place for the time printed on the device, usually a few seconds.
- Inject into the outer thigh only, even if the person is moving. Never inject into the buttock or a vein.
- Note the time of each dose on the packaging or a card, so the next helper knows what was given.

## Step-by-step in detail

Before the reaction, make sure the device is in date, the cap is intact and the person knows which trigger to avoid. During the reaction, one helper should hold the device and read the steps aloud while another calls for help. After the injection, place the device and its packaging beside the person so paramedics can see the dose and time. Do not leave the person alone, and do not let them drive themselves to care.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** their weight is lower, so the same device still applies, but they tire quickly and may need holding still.
- **Heart conditions:** a person with heart disease may collapse faster, so keep them lying down unless breathing is hard.
- **Beta blockers:** some medicines can reduce the response to adrenaline. Tell responders about any regular medicines.

## Mild reactions

A mild rash or itch can be treated with an antihistamine if the person has one and is not having breathing trouble. Watch closely, because a mild reaction can turn severe within minutes.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for the person to get worse before using the auto-injector.
- Giving an antihistamine instead of adrenaline in a severe reaction.
- Letting the person walk around, which can strain the heart and worsen collapse.
- Forgetting that a second dose may be needed.
- Storing the device in a bag or drawer where it cannot be reached quickly.

## Troubleshooting

If the device does not seem to work, check the window or indicator, then follow the instructions printed on it. Do not delay calling for help while you investigate. If the device has been dropped or left in heat, the medicine may be less reliable, so carry a spare if one is prescribed.

## Checklist

- Know the known allergens of each household member.
- Keep the auto-injector with the person, not in a drawer.
- Check expiry dates every six months.
- Practise the steps with a trainer device if one is available.
- Keep a written allergy card in the wallet and the first aid kit.

## Practice before you need it

Use a trainer device to rehearse the hold-and-count motion, and say the steps out loud until they are automatic. Set a reminder for the expiry check. Ask one other adult to practise calling for help so that the roles are clear during a real event.

## When it fails

If the person stops breathing, begin CPR as described in Basic CPR. If the injector does not work, is missing or has expired, act on everything else at once. Do not wait for a second dose to call for help.

## Prevent

- Know each person's allergies and keep a note of them in the first aid kit.
- Keep any prescribed auto-injector in date and with the person.
**Warning:** always treat anaphylaxis as an emergency, even if the person seems better after the first dose.

# Spinal Injury: Keeping the Neck Still

The spinal cord runs through the bones of the back and neck, and it carries the signals that control movement and feeling. A spinal injury can cause permanent paralysis, and the greatest risk is that a careless movement turns a stable fracture into one that damages the cord. For that reason, anyone who has had a significant fall, crash, or blow to the head or body should be treated as if the spine is injured until a clinician has ruled it out. The rule is simple: keep the neck and back still, and move the person only when it is essential to protect them from a greater danger.

## When to suspect a spinal injury

Suspect a spinal injury after any of the following:

- A fall from a height, especially onto the head, neck, or buttocks.
- A road traffic collision, particularly if the person was not wearing a seatbelt or was thrown from a vehicle.
- A diving or swimming accident in shallow water.
- A blow to the head with signs of a head injury.
- Pain, tenderness, or deformity of the neck or back.
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the arms or legs.
- A person who is unconscious, drowsy, or confused after a serious injury.

## Signs to look for

- Pain or tenderness along the spine, particularly in the neck.
- Visible deformity, such as a step in the line of the spine.
- Numbness or tingling in the hands, feet, arms, or legs.
- Weakness or inability to move a limb.
- Loss of bladder or bowel control.
- Difficulty breathing, which can occur with high neck injuries.
- Priapism, a persistent erection, which can be a sign of spinal cord injury in males.

**Warning:** Any weakness, numbness, or loss of control of the bladder or bowel after a spinal injury is an emergency. Keep the spine still and call for help without delay.

## Keeping the neck still

1. Do not move the person unless they are in immediate danger, such as fire, drowning, or traffic.
2. Ask the person not to move their head, and tell them to stay still.
3. Kneel at the head of the person and place your hands on either side of the head, supporting it firmly.
4. Hold the head and neck in the position in which you found them, unless there is a breathing or airway problem.
5. If the person is lying face down, keep the head in line with the body and support it with both hands.
6. Hold your position until emergency help arrives, or until a suitable collar and support is available.

**Warning:** Do not twist, bend, or tilt the neck to make breathing easier, unless the person has stopped breathing and you have been trained to open the airway safely.

## Maintaining the airway without moving the spine

Keeping the airway open is the first priority, but it must be done carefully.

1. Use the jaw thrust technique, moving the lower jaw forward without tilting the head.
2. Keep the head still and maintain an open mouth by moving the jaw only.
3. Clear any vomit or blood from the mouth gently, rolling the person as a single unit if you must turn them.
4. If the person is unconscious and breathing, keep them still, and watch the breathing constantly.

## Moving the person: only when essential

Moving a person with a suspected spinal injury can cause permanent damage. Move them only if they are in danger that cannot be removed in place, or if you need to perform life-saving treatment.

1. Assess whether the danger is immediate and whether the person can be protected where they are.
2. If movement is essential, use a log-roll technique, where the whole body rolls as one unit with the head and neck held in line.
3. Use several rescuers, one for the head and neck, and others for the shoulders, hips, and legs.
4. Move slowly and smoothly, without jerking or lifting the body by the limbs.
5. Support the person on a firm surface such as a backboard or stretcher, once one is available.
6. Keep the spine straight and still during the move, and talk to the person throughout.

**Warning:** Do not move a person with a suspected spinal injury by lifting them up or dragging them along the ground. These methods can cause permanent paralysis.

## Monitoring the injured person

- Check breathing regularly, watching the chest rise and fall.
- Check responsiveness and any change in consciousness.
- Ask about sensation in the hands and feet, and whether the person can move their toes and fingers.
- Watch for changes in colour, temperature, or swelling of the limbs.
- Keep the person warm, since injured people lose heat rapidly, and cover them with a blanket.
- Record the time of injury, the findings, and any changes, to share with emergency staff.

# Basic CPR

CPR keeps blood moving to the brain and heart when the heart has stopped. Start as soon as you find a person who is not responding and not breathing normally. Every minute without circulation lowers the chance of survival, so action matters more than perfection.

## Why it matters

When the heart stops, the brain loses oxygen within minutes. Chest compressions push blood through the body by hand, buying time until the heart can be restarted with a defibrillator or until advanced care arrives. Bystander CPR started at once is one of the strongest factors in survival, so a rough but continuous effort is far better than waiting for the perfect technique.

## Check

1. Make sure the scene is safe for you and the person.
2. Tap the person's shoulders and shout.
3. Check for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Gasping is not normal breathing.

If there is no normal breathing, start compressions immediately.

## Compressions

1. Call for help, or send someone to get it.
2. Place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest, between the nipples.
3. Place the other hand on top, interlace the fingers if you can, and lock your elbows.
4. Push down about 5 cm, then let the chest rise fully.
5. Compress at about 100 to 120 times per minute, which is close to the rhythm of a well-known pop song.

![Cycle: step-box thirty compressions, arrow-straight, step-box two breaths, arrow-loop back to start; labels: 30 compressions, 2 breaths; the ratio repeats until help takes over. NEEDS: cpr counter](assets/basic-cpr-2.svg)

## Step-by-step in detail

Kneel beside the chest with your shoulders directly above your hands, so your body weight drives the push. Keep your arms straight. Compress hard and fast, counting out loud if it helps you keep rhythm. Let the chest return fully after each compression, but keep your hands on the chest so you do not lose position. Pauses should be as short as you can manage, especially between cycles.

## Breaths

- After 30 compressions, give 2 breaths if you are trained and willing to do so.
- Tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose and blow until the chest rises.
- If you are not trained, continue compressions only. Compression-only CPR is far better than no CPR.

## Using an AED

An automated external defibrillator (AED) analyses the heart rhythm and advises whether to deliver a shock. Turn it on and follow the voice prompts. Attach the pads to bare skin as the diagrams on the device show, keep everyone clear while it analyses, and press the shock button only when instructed. Resume compressions immediately after a shock, and do not wait to check the person again.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children and infants:** the technique differs, with a lighter touch and different hand placement. If you are untrained, begin compressions as described and send for help.
- **Late pregnancy:** the same steps apply, but the technique may need adjusting, so get trained help as quickly as possible.
- **Wet or cold settings:** dry the chest where possible, and keep the person away from standing water.

## Materials

- A firm, flat surface such as the floor.
- A phone or a person to call for help and bring an automated external defibrillator (AED) if one is nearby.
- Gloves or a cloth barrier if you have them, for breaths.

## Common mistakes

- Pushing too shallowly or too slowly.
- Leaning on the chest between compressions, which stops the heart refilling.
- Stopping to check too often.
- Placing the hands on the stomach or the lower part of the breastbone.
- Forgetting to swap rescuers, so the first helper tires and the compressions weaken.

## Troubleshooting

If the chest does not rise with breaths, check the head position and the seal over the nose and mouth, then try again. If you feel the ribs move under your hands, reposition slightly but keep going. If you are tiring, call out for a relief rescuer rather than slowing down.

## Checklist

- Scene safe, person unresponsive, breathing not normal.
- Help called, AED requested if available.
- Hands centred, elbows locked, depth about 5 cm.
- Rate 100 to 120 per minute.
- Rescuers switched about every two minutes if possible.

## Practice before you need it

Practise on a manikin or a firm cushion with a timer or a song at the right tempo. Rehearse the call for help and the order of roles, so that the first minute is spent compressing rather than deciding who does what.

## When it fails

If the person does not respond after many minutes, keep going until help takes over or you are exhausted. If the person begins to breathe normally, roll them onto their side in the recovery position and keep watching their breathing. If a rib cracks, continue; the injury is far less dangerous than an untreated arrest.

## Continue

Keep going until the person breathes normally, help arrives, or you are too exhausted to continue. Change with another rescuer every two minutes if possible.

**Warning:** do not stop to check repeatedly. Only check for normal breathing when the person shows signs of life.

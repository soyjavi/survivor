# Wound Closure Without a Surgeon

Closing a wound is not the same as covering it. A closed wound heals faster and with less scarring, but closing a dirty or deep wound too early traps bacteria inside and can turn a manageable injury into a serious infection. Your job in the field is to decide whether closure is appropriate, make the wound as clean as you can, and then watch it closely.

## Decide whether closure is appropriate

1. Look at the wound edges. If they come together easily with light pressure, closure is a candidate.
2. Check how old the wound is. Wounds closed within a few hours of injury carry the lowest infection risk. Older wounds are usually better left open and dressed.
3. Check where the wound is. Wounds on the face and scalp heal well and are often closed when they are clean and fresh.
4. Check the mechanism. Clean cuts from a sharp edge are good candidates. Crush injuries, bites, and wounds from animals or dirty objects are not.

**Do not close** a wound that is heavily contaminated, has dirt or gravel you cannot remove, is a puncture, is a bite, shows tissue that looks dead or grey, or sits over a joint where movement will pull it apart.

## Clean before anything else

1. Put on gloves if you have them.
2. Stop the bleeding first with direct pressure, then turn to cleaning.
3. Rinse the wound with clean water, ideally under steady flow from a bottle or a syringe, for several minutes.
4. Remove visible debris with clean tweezers that you have wiped or boiled. Stop if the process causes fresh bleeding that you cannot control.
5. Avoid strong antiseptics poured deep into the wound. They damage healing tissue. Use them only on the skin around the edges if you have nothing better.
6. Pat the skin dry and cover the wound with a clean dressing until you are ready to close it.

**Warning:** Never bury debris in the wound to make it look neat. Foreign material left inside is the most common reason a field closure fails.

## Closing with adhesive strips

Adhesive skin strips, sometimes called butterfly closures, are the safest closure method for most people without training.

1. Dry the skin on both sides of the wound and shave any hair that will stick under the strips.
2. Bring the edges together gently with your fingers. Do not force them.
3. Apply one strip across the wound near one end, pressing the skin on the far side toward it.
4. Place the next strip across the wound about a few millimetres from the first, leaving a small gap so drainage can escape.
5. Use strips along the length of the wound, but do not close the whole wound if you suspect drainage will need to escape.
6. Cover the strips with a non-stick dressing. Change the dressing every day or when it becomes wet or dirty.

Stitches require training and proper material. If you do not have both, do not attempt them.

## Watching for infection

- Redness that spreads beyond the first day, especially in a widening ring.
- Swelling that increases after the first two days rather than settling.
- Warmth around the wound that is clearly greater than the skin elsewhere.
- Pus, cloudy discharge, or a bad smell.
- Pain that gets worse instead of better after the first day or two.
- Red streaks running away from the wound.
- Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell.

**Warning:** Red streaks, fever, or a wound that opens again after being closed mean the infection may be spreading into the body. Seek professional care as soon as you can reach it, and do not wait for the wound to look worse.

## Removing and replacing closures

- Leave strips in place until they fall off on their own, usually within a week or two.
- If a strip loosens, replace it rather than leaving a gap.
- If the edges separate and the wound fills with fluid, stop closing it and treat it as an open wound.
- Keep the area clean and dry, and avoid heavy stretching of the skin over the wound.

# Frostbite: Recognition and Care

Frostbite is the freezing of skin and the tissue beneath it. It most often affects fingers, toes, ears, the nose, and the cheeks. It develops when the body diverts blood away from the extremities to protect the core, and the exposed parts cool until ice crystals form in the tissue. Frostbite can cause lasting damage, and the way you rewarm the area affects how much tissue is saved. Recognise it early, protect it from further cold, and rewarm it only when you can keep it warm.

## Stages and signs

Frostbite is often described in stages, and the early stages are the easiest to treat.

1. **Frostnip:** The skin is cold, red, or pale, and it stings or aches. It is reversible and does not involve deep tissue.
2. **Superficial frostbite:** The skin feels hard or waxy, is pale or grey-white, and may be numb. The tissue beneath still feels soft when pressed.
3. **Deep frostbite:** The affected part feels solid and wooden. It is numb, pale, grey, or blue. Blisters may appear after rewarming, and the tissue may be permanently damaged.

**Warning:** A limb that is hard and frozen all the way through, or that does not change colour after rewarming, has deep tissue injury. Seek professional care as soon as possible.

## Protecting the area first

1. Move the person out of the cold and wind as soon as possible.
2. Remove wet gloves, socks, and boots, and replace them with dry, insulating materials.
3. Remove rings and tight items from the affected area before swelling develops.
4. Cover the frostbitten part with dry, loose layers rather than wrapping it tightly.
5. Keep the person warm overall, since a cold body reduces blood flow to the extremities.

## Rewarming safely

Rewarming should happen only when the area can be kept warm until it is fully thawed. Refreezing causes far more damage than the initial cold.

1. Use water that is comfortably warm, close to body temperature, testing it with an unaffected part of the body such as the inside of the elbow.
2. Immerse the affected part gently and keep it there until the skin is soft and the colour returns.
3. Support the limb in the water, and move the frostbitten fingers or toes gently if you can.
4. Give warm drinks if the person is conscious and able to swallow, avoiding alcohol and caffeine.
5. Keep the area dry and warm after rewarming, wrapping it in dry, soft material.
6. Do not allow the person to walk on frostbitten feet once they have been rewarmed, because the tissue is fragile.

**Warning:** Once the area has thawed, do not allow it to refreeze. Refreezing causes severe and permanent tissue damage, so if there is any chance of re-exposure to cold, leave the area thawed and protected instead.

## What not to do

- Do not rub or massage frostbitten skin, because this damages the fragile tissue.
- Do not use dry heat such as a fire, heater, hot-water bottle, or direct sun, which can burn numb skin.
- Do not break blisters that form after rewarming.
- Do not walk on frostbitten feet unless there is no alternative for survival.
- Do not apply snow or ice to frostbitten skin.
- Do not assume that numbness means the injury is minor.

## When to seek professional care

Seek assessment promptly if any of these occur:

- The affected area is hard, white, grey, or blue after rewarming.
- Blisters are large, blood-filled, or numerous.
- The skin turns black or stays discoloured for many days.
- Swelling or pain increases over a day or two.
- The person has signs of hypothermia, such as confusion, shivering that stops, or drowsiness.
- Any sign of infection develops, including redness, pus, or fever.
- The person has diabetes, poor circulation, or any other condition that affects healing.

**Warning:** Shivering that stops, confusion, or drowsiness together with cold skin means hypothermia, which is an emergency. Keep the person warm and get help at once.

## Caring for the injury afterwards

1. Keep the area clean and dry, with loose, soft dressings.
2. Elevate the affected part gently when resting to reduce swelling.
3. Avoid pressure on the area from tight shoes or gloves.
4. Protect the skin from sun and cold while it heals.
5. Record the appearance and sensation of the area each day to track progress.

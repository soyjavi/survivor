# Hypoglycaemia

Low blood sugar can develop quickly, especially when food runs short or a person with diabetes is unwell. It can cause confusion, collapse and seizures. The brain depends on glucose, so treatment must be fast, and it must be followed by food.

## Why it matters

Glucose is the brain's main fuel, and when it runs low the brain changes behaviour before it stops working. A person who is confused, sweaty or unusually angry may be experiencing low blood sugar, not intoxication or stress. Recognising the pattern early allows a simple treatment to work. Waiting for the person to recognise it themselves is unreliable, because judgement is often impaired first.

## Signs

- Shaking, sweating, pale skin.
- Hunger, headache, irritability.
- Confusion, slurred speech, unusual behaviour.
- Drowsiness or loss of consciousness.

The person may not realise their blood sugar is low, so others should look for changes in behaviour, especially in a person who has skipped a meal or taken too much insulin.

## Treatment

1. If the person is awake and can swallow, give a fast-acting sugar: 15 to 20 grams, such as sugar in water, juice or glucose tablets.
2. Wait 10 to 15 minutes, then recheck the symptoms.
3. Repeat if the symptoms continue.
4. Once better, give a longer-acting food, such as bread or biscuits, if available.

## Step-by-step in detail

Sit the person down before giving anything, and make sure they can swallow without choking. Give the fast sugar slowly, with the person's own cup if possible. Stay with them and watch for improvement while you prepare the next dose if one is needed. Once the person is alert and able to talk normally, offer the longer-acting food. If the person has a glucose meter, check the reading before and after the treatment and write both numbers down.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** they may not explain how they feel, so watch for pale skin, unusual crying or sudden tiredness.
- **Older adults:** symptoms can be mild or mistaken for other problems, so check blood sugar when in doubt.
- **People who take insulin or certain tablets:** the risk rises with missed meals, unusual exercise or illness.
- **Pregnancy:** low blood sugar should be treated promptly and reported to the clinician who manages the pregnancy.

## Unconscious person

- Do not give food or drink.
- Place the person in the recovery position.
- Seek urgent help. If a glucagon injection is available, it can be given by a trained person.

![Recovery: recovery-position figure; labels: on side, top leg bent, head tilted back; keeps the airway open for an unconscious person.](assets/hypoglycaemia-5.svg)

## Materials

- Glucose tablets or gel, if prescribed.
- Sugar, honey or juice for quick treatment.
- A longer-acting snack such as bread, biscuits or a banana.
- A written note of the person's usual doses and the insulin or tablets they take.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for the symptoms to pass without treating them.
- Giving a large meal instead of a small, fast sugar.
- Assuming a person with diabetes who is confused is simply tired or drunk.
- Giving food or drink to someone who cannot swallow safely.
- Stopping the treatment as soon as the person feels a little better, before the food has been given.

## Troubleshooting

If the person refuses to take the sugar, offer it again slowly and calmly rather than forcing it, and seek help if they cannot take it. If the person improves briefly then becomes drowsy again, recheck quickly and seek care, because the sugar may have worn off. If you have no meter and the person is not improving, treat it as an emergency.

## Checklist

- Symptoms recognised early.
- Fast sugar given if the person can swallow.
- Recheck after 10 to 15 minutes.
- Longer-acting food given once better.
- Medical help arranged if the person does not recover.

## Practice before you need it

Keep sugar and glucose tablets together in a labelled place and check their expiry each year. Practise asking a family member to show you the warning signs, so that the pattern is familiar when it matters.

## When it fails

If the person does not improve after two rounds of sugar, or becomes drowsy and cannot swallow, call for emergency help. Keep the person in the recovery position and do not force anything into the mouth.

## Prevent

- Keep sugar and glucose tablets with any person who has diabetes.
- Eat regularly, and do not skip meals.
- Keep the person's medicine and a note of their usual doses.

**Warning:** do not give sugar to an unconscious person, because they can choke.

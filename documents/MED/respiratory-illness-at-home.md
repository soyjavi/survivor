# Respiratory Illness at Home: Isolation and Care

Respiratory illnesses such as colds, influenza, and many chest infections spread through droplets from coughs and sneezes and through contact with contaminated surfaces. Most people recover at home with rest and fluids. The home carer has two jobs: reduce the chance that the illness spreads to others, and watch the person's breathing closely, because breathing is where serious problems first appear.

## Separating the sick person

1. Place the sick person in a room with a door that closes, and if possible, a separate bathroom.
2. Assign a single carer who will do most of the contact.
3. Keep food and drink on a tray at the door, and collect used dishes without lingering.
4. Give the sick person their own towels, cups, and utensils, washed separately in hot water.
5. Ask visitors to stay away, and keep children and anyone with a weakened immune system well away from the room.

**Warning:** The carer must not enter other rooms while carrying used tissues or dishes, and must wash hands after each contact with the sick room.

## Ventilation

Fresh air dilutes the virus particles that accumulate in closed rooms. Good ventilation does not require exposure to cold for the sick person.

1. Open a window in the sick room slightly, or open a window in an adjoining space, to bring in fresh air.
2. Create a cross-flow of air by opening windows on opposite sides when weather allows.
3. Use a fan to move air away from the carer and toward an open window, if possible.
4. Keep the sick person warm with extra blankets rather than closing the room fully.
5. Ventilate the room for several minutes after leaving it, with the door closed.

## Hygiene in the sick room

- Wash hands with soap and water before and after entering the room.
- Dispose of used tissues in a lined bin and tie the bag before removing it.
- Wipe surfaces the sick person touches, including bedside tables, door handles, and light switches, with a suitable cleaner.
- Wash used linen and clothing separately, using hot water when practicable.
- Clean the carer's hands after handling any used items, even when wearing gloves.

## Hydration and rest

Fever, sweating, and reduced appetite all increase fluid loss. Keeping the person well hydrated supports the body's immune response and keeps the mucus thin enough to clear.

1. Offer water, weak tea, broth, or oral rehydration solution in small, frequent sips.
2. Keep a simple count of how often the person passes urine. Pale urine suggests adequate fluid intake.
3. Offer soft food in small portions if the person can eat.
4. Encourage rest, with the head raised on pillows if breathing feels easier that way.
5. Use approved pain and fever medicine as directed on the label, and note the times given.

## Breathing warning signs

Watch the breathing constantly, not only at scheduled checks. The following signs mean the person needs urgent medical help.

- Breathing that is fast, or that seems hard, even when resting.
- Pulling in of the skin between the ribs or at the base of the neck with each breath.
- Inability to speak in full sentences without stopping for breath.
- Lips, face, or fingertips that look blue or grey.
- Chest pain, or a feeling of tightness in the chest that does not ease.
- Confusion, unusual drowsiness, or difficulty staying awake.
- Coughing up blood, or breathing that makes a high-pitched whistling sound.

**Warning:** Breathing that is struggling or becoming more laboured is an emergency. Call emergency services immediately and keep the person sitting up while you wait.

## Recognising when a chest infection is developing

A cold that stays mild usually improves within a week or two. Seek professional assessment if:

- The person's fever returns after it has settled, or stays high beyond a few days.
- The cough becomes productive with coloured or heavy sputum that continues to worsen.
- The person feels worse on the fifth or sixth day than they did earlier.
- An older person, a pregnant person, or someone with a long-term condition becomes more unwell.

## Ending the period of isolation

Keep the sick person separated until they are clearly recovering and until advice from a clinician or public health authority says it is safe to move about the house more freely. After that, clean the sick room thoroughly, ventilate it fully, and launder all linen before the room is used again.

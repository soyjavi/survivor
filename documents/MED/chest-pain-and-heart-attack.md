# Chest Pain and Heart Attack

A heart attack happens when the blood supply to part of the heart muscle is blocked, usually by a clot forming on a narrowed artery. Every minute without restored blood flow means more heart muscle is lost. Chest pain is the most common warning, but it does not always look dramatic, and it is not always in the chest. The right response is to recognise the possibility early, call for emergency help, and keep the person as still and calm as possible while help is on its way.

## Recognising the warning signs

A heart attack often produces pain or pressure that does not resemble a simple muscle ache.

- A feeling of heaviness, squeezing, tightness, or crushing pressure in the centre of the chest.
- Pain that spreads to the arms, particularly the left arm, the neck, jaw, back, or stomach.
- Breathlessness, with or without chest discomfort.
- Sweating, often cold and clammy, that appears without exertion.
- Nausea, vomiting, or a sense of impending doom.
- Light-headedness, dizziness, or fainting.
- In some people, especially women and older adults, the main symptoms are breathlessness, unusual fatigue, or pain in the back or jaw without much chest pain.

**Warning:** Chest pain that lasts more than a few minutes, or that comes back or changes, needs emergency assessment even if it eases. Do not wait to see whether it passes.

## Immediate actions

1. Call emergency services without delay, and give the location and the symptoms clearly.
2. Keep the person sitting or lying in a position that makes breathing easiest, usually half-sitting with the knees bent.
3. Loosen tight clothing around the neck and chest.
4. Stay with the person and keep them calm. Anxiety makes the heart work harder.
5. Do not allow the person to walk, climb stairs, or drive themselves anywhere.
6. If the person becomes unresponsive and stops breathing normally, begin CPR and use an automated external defibrillator if one is available, following the instructions given by the emergency operator.

## Aspirin: when and when not

Aspirin is sometimes recommended in suspected heart attack because it helps prevent clots from growing. It is not appropriate for everyone, and it must be used only on the advice of an emergency operator or clinician.

1. If the emergency operator advises aspirin, follow their instructions for the dose and method.
2. Confirm that the person is not allergic to aspirin or to similar medicines.
3. Confirm that the person is an adult who has no known bleeding disorder.
4. Confirm that the person has not recently had a stomach ulcer, bleeding in the stomach or brain, or a serious head injury.
5. Confirm that the person is not already taking an anticoagulant medicine, unless a clinician has said it is appropriate.

**Warning:** Do not give aspirin to a child or teenager, because it can trigger a serious illness affecting the liver and brain. Do not give aspirin to anyone with a known allergy, a bleeding condition, or a history of stomach bleeding. Do not give aspirin on your own initiative without advice from emergency services.

## While waiting for help

- Keep the person calm, warm, and as still as possible.
- Monitor breathing and level of response. Record the time of onset.
- If the person has prescribed medicines for the heart, such as glyceryl trinitrate, follow the instructions on their prescription only if they have been trained to use it and the person has used it before.
- Do not give food or drink, in case emergency treatment requires a procedure.
- Be ready to tell emergency staff the person's name, age, medical history, current medicines, and any allergies.

## Chest pain that may not be a heart attack

Chest pain has many causes, and not all are life-threatening. However, you cannot tell reliably at home which cause is present. Treat any chest pain that is severe, sudden, or accompanied by the warning signs above as an emergency. Pain after injury, pain that changes with breathing or movement, or pain that matches a known pattern in the person is still worth professional assessment.

**Warning:** Chest pain with breathlessness, fainting, sweating, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw should never be treated as a simple muscle strain. Assume the worst until a professional has examined the person.

## Reducing risk

- Stop smoking, and avoid exposure to smoke.
- Keep blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar under regular review where medical care is available.
- Maintain regular physical activity appropriate to age and condition.
- Eat a diet with plenty of vegetables, fruit, and whole grains, and limit salt and saturated fat.
- Know your family history, and tell clinicians about any early heart disease in relatives.

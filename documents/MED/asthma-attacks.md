# Asthma Attacks

An asthma attack narrows the airways and makes breathing hard. The airway muscles tighten, the lining swells and extra mucus blocks the passage. Most attacks respond to the person's own reliever inhaler.

## Why it matters

Asthma is a long-term condition, so the person often knows their triggers and their routine better than anyone else. Your job is to support their plan, keep them calm and recognise when the usual plan is no longer working. An attack that is caught early usually settles quickly, while one that is left alone can progress over an hour or more.

## Signs

- Wheezing, coughing, chest tightness.
- Short, fast breaths and difficulty speaking in full sentences.
- Blue lips or drowsiness in a severe attack.
- Use of the neck and chest muscles to breathe, with the ribs pulling in.

A person who can speak only one or two words at a time is having a severe attack and needs help at once.

## Steps

1. Help the person sit upright and stay calm with them. Panic makes breathing harder.
2. Help them take the reliever inhaler, usually one puff at a time, with a spacer if they have one.
3. Ask them to breathe out fully before each puff, then take a slow breath in as the puff is released.
4. Wait about a minute between puffs. Repeat up to ten puffs if needed, as the label or doctor advised.
5. Keep watching. Repeat the dose if the symptoms do not improve in about five minutes.

## Step-by-step in detail

Shake the inhaler before each puff if the label says so. Fit the mouthpiece into the spacer, press once, and ask the person to take five to six slow breaths through the spacer. Without a spacer, have them breathe in steadily and hold their breath for a few seconds after each puff. Write down the time of each dose on the card that travels with the person, so responders know how much was given and when.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** a spacer with a mask is often easier than a mouthpiece. Stay close and keep them sitting upright on your lap.
- **Older adults:** breathing is often weaker to begin with, so lower the threshold for calling help.
- **Pregnancy:** asthma should still be treated promptly, and the same warning signs apply.
- **Hot or cold weather:** cold air and smoke can trigger attacks, so move the person indoors to a calm, clean room.

## Materials

- The person's reliever inhaler and any preventer inhaler.
- A spacer, if one was prescribed.
- A written plan from the doctor, if the person has one.
- A watch or phone to time the doses.

## Call for help if

- The person cannot speak in full sentences.
- There is no improvement after the inhaler.
- The lips or face turn blue or grey.
- The person becomes drowsy or confused.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the inhaler without a spacer, so much of the dose stays in the mouth.
- Leaving the person lying down, which makes breathing harder.
- Waiting too long before calling for help because the person seems to be coping.
- Running out of reliever medicine during a long crisis.
- Letting the person exercise through the attack, which can narrow the airways further.

## Troubleshooting

If the inhaler makes no sound or does not release a puff, it may be empty or blocked. Check the counter if it has one, and try a different inhaler if the person has a spare. If the spacer is cracked or static-charged, replace it, because it will hold less of the dose.

## Checklist

- Keep the reliever and any preventer inhaler with the person at all times.
- Keep a spacer in the kit.
- Check the expiry date of each inhaler.
- Note the number of doses used and the time.
- Keep a copy of the person's written plan in the kit.

## Practice before you need it

Practise the spacer technique with a family member on a calm day, not during an attack. Know where the reliever is kept in your home and check that it has enough doses left to get through a bad night.

## When it fails

If the inhaler is empty, not working or the person does not improve, treat the attack as an emergency. Keep the person upright, keep them calm and get help. Do not wait for it to get worse before acting.

## Prevent attacks

- Avoid smoke, dust, strong fumes and cold air where possible.
- Keep a clean environment and remove known triggers, such as pets or mould.
- Keep the preventer inhaler taken as prescribed, even on good days.

**Warning:** a severe attack is an emergency. Do not wait for it to get worse.

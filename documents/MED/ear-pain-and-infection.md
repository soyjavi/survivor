# Ear Pain and Ear Infections

Ear pain is one of the most common complaints in people of every age, and in children it is often the first sign of an infection that started in the throat or nose. Many cases settle on their own with simple comfort measures. A smaller number develop into infections that need assessment, and a very small number lead to serious complications. The task is to ease the pain, avoid causing damage, and recognise the signs that change the picture.

## Common causes

- Infection of the middle ear, often after a cold, which causes pressure and pain behind the eardrum.
- Infection of the outer ear canal, sometimes called swimmer's ear, caused by water that stays in the canal.
- Pressure changes during flight, diving, or fast descent from altitude.
- Wax build-up, which can block the canal and cause pain and muffled hearing.
- Pain that is referred from the jaw, teeth, or throat, so the ear itself is not the source.

## Easing the pain

1. Use an approved pain reliever following the label, adjusted for the person's age and weight.
2. Place a warm, damp cloth or a warm, not hot, pack against the outside of the ear for short periods.
3. Keep the head raised when lying down, which can reduce pressure.
4. Encourage swallowing, chewing, or sipping fluids, which helps open the tubes that drain the middle ear.
5. Check for a foreign object in a child's ear, and seek assessment if one may be present.

**Warning:** Do not put oils, alcohol, or home remedies into the ear canal if the eardrum may be perforated or if there is discharge. These can cause serious harm and worsen infection.

## What drainage means

A discharge from the ear is one of the most important signs to notice.

- Clear or pale fluid that drains after a cold may indicate a perforated eardrum that is healing or an infection that has ruptured the drum.
- Pus that is yellow, green, or thick is a sign of infection.
- Blood-stained discharge after an injury needs urgent assessment.
- Discharge with a strong smell suggests infection.

Do not try to clean out the ear with cotton buds or any sharp object. Wipe the outside of the ear gently with a clean cloth. Keep the canal dry, and if the person has been swimming, tilt the head to allow water to drain.

**Warning:** Any drainage from the ear after a blow to the head, a fall, or a severe headache is an emergency. Seek professional care at once.

## Recognising when help is needed

Seek professional assessment if any of the following occur:

- Pain that does not ease after a day or two of comfort measures.
- Fever above a level that concerns you, especially in a young child.
- Swelling or redness behind the ear, or the ear sticking out.
- Discharge of pus, or any blood or clear fluid from the ear.
- Hearing loss that persists or worsens.
- Dizziness, vomiting, or loss of balance.
- Stiffness of the neck, or facial weakness or drooping.
- A child who is unusually irritable, not feeding well, or pulling at the ear persistently.

**Warning:** Facial drooping, a stiff neck with fever, or severe dizziness with vomiting may signal a complication that spreads beyond the ear. Seek urgent professional care.

## Preventing ear problems

1. Keep the ears dry after swimming, drying the outer ear gently with a towel.
2. Avoid inserting objects into the ear, including cotton buds, which can push wax deeper.
3. Treat colds and sore throats promptly, since they commonly lead to middle ear infections.
4. Chew or swallow during take-off and landing in aircraft, and during changes in altitude.
5. Keep a child's feeding position upright during feeds to reduce the chance of fluid entering the middle ear.

## Caring for someone at home

Keep a note of the time pain started, its severity, any fever readings, and whether any drainage appears. This record helps whoever assesses the ear, and it shows whether the condition is improving. Most ear pain eases within a few days, but a condition that goes on getting worse should not be managed at home alone.

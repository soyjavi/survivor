# Chronic Medicines Without a Pharmacy

People with long-term conditions need their medicines every day. A missed dose can cause a crisis within days, so plan the supply before a disruption, not during one.

## Why it matters

Long-term medicines hold back conditions that can become emergencies quickly, such as high blood pressure, heart failure, epilepsy, diabetes and asthma. When the supply stops, symptoms often return slowly at first and then suddenly. Families tend to notice the change only when the person is already unwell. A written plan, a spare stock and a clear contact point turn a potential crisis into a manageable delay.

## Plan ahead

1. Make a list of every regular medicine, its dose and how often it is taken.
2. Ask the prescriber whether a larger supply can be issued, and keep it dated.
3. Keep at least two weeks of spare medicine where possible.
4. Keep a copy of the prescription list in a waterproof bag.

![Calendar: table-grid of two weeks, threshold line, pills icon per row; labels: two weeks, spare stock, refill; request the refill when stock drops below two weeks, not at the last tablet.](assets/chronic-medicines-without-a-pharmacy-2.svg)

## Step-by-step in detail

Start by writing the name, strength, dose and time of each medicine on a single card, with the prescriber's name and the nearest clinic. Next, count what you have and calculate how many days it will last. Mark the date when the stock falls below two weeks, and request a refill then, not at the last tablet. Keep the card in the same place as the supply, so that anyone helping can find both quickly.

## Keep medicines stable

- Store them dry, cool and away from light, as the label says.
- Keep insulin and some other medicines refrigerated. If the power fails, keep them in an insulated box with ice.
- Never share prescription medicines.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Insulin and glucose-lowering medicines:** keep a supply of fast sugar and a working meter, because a change in diet or supply can cause dangerous swings.
- **Epilepsy medicines:** missed doses can trigger seizures, so treat any gap as urgent.
- **Heart and blood pressure medicines:** some should not be stopped suddenly, so ask a clinician before making any change.
- **Children:** prescriptions often depend on weight, so keep the latest weight recorded with the card.
- **Pregnancy:** some medicines must not be changed without advice, so involve a clinician early.

## Materials

- A waterproof bag or sealed plastic box for documents.
- A written list of doses, prescribers and pharmacies.
- An insulated box with ice packs for refrigerated items.
- A small notebook for recording each dose taken.

## If you run out

- Ask any clinic, health post or pharmacy that is still open.
- Do not stop a medicine abruptly without advice if it prevents a serious problem, such as seizures or heart failure.
- Do not take another person's medicine, or a similar one, unless a clinician has said it is safe.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting until the last tablet to ask for a refill.
- Storing medicines in a hot car or a humid bathroom.
- Stretching doses without advice from a clinician.
- Keeping the medicine list only on a phone that may run out of battery.
- Assuming a medicine that looks the same as the usual one is interchangeable.

## Troubleshooting

If a refrigerated item has warmed beyond the time it can safely be kept, ask a pharmacist or clinician before using it. If a tablet looks different from usual, check the name on the packet against your list before taking it. If the supply is short, do not split doses unless a clinician advises it, and ask for guidance on which medicines to prioritise.

## Checklist

- Medicine list written and copied.
- At least two weeks of supply on hand.
- Refrigerated items kept cold.
- Prescriber and nearest open clinic known.
- Doses recorded each day.

## Practice before you need it

Once a month, read the list aloud and check it against the medicines in the box. Practise moving the cold-chain box to another room so that you know it is manageable. Tell a trusted neighbour where the card is kept.

## When it fails

If a medicine runs out and no supply is available, contact the nearest clinic or health post the same day. Tell them which medicine was missed and for how long. If the person develops warning symptoms, treat it as a medical emergency rather than waiting for the supply to return.

## Warning signs

Watch for symptoms that return when a medicine runs out: high blood sugar, seizures, breathlessness, swelling or chest pain. Seek help if they appear.

**Warning:** keep a written note of the doses taken, so the next clinician knows what the person was on.

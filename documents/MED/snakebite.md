# Snakebite

Most snakebites are not fatal, but venom can cause serious harm. Stay calm and get help. Panic speeds up the heart and spreads venom faster, so slowing the person down is itself part of the treatment.

## Why it matters

The most important decisions after a bite are made in the first minutes: whether to get the person to care quickly, how to keep them still, and what to avoid. Many harmful reactions come from well-meant actions such as cutting, sucking, applying ice or chasing the snake. Calm, deliberate handling reduces both the spread of venom and the fear that makes the person move around. Time of the bite is also important, because the care team will use it to judge how the venom is progressing.

## Do

1. Move the person away from the snake, and keep them still and calm.
2. Remove rings and tight clothing near the bite before swelling starts.
3. Keep the bitten limb at about heart level, or slightly below.
4. Note the time of the bite and describe the snake if you can do so safely.
5. Get medical help as quickly as possible.

![Carrying: person-carrying with limb supported level, direction-of-travel; labels: kept still, heart level; the limb is level, not raised.](assets/snakebite-2.svg)

## Do not

- Do not cut the wound or try to suck out venom.
- Do not apply a tourniquet.
- Do not apply ice.
- Do not give alcohol or stimulants.
- Do not try to catch or kill the snake. A photo from a safe distance helps identification.

## Step-by-step in detail

Help the person sit or lie down in a safe, comfortable place, away from the snake and away from water and traffic. Keep their clothing loose around the bite. Support the bitten limb with a cushion or a folded blanket, so that it does not hang or move. Ask the person to stay still and to speak only if they need to. Write down the time, the location of the bite and any change in symptoms every fifteen minutes, so the next helper has a clear timeline.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Children:** they are more affected by smaller amounts of venom, so seek care even if the bite looks small.
- **Older adults and people with heart disease:** stress and exertion can worsen the situation quickly, so keep them especially still.
- **Pregnancy:** seek medical care regardless of how the bite appears.
- **Bites on the head, neck or trunk:** these need immediate help because breathing and swallowing can be affected quickly.

## Watch for

- Swelling, pain and bruising spreading from the bite.
- Nausea, vomiting, dizziness.
- Difficulty breathing or swallowing.
- Bleeding from the gums or the wound.

## Materials

- A clean bandage or cloth to cover the wound lightly.
- A clock or watch to record the time.
- A phone or notebook to record the description and photo.
- Transport that can take the person to care without walking.

## Common mistakes

- Walking the person to care instead of carrying or helping them to ride.
- Making the person run to fetch help.
- Trying to identify the snake by handling it.
- Waiting for symptoms before seeking help.
- Covering the bite so tightly that swelling is trapped.

## Troubleshooting

If the swelling reaches a new area, mark its edge with a pen and note the time, so you can see whether it is spreading. If the person feels faint, lay them flat with the legs raised and keep them warm. If the bitten area becomes numb or changes colour, loosen the covering and tell responders.

## Checklist

- Person moved away from the snake.
- Limb supported and still.
- Rings and tight clothing removed.
- Time recorded.
- Transport and medical help arranged.

## Practice before you need it

Learn the local snakes that are dangerous in your area, and keep a note of the nearest care facility and the route to it. Agree with your household on who will drive or arrange transport, so that the first minutes are spent on the person rather than on planning.

## When it fails

If breathing becomes difficult, the person collapses or the swelling spreads rapidly, call for emergency help and keep the person still. If no transport is available, keep the person calm and resting, and keep sending for help. Do not try to cut or suck the wound at any stage.

## Dry bites

Some bites release no venom. Treat them as a wound, but still seek medical care.

**Warning:** any snakebite with breathing difficulty, collapse or spreading swelling needs urgent medical care.

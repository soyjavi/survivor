# Austere Medicine: Priorities When Care Is Far Away

When help is hours or days away, you cannot treat everything at once. Medicine in these conditions is about order: deal with what kills fastest, then deal with what will become dangerous if ignored, then make the person as comfortable as possible. A calm, repeated check of the basics often matters more than any single treatment.

## The first principle: check the basics in order

Work through the body's essential systems in sequence. Do not move to the next step until the current one is safe, or until you have done everything you can do for it.

1. **Danger:** Make sure the scene is safe for you before approaching. Do not become the second casualty.
2. **Response:** Speak to the person, and check whether they respond to voice or touch.
3. **Airway:** Confirm the airway is open and clear of blood, vomit, or objects.
4. **Breathing:** Check whether the person is breathing normally and at a sensible rate.
5. **Bleeding and circulation:** Look for severe bleeding and control it.
6. **Disability:** Check consciousness, pupils, and movement of the limbs.
7. **Exposure:** Remove enough clothing to find all injuries, and then keep the person warm.

## What to treat first

Treat the following problems before anything else, in roughly this order:

1. An airway that is blocked or closing.
2. Severe bleeding from a limb or the body that is not stopping with pressure.
3. Breathing that is absent, very slow, or very fast with obvious distress.
4. A person who cannot be woken or who is confused and getting worse.
5. A broken neck or back suspected after a fall or crash.
6. Severe burns or a chest injury that affects breathing.

**Warning:** Severe bleeding can kill in minutes. Apply firm direct pressure first, before cleaning, splinting, or any other task.

## What to watch and monitor

Some problems are not immediately life-threatening, but they become dangerous if no one checks them. Write down the time and reading each time you check.

- Breathing rate and effort, and whether it is getting faster or slower.
- Pulse strength and rate, and whether the skin is cool, pale, or clammy.
- Level of consciousness, including whether the person answers questions correctly.
- Urine output, since very low output suggests the body is losing fluid faster than it is replacing it.
- Temperature trend, not just a single value.
- Pain level and whether pain is spreading or changing character.
- Colour of the lips, fingertips, and skin.

## Making decisions with limited resources

When supplies are short, allocate them where they will do the most good.

1. Use your scarcest items first on the problems that are most likely to kill.
2. Save dressings and pain relief for people whose injuries will benefit from them.
3. Do not spend a long time on a problem you cannot reverse with what you have, if others can be helped.
4. Record what you used and what remains, so the next carer can plan.
5. Ask for help from anyone present who has skills, and assign them clear tasks.

## When to change the plan

Reassess the casualty at intervals, and re-prioritise immediately if any of these happen:

- Breathing stops or becomes very slow.
- Bleeding returns through a dressing that was holding.
- The person loses consciousness or becomes harder to wake.
- Skin turns grey, blue, or very cold.
- A new, severe pain appears, particularly in the chest or abdomen.

## Communication and records

- Keep the casualty informed in a calm voice, even if they cannot respond.
- Keep a simple written log of the time, the injury, actions taken, and vital signs.
- Make a plan for who will move the person, when, and by what route if evacuation becomes possible.
- If you hand over care, give the log and state clearly what has been done and what must happen next.

Austere medicine is less about knowing every treatment and more about doing the simple things consistently. Check, prioritise, treat the most dangerous problem, and check again. The next check tells you whether the plan is working.

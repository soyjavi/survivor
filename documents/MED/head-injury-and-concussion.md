# Head Injury and Concussion

A blow to the head can cause a concussion, which is a temporary disturbance of brain function, or it can cause bleeding and swelling inside the skull. Most concussions are not dangerous on their own, but they cannot be assessed reliably in the field, and some injuries that look minor hide serious harm. Your job is to recognise the warning signs, rest the person, and watch them carefully over the hours and days that follow. When in doubt, treat the injury as serious.

## Recognising a concussion

Concussion often shows up in the minutes and hours after the injury, not always right away.

- Headache, which may be persistent or worsen gradually.
- Confusion, including not knowing the date, place, or what happened.
- Dizziness, unsteadiness on the feet, or poor balance.
- Nausea, or vomiting once.
- Blurred or double vision, or sensitivity to light and noise.
- Feeling foggy, slowed down, or unusually emotional.
- Memory gaps around the injury.
- Loss of consciousness, even briefly.

**Warning:** Any loss of consciousness, even brief, means the person needs assessment by a clinician. Do not dismiss it because the person seems to recover quickly.

## Immediate care after the injury

1. Keep the person still and sitting or lying down, supported so they do not fall.
2. If the person is unresponsive but breathing, place them in the recovery position, and keep the neck in line with the body.
3. If the head or neck may be injured, avoid moving the person unnecessarily until a suitable assessment is possible.
4. Control any bleeding from the scalp with direct pressure, using a clean dressing.
5. Apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth to the swelling to ease discomfort.
6. Ask simple questions to check orientation, such as the person's name, where they are, and what day it is.

**Warning:** If the person has been struck hard, has a visible deformity of the skull, or bleeds heavily, do not move them more than necessary, and call emergency services.

## Warning signs that mean an emergency

Call emergency services immediately if any of these occur:

- Repeated vomiting, especially if it comes on after the head was hit.
- Increasing headache that does not respond to rest.
- Growing confusion, agitation, or behaviour that is unlike the person.
- Drowsiness that makes the person hard to wake, or that progresses toward unconsciousness.
- Seizure.
- Weakness or numbness in an arm or leg, or difficulty walking or speaking.
- Unequal pupil size, or one pupil that does not react to light.
- Clear or pink fluid leaking from the ears or nose.
- Bruising behind the ears or around the eyes that appears after the injury.
- A child who is unusually irritable, not feeding, or cannot be consoled.

**Warning:** Clear fluid from the nose or ears, bruising behind the ears, or unequal pupils after a head injury can signal a skull fracture or bleeding inside the head. Seek emergency care at once.

## Rest and monitoring

Rest is the most important treatment for a concussion. Rest in this context means reducing mental and physical effort, not necessarily lying in a dark room for days.

1. Allow the person to sleep, but wake them at intervals in the first hours, checking that they respond normally and know who they are.
2. Keep screens, reading, and loud environments to a minimum during the early period.
3. Avoid strenuous physical activity, contact sport, and work that requires concentration.
4. Give approved pain relief for headache as directed on the label, but avoid aspirin and other blood-thinning medicines in the first days because they may increase bleeding.
5. Avoid alcohol completely while recovering.
6. Record symptoms, including headache, nausea, balance, mood, and sleep, at regular intervals.

**Warning:** Do not give alcohol to a person with a head injury, and do not give sedatives or sleeping tablets without medical advice, because they can hide important changes in consciousness.

## Returning to activity

A person recovering from concussion should return to activity gradually, and only when symptoms have settled.

1. Begin with light daily activity and watch whether symptoms return.
2. Increase effort slowly over several days, as long as there is no return of headache, dizziness, or confusion.
3. Delay returning to sport, climbing, driving, or any work involving heights or machinery until a clinician has assessed the person.
4. If symptoms return after increasing effort, go back to the previous level and rest again.

## Preventing head injuries

- Wear a helmet for cycling, climbing, skiing, and any activity where falls are likely.
- Keep walkways clear of tripping hazards, and use handrails on stairs.
- Secure heavy items at height, and do not stand on unstable surfaces when working overhead.
- Avoid risky behaviour that involves the head, such as diving into shallow water.

## Record for the clinician

If you hand over care, give a clear account of the injury. Include the time and mechanism, whether the person lost consciousness, the length of any unconsciousness, the progression of symptoms, any medicines given, and any vomiting or seizures. This account helps the clinician decide whether scanning or admission is needed.

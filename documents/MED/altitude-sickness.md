# Altitude Sickness

Altitude sickness develops when a person rises too quickly to a height where the air holds less oxygen. The body needs time to adjust by breathing faster, producing more red blood cells over days, and adjusting fluid balance. When the rate of ascent outpaces this adjustment, symptoms appear, usually above about two and a half thousand metres, though they can start lower in some people. Most cases are mild and resolve with rest. A few become life-threatening, and the difference lies in recognising the warning signs early and acting on them.

## Mild altitude sickness

Mild altitude sickness is common and usually appears within the first one to two days of arriving at a higher altitude.

- Headache, often throbbing, and worse in the morning or after exertion.
- Nausea or loss of appetite.
- Tiredness and difficulty sleeping.
- Dizziness on standing up quickly.
- A feeling of being short of breath with exertion that eases with rest.

These symptoms are uncomfortable but not dangerous in themselves. The standard response is to stop ascending and allow the body to adjust.

## Acclimatising

Acclimatisation is the process of allowing the body to adapt. It is the most reliable way to prevent serious altitude illness.

1. Climb gradually, avoiding large gains in altitude on a single day.
2. Once above about three thousand metres, aim to sleep no more than a few hundred metres higher than the previous night.
3. Include a rest day every few days, and take a day to rest after each major ascent.
4. Spend time at a moderate altitude before pushing on to higher ground when the schedule allows.
5. Drink fluids steadily throughout the day, but do not overdrink to the point of discomfort.
6. Eat regular, easily digested meals, and avoid heavy alcohol consumption.

**Warning:** Do not climb while symptoms are getting worse. Sleeping at a higher altitude than where the symptoms began will usually worsen them, so the right response is to descend or to stay where you are until they improve.

## Descent is the main treatment

Descent is the most effective treatment for altitude sickness, and it is often the only one needed. Even a descent of several hundred metres can produce a noticeable improvement.

1. Stop climbing as soon as symptoms appear, and do not continue to the planned destination.
2. If symptoms are mild and stable, rest and reassess the next morning.
3. If symptoms worsen, descend at once, even at night, and do not wait for daylight.
4. Keep the person company during the descent and monitor them constantly.
5. Once at a lower altitude, rest and recheck symptoms before any further ascent.

## Dangerous forms of altitude illness

Two serious conditions are caused by altitude, and both require immediate descent.

- **High altitude pulmonary oedema:** Fluid builds up in the lungs. Signs include breathlessness at rest, a persistent cough that may produce pink or frothy sputum, a racing heart, extreme tiredness, and blue or grey lips.
- **High altitude cerebral oedema:** Swelling in the brain. Signs include confusion, severe headache not relieved by rest, clumsy movement or inability to walk in a straight line, unusual behaviour, and drowsiness that progresses toward unconsciousness.

**Warning:** Confusion, inability to walk in a straight line, breathlessness at rest, or pink frothy sputum are emergencies. Descend immediately, and do not wait to see whether the symptoms improve. Seek professional care as soon as it is reachable.

## Treatment beyond descent

Medicines can help some people with altitude sickness, but their use depends on the person's history and the advice of a clinician. Oxygen, where available, can ease symptoms while descent is arranged. Hyperbaric chambers, where available, can provide temporary treatment for serious cases. Neither replaces descent.

**Warning:** Do not give medicines for altitude sickness to a person whose symptoms are severe without medical guidance, and never use them as a reason to delay descent.

## Preventing altitude problems

- Plan the ascent with rest days built in, rather than racing toward a goal.
- Know the symptoms in yourself and in every member of the group.
- Keep an honest log of how you feel each morning, including headache, appetite, and sleep quality.
- Do not hide symptoms to avoid disappointing the group. Honest reporting saves lives.
- Carry a means of communication, and know where the nearest medical help is located.

## Group decisions

When one person is unwell, the group should decide together, and the decision should lean toward descent. If the person cannot walk unassisted, arrange transport. A group that splits up at altitude creates additional risk, so keep the group together and move at the pace of the slowest person who is unwell.

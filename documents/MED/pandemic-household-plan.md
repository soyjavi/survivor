# A Household Plan for a Pandemic

During a widespread outbreak, health services may be overloaded, shops may run short, and people may be asked to stay home. A household that has thought about these conditions in advance can keep its members fed, hydrated, and as safe as possible. The plan should be written down, reviewed every few months, and understood by every adult in the home.

## Supplies to keep on hand

Build the stock gradually, rotating older items into daily use so nothing expires unnoticed.

1. Several weeks of food that stores well and needs little or no cooking.
2. Clean drinking water, or the means to make water safe, with a reliable reserve.
3. Basic hygiene items, including soap, hand sanitiser, tissues, and bin bags.
4. Cleaning supplies suitable for surfaces that are touched often.
5. Approved pain and fever medicine, with a written list of what is stored.
6. A thermometer, spare batteries, and a notebook for recording symptoms.
7. Face coverings and disposable gloves for caring for anyone who is ill.
8. Copies of important medical information and emergency contacts.
9. Prescription medicines, with a supply extended as far as your clinician permits.

## Isolation at home

Separating a sick person from the rest of the household reduces spread more than almost any other step.

1. Choose one room for the sick person, ideally with its own bathroom or a dedicated toilet.
2. Keep the door closed and limit visits to one designated carer.
3. Leave food and drink outside the door or place it on a tray so the carer minimises contact.
4. Give the sick person their own towels, cups, plates, and bedding.
5. Assign one adult to be the carer, and keep that role consistent.
6. Keep children and vulnerable adults away from the sick room as far as practicable.

**Warning:** A carer who must share space with others should wash hands before and after every contact, and should not visit other rooms while handling the sick person's items.

## Hygiene that is realistic to maintain

- Wash hands with soap and water for at least twenty seconds, especially after touching the sick person or their belongings.
- Use hand sanitiser when water and soap are not available, rubbing until dry.
- Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or the inner elbow, and dispose of tissues promptly.
- Clean frequently touched surfaces such as door handles, light switches, taps, and phones each day.
- Wash bedding and clothing of the sick person in hot water when practicable, and dry them fully.

## Caring for the sick at home

1. Keep the person hydrated with small, frequent drinks.
2. Offer simple food that they can tolerate.
3. Record temperature, fluid intake, urine output, and symptoms at least twice a day.
4. Encourage rest, and keep the room ventilated.
5. Use approved fever and pain reliever as needed, following the label.
6. Keep a list of medicines given and the times they were given.

## Signs that require outside help

Seek professional care, by phone or in person, if any of these appear:

- Difficulty breathing, rapid breathing, or chest pain.
- Blue or grey lips or face.
- Confusion, unusual drowsiness, or difficulty waking the person.
- Signs of severe dehydration, such as very little urine or inability to keep fluids down.
- A fever that is very high, or that persists or returns after improvement.
- Symptoms that continue to worsen over several days.

**Warning:** Do not wait for a fixed period when a warning sign appears. Breathing trouble, confusion, and blue lips are emergencies, and emergency services should be called at once.

## Keeping the household functioning

- Agree on a schedule for shared tasks, including cooking, cleaning, and watching the sick person.
- Keep children occupied and informed in simple terms about what is happening.
- Look after the carer's own health, including rest and food, because a tired carer makes mistakes.
- Check on neighbours or relatives who live alone, by phone or from a safe distance.
- Review the plan every few months, updating quantities and contact details.

## Ending isolation

Isolation should end according to clear advice from health authorities or from a clinician. Do not end it simply because the sick person feels better on one day. After isolation ends, continue good hygiene and clean the sickroom thoroughly, ventilating the space before the rest of the household uses it again.

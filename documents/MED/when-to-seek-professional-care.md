# When to Seek Professional Care

Home care has limits. Know the signs that mean a person needs professional help, and act on them early. A short delay with a serious problem can cost far more than an unnecessary visit, so use these lists as a decision aid, not a guess.

## Why it matters

Most people delay seeking care because they hope the problem will pass, or because they do not want to be a bother. Those instincts are understandable, but they cause avoidable harm. The lists in this guide are designed to remove the guesswork: if a red flag is present, the decision is already made. Home care is still useful for minor problems, and recognising when it is not enough is the skill that protects people.

## Call for emergency help at once if someone has

- Difficulty breathing, or blue lips or face.
- Chest pain or pressure, especially with sweating or pain in the arm or jaw.
- Sudden weakness, numbness or drooping on one side of the face, or confusion or slurred speech.
- Unconsciousness, or a seizure that does not stop.
- Severe bleeding that does not stop with pressure.
- A severe allergic reaction with swelling of the face or throat.
- A head injury with vomiting, drowsiness or confusion.
- Poisoning or swallowing a dangerous substance.

## Seek care soon if someone has

- A wound that is deep, dirty, or not closing.
- A broken bone, or a joint that looks wrong.
- A fever that lasts more than three days, or a high fever in a baby.
- Diarrhoea with blood, or vomiting that prevents drinking.
- Signs of infection spreading from a wound.

## Step-by-step in detail

When someone seems unwell, ask three questions in order. First, is the person breathing normally and staying awake? If not, call for emergency help immediately. Second, are any of the red flags present? If so, arrange transport now. Third, is the problem getting worse over the next few hours? If yes, seek care sooner rather than later. Write the answers down, because they help the clinician sort priorities quickly.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Babies and young children:** they cannot describe symptoms, so lower the threshold for seeking care, especially with fever or poor feeding.
- **Older adults:** serious illness can present as confusion or falls rather than the classic signs, so treat sudden change as significant.
- **Pregnancy:** bleeding, severe headache, or reduced fetal movement warrant prompt advice.
- **People with long-term conditions:** they may know their own warning signs, so take their concerns seriously.

## If help is far away

- Write down the time the problem started, the symptoms, and any treatment given.
- Keep the person comfortable and monitor them every hour.
- Send for help as soon as there is any way to do so.

## Materials

- A notebook or card for the timeline of symptoms and treatments.
- A torch, blanket and water for the person.
- A list of medicines and allergies to take with them.
- A means of transport, such as a cart, boat or vehicle.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting until the person is very sick before arranging transport.
- Giving food or drink to someone who is drowsy or unconscious.
- Forgetting to tell the clinician what treatment was already given.
- Assuming that a person who seems to improve no longer needs care.
- Treating a red flag as a minor problem because the person says they feel fine.

## Troubleshooting

If you cannot reach a clinic by phone, travel toward the nearest facility while the person is stable, and keep monitoring them on the way. If the person refuses care, explain the specific red flags clearly, record their decision and the time, and keep trying to arrange help. If symptoms improve briefly then return, treat the return as a new warning sign.

## Checklist

- Red flags checked.
- Help called or sent for.
- Timeline written down.
- Person kept comfortable and watched.
- Medicines and allergy information packed.

## Practice before you need it

Keep the emergency number and the nearest clinic on a card in the first aid kit, and make sure every adult knows where it is. Once a year, talk through the red-flag lists with the household so that recognising them becomes automatic.

## When it fails

If you cannot reach care, keep the person as comfortable as possible and keep looking for ways to send for help. If the person gets worse, treat it as an emergency and act on the signs in the first list. Keep notes, because a clear record helps the next clinician.

**Warning:** if you are unsure, treat it as serious. A delayed visit is worse than an early one.

# Waterborne Illness

Contaminated water spreads diarrhoea, vomiting, hepatitis and other infections. In a crisis, these can spread through a whole household quickly. Prevention starts with the water supply and continues with hands, toilets and food handling.

## Why it matters

Water that looks clear can still carry germs that make people sick, and the illnesses it causes often spread from person to person. One sick household member can contaminate shared towels, toilets, cooking surfaces and the water container itself. In a disaster, where sanitation is disrupted, a small cluster of cases can grow into a community outbreak within days. Early action at the household level is one of the most effective ways to break that chain.

## Common signs

- Watery or bloody diarrhoea.
- Vomiting and stomach cramps.
- Fever and headache.
- Jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes), which can mean hepatitis.

## What to do

1. Stop drinking the suspected water and switch to treated water.
2. Give oral rehydration. See Diarrhoea and Dehydration.
3. Wash hands with soap and clean water, especially after the toilet and before food.
4. Keep the sick person's clothes and bedding separate, and wash them in hot water.
5. Clean toilets and surfaces with bleach solution.

## Step-by-step in detail

If you must treat water at home, bring it to a rolling boil, let it cool in the covered pot, and pour it into a clean container with a lid. Use a clean cup that is dedicated to that container, and never dip hands or unwashed cups into the stored water. Label the container with the date it was boiled, and use it within the day. Keep the container away from the toilet area and off the ground.

## Adjusting for conditions

- **Infants and young children:** they lose fluid fastest, so seek care early and keep offering oral rehydration.
- **Older adults:** dehydration can develop quietly, so track urine output and check for dizziness on standing.
- **Pregnancy:** infections such as hepatitis are more serious, so tell a clinician promptly about any jaundice.
- **Immunocompromised people:** infections can be more severe and last longer, so seek advice early.

## Prevent spread

- Keep the water supply and toilets separate.
- Burn or bury waste carefully, away from water sources.
- Do not let the sick person prepare food for others.

## Materials

- Bleach for cleaning surfaces, mixed to the strength on the label.
- Soap, clean water and a towel for each person.
- Covered containers for stored water.
- Gloves and a bin for soiled cloths and waste.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming clear water is safe without boiling or treating it.
- Storing boiled water in an open container where it becomes contaminated again.
- Sharing towels between the sick person and others.
- Cleaning hands with water only.
- Using the same cloth to wipe the toilet and then a food surface.

## Troubleshooting

If the water tastes or smells wrong after boiling, do not drink it, and find another source. If the container has been left open for a day, empty it and refill it from a freshly boiled batch. If diarrhoea continues in several people at once, report it to a clinic rather than assuming it will pass.

## Checklist

- Suspected water source stopped.
- Treated water in use.
- Hands washed with soap at the right times.
- Sick person's linen washed separately.
- Toilets and surfaces cleaned daily.

## Practice before you need it

Agree on a household hand-washing routine, and place soap and a towel where they are used most. Practise the boil, cool and store sequence with the water container you will actually use, so that the steps become familiar before a crisis.

## When it fails

If several people in the household fall ill, treat it as a possible outbreak. Tell the nearest clinic or health post, and keep a record of who is sick and when. If the water source cannot be made safe, move to a different source and stop using the first one.

## When to seek help

Seek help for blood in the stool, signs of dehydration, yellow skin or eyes, or a fever that lasts more than a day.

**Warning:** a child or older person with diarrhoea can become dangerously dehydrated within hours. Watch them closely.
